十一宗諸子
Sons of the Eleven Zongs.
2
玄宗三十子：劉華妃生琮、第六子琬、第十二子璲，趙麗妃生瑛，元獻皇后生肅宗皇帝，錢妃生琰，皇甫德儀生瑤，劉才人生琚，武惠妃生一、第十五子敏、第十八子瑁、第二十一子琦，高婕妤生璬，郭順儀生璘，柳婕妤生玢，鐘美人生環，盧美人生瑝，閻才人生玼，王美人生珪，陳才人生珙，鄭才人生瑱，武賢儀生璿、第三十子璥； 余七子夭，母氏失傳。
Emperor Xuanzong had thirty sons. Consort Liu of Huahua bore Cong, the sixth son Wan, and the twelfth son Xun. Consort Zhao Li bore Ying; Empress Yuanxian bore Emperor Suzong; Consort Qian bore Yan. Lady Huangfu bore Yao; Lady Liu bore Ju. Consort Wu Hui bore Yi, the fifteenth son Min, the eighteenth son Mao, and the twenty-first son Qi. Lady Gao bore Wei; Lady Guo Shun bore Lin; Lady Liu of Willow bore Bin; Lady Zhong bore Huan; Lady Lu bore Huang; Lady Yan bore Ci; Lady Wang bore Gui; Lady Chen bore Gong; Lady Zheng bore Tian. Lady Wu Xian bore Xuan and the thirtieth son Jing. Seven other sons died in infancy, and the names of their mothers have not been preserved.
3
奉天皇帝琮，景雲元年，王許昌郡，與真定王同封。 先天元年，進王郯，與郢王同封。 開元四年，領安西大都護、安撫河東關內隴右諸蕃大使。 十三年，徙王慶，與忠、棣、榮、光、儀、潁、永、壽、延、盛、濟十一王同封。 十五年，與十王並領節度，不出閤。 琮以涼州都督兼河西諸軍節度大使。 天寶元年，改節河東。 十載薨，贈太子，謚靖德。 肅宗立，詔曰：「靖德太子琮，親則朕兄，睿悊聰明，朕昔踐儲極，顧誠非次，君父有命，不敢違，永言懇讓，不克如素。 宜進謚奉天皇帝，妃竇為恭應皇后。 」詔尚書右仆射裴冕持節改葬，群臣素服臨送達禮門，帝禦門哭以過喪，墓號齊陵。 無子，以太子瑛子俅嗣王。 琮始名嗣直，太子嗣謙，棣王嗣真，鄂王嗣初，靖恭太子嗣玄。 開元十三年，更名曰潭，曰鴻，曰洽，曰涓，曰滉。 後十年改今名。
Cong, later posthumously honored as Emperor Fengtian, was first enfeoffed as Prince of Xuchang in Jingyun 1, on the same occasion as the Prince of Zhending. In Xiantian 1 he was promoted to Prince of Tan and enfeoffed together with the Prince of Ying. In Kaiyuan 4 he was appointed Protector-General of Anxi and commissioner charged with pacifying the tribes of Hedong, Guannei, and Longxi. In year 13 he was transferred to Prince of Qing, enfeoffed on the same day as the eleven princes of Zhong, Di, Rong, Guang, Yi, Ying, Yong, Shou, Yan, Sheng, and Ji. In year 15 he and ten other princes were all given frontier military commissions as circuit commissioners, yet none of them left the palace to take up their posts. Cong held Liangzhou as military governor and concurrently commanded the Hexi armies. In Tianbao 1 his command was transferred to Hedong. He died in year 10 and was posthumously honored as crown prince with the title Prince Jingde. When Emperor Suzong took the throne, he issued an edict: 'Crown Prince Jingde Cong was my elder brother by blood—wise, discerning, and clear-minded. When I first ascended the throne, I knew the succession was not rightfully mine, yet our father and sovereign had commanded it and I dared not disobey. I have ever wished in earnest to yield the throne, yet could not restore things as they once were. His posthumous title should be raised to Emperor Fengtian, and his consort, Lady Dou, should be honored as Empress Gongying.' An edict ordered Pei Mian, Right Vice Director of the Secretariat, to proceed with imperial credentials and oversee the reinterment. Ministers in plain mourning dress escorted the coffin to the Gate of Rites, and the emperor came to the gate and wept beyond the prescribed rites. The tomb was named Qiling. He left no sons, so Qiu, son of Crown Prince Ying, was appointed to succeed to the title. Cong had originally been named Sizhi; the crown prince Siqian; the Prince of Di Sizhen; the Prince of E Sichu; and Crown Prince Jinggong Sixuan. In Kaiyuan 13 their names were changed to Tan, Hong, Qia, Juan, and Huang. A decade later they received the names by which they are now known.
4
太子瑛，始王真定，進王郢。 開元三年，立為皇太子。 七年，詔太子、諸王入國學行齒胄禮，太常擇日謁孔子，太子獻。 詔右散騎常侍褚無量執經，群臣、學官、諸生以差賜帛。 明年，瑛加元服，見太廟。 十六年，詔九品官息女可配太子者，有司采閱待進止，以太常少卿薛縚女為妃。 帝種麥苑中，瑛、諸王侍登，帝曰：「是將薦宗廟，故親之，亦欲若等知稼穡之難。 」因分賜侍臣，曰：「《春秋》書『無麥禾』，古所甚重。 比詔使者閱田畝，所對不以實，故朕自蒔以觀其成」雲。 初，瑛母以倡進，善歌舞，帝在潞得幸。 及即位，擢妃父元禮、兄常奴皆至大官。 鄂、光二王母亦帝為臨淄王時以色選。 及武惠妃寵幸傾後宮，生壽王，愛與諸子絕等。 而太子、二王以母失職，頗怏怏。 惠妃女咸宜公主婿楊洄揣妃旨，伺太子短，嘩為醜語，惠妃訴於帝，且泣，帝大怒，召宰相議廢之。 中書令張九齡諫曰：「太子、諸王日受聖訓，天下共慶。 陛下享國久，子孫蕃衍，奈何一日棄三子。 昔晉獻公惑嬖姬之讒，申生憂死，國乃大亂； 漢武帝信江充巫蠱，禍及太子，京師蹀血； 晉惠帝有賢子，賈後譖之，乃至喪亡； 隋文帝聽後言，廢太子勇，遂失天下。 今太子無過，二王賢。 父子之道，天性也，雖有失，尚當掩之。 惟陛下裁赦。 」帝默然，太子得不廢。 俄而九齡罷，李林甫專國，數稱壽王美以揠妃意，妃果德之。 二十五年，洄復構瑛、瑤、琚與妃之兄薛銹異謀。 惠妃使人詭召太子、二王，曰：「宮中有賊，請介以入。 」太子從之。 妃白帝曰：「太子、二王謀反，甲而來。 」帝使中人視之，如言，遽召宰相林甫議，答曰：「陛下家事，非臣所宜豫。 」帝意決，乃詔：「太子瑛、鄂王瑤、光王琚同惡均罪，並廢為庶人； 銹賜死。 」瑛、瑤、琚尋遇害，天下冤之，號「三庶人」。 歲中，惠妃數見庶人為祟，因大病。 夜召巫祈之，請改葬，且射行刑者瘞之，訖不解。 妃死，崇亡。 寶應元年，詔贈瑛皇太子，瑤等復王。 瑛子五人：儼、伸、倩、俅、備。 瑛之廢，帝使慶王畜儼等為子。 儼封新平郡王，伸平原郡王，俅嗣慶王，備太仆卿，倩失傳。
Crown Prince Ying was first created Prince of Zhending, then promoted to Prince of Ying. In Kaiyuan 3 he was formally invested as crown prince. In year 7 an edict ordered the crown prince and the princes to enter the imperial academy and perform the coming-of-age ceremony in armor. The Court of Imperial Sacrifices chose a day for the visit to Confucius, and the crown prince led the offerings. An edict appointed Chu Wuliang, Right Regular Attendant, to expound the classics, and ministers, academy officials, and students received gifts of silk according to rank. The following year Ying underwent the capping ceremony and paid homage at the Imperial Ancestral Temple. In year 16 an edict declared that daughters of officials down to the ninth rank, if deemed suitable for the crown prince, were to be reviewed by the relevant offices and presented for the throne's decision. The daughter of Vice Minister of Rites Xue Zong was chosen as consort. The emperor planted wheat in the palace park, and Ying and the princes attended him on the rise. The emperor said, 'This grain is destined for the ancestral temple, so I tend it myself—and I want you to understand how arduous the work of sowing and harvest truly is.' He then divided shares among the attending ministers and said, 'The Spring and Autumn Annals records "no wheat, no grain"—the ancients treated such failure as a matter of the gravest import.' Lately I ordered envoys to inspect the fields, yet their reports did not reflect the truth. That is why I have planted this crop myself, to see how it truly grows,' he said. In the beginning Ying's mother had been brought into the palace as a singing-girl, skilled in song and dance. While the emperor was still at Lu she won his favor. Once he took the throne, he promoted her father Yuanli and her elder brother Changnu to high office. The mothers of the Princes of E and Guang had likewise been chosen for their beauty when the emperor was still Prince of Linzi. When Consort Wu Hui's favor came to eclipse the entire inner palace and she bore the Prince of Shou, the emperor's love for that son surpassed all his other sons by far. The crown prince and the two princes, whose mothers had fallen from favor, grew deeply resentful. Consort Wu Hui's daughter, Princess Xianyi, had married Yang Hui. Divining the consort's wishes, he watched for the crown prince's faults and spread ugly rumors about him. Consort Wu Hui complained to the emperor and wept. The emperor flew into a rage and summoned the chief ministers to discuss deposing the crown prince. Chief Minister Zhang Jiuling remonstrated: 'The crown prince and the princes receive Your Majesty's instruction every day, and the realm rejoices in them.' Your Majesty has long held the throne and your descendants multiply—how can you cast aside three sons in a single day?' In former times Duke Xian of Jin was beguiled by a favorite woman's slander; Shen Sheng died in grief and the state fell into chaos; Emperor Wu of Han believed Jiang Chong's witchcraft charges, and disaster reached the crown prince until the capital ran with blood; Emperor Hui of Jin had a worthy son, yet Empress Jia slandered him and the dynasty was lost; Emperor Wen of Sui listened to his empress, deposed Crown Prince Yong, and thereafter lost the realm. Today the crown prince is without fault, and the two princes are worthy men. The bond between father and son is rooted in nature; even when faults exist, they ought still to be covered over. I beg Your Majesty to judge wisely and show mercy.' The emperor fell silent, and the crown prince was spared. Before long Jiuling was dismissed. Li Linfu monopolized the government and repeatedly praised the Prince of Shou's virtues to please the consort, and she came to regard him with gratitude. In year 25 Hui again fabricated a plot implicating Ying, Yao, and Ju together with the consort's elder brother Xue Xiu in a treasonous conspiracy. Consort Wu Hui sent someone to summon the crown prince and the two princes under false pretenses, saying, 'There are bandits in the palace—please come in wearing armor.' The crown prince complied. The consort reported to the emperor, 'The crown prince and the two princes are plotting rebellion—they have come in armor.' The emperor sent eunuchs to verify the report; finding it true, he hastily summoned Chief Minister Linfu. Linfu replied, 'This is Your Majesty's family affair—not something a subject should venture upon.' The emperor's mind was made up. An edict declared, 'Crown Prince Ying, the Prince of E Yao, and the Prince of Guang Ju share equal guilt in the same evil deed—all three are deposed to commoner status; Xiu is sentenced to death.' Ying, Yao, and Ju were soon killed. The realm regarded their deaths as a grievous injustice and called them 'the Three Commoners.' Within the year Consort Wu Hui repeatedly saw the deposed princes haunting her and fell gravely ill. At night she summoned shamans to pray, requested that the victims be reburied, and had the executioners shot and buried as well—yet she was not cured. When the consort died, the hauntings ceased. In Baoying 1 an edict posthumously restored Ying as crown prince and returned Yao and the others to princely rank. Ying had five sons: Yan, Shen, Qian, Qiu, and Bei. When Ying was deposed, the emperor had the Prince of Qing take Yan and the others and raise them as his own sons. Yan was enfeoffed as Prince of Xinping; Shen as Prince of Pingyuan; Qiu succeeded to the title of Prince of Qing; Bei became Minister of the Imperial Stud; Qian's record is lost.
5
隸王琰，開元二年始王鄫，與鄂、鄄二王同封。 後徙王棣，領太原牧、太原以北諸軍節度大使。 天寶初，為武威郡都督，經略節度河西、隴右。 會妃韋以過置別室，而二孺人爭寵不平，求巫者密置符琰履中以求媚。 仇人告琰厭魅上，帝伺其朝，使人取履視之，信。 帝怒責琰，琰頓首謝曰：「臣罪宜死，然臣與婦不相見二年，有二孺人爭長，臣恐此三人為之。 」及推，果驗。 然帝猶疑琰，怒未置，太子以下皆為請，乃囚於鷹狗坊，以憂薨，妃，縚之女，無子，還本宗。 琰凡五十五子，得王者四人，僎王汝南郡，僑宜都，俊濟南，侒順化； 僚太仆卿，俠國子祭酒，仁殿中監，僾秘書監。 寶應元年，詔復琰王爵。
Prince Yan was first enfeoffed as Prince of Zeng in Kaiyuan 2, together with the Princes of E and Juancheng. He was later transferred to Prince of Di, serving as Governor of Taiyuan and Grand Military Commissioner of all armies north of Taiyuan. At the opening of the Tianbao era he served as military governor of Wuwei and commissioner overseeing Hexi and Longyou. Once his consort Lady Wei was confined to a separate chamber for an offense. Two young concubines quarreled over favor and, seeking a sorcerer's aid, secretly placed talismans in Yan's shoes to win his affection. An enemy reported that Yan had used sorcery against the emperor. The emperor waited until Yan came to court, then sent men to fetch his shoes and inspect them—and found the report credible. The emperor angrily rebuked Yan. Yan kowtowed and said, 'Your servant deserves death. Yet I have not seen my wife for two years, and two concubines contend for precedence—I fear these three women are responsible.' When the matter was investigated, it proved true. Yet the emperor still harbored doubts about Yan, and his anger was not assuaged. The crown prince and all the princes below him pleaded on Yan's behalf, whereupon Yan was imprisoned in the Hawk and Hound Yard and died of grief. His consort, Zong's daughter, had no sons and was returned to her clan. Yan had fifty-five sons in all; four received princely rank: Fu as Prince of Runan, Qiao of Yidu, Jun of Jinan, and An of Shunhua; Liao became Minister of the Imperial Stud; Xia became Chancellor of the Directorate of Education; Ren became Director of the Palace Administration; and Ai became Director of the Secretariat. In Baoying 1 an edict restored Yan's princely title.
6
鄂王瑤，既封，遙領幽州都督、河北節度大使。 開元二十三年，與榮、光、儀、潁、永、壽、延、盛、濟、信、義十一王並授開府儀同三司，實封二千戶。 詔詣東宮、尚書省，上日百官集送，有司供張設樂。 是日，悉拜王府官屬，然未有府也，而選任冒濫，時不以為榮。
The Prince of E, Yao—once enfeoffed—held the posts of military governor of Youzhou and grand military commissioner of Hebei in absentia. In Kaiyuan 23 he and the eleven princes of Rong, Guang, Ceremonial Rites, Ying, Yong, Shou, Yan, Sheng, Ji, Xin, and Righteousness were all granted the honorary rank of Grand Master with Gold Seal and Purple Cord, with an actual fief of two thousand households. An edict ordered them to proceed to the Eastern Palace and the Secretariat. On the appointed day the hundred officials assembled to escort them, and the relevant offices provided banners and music. That same day all were appointed to princely household offices—yet there were as yet no actual princely establishments, and appointments were made with reckless indiscrimination. The age did not regard it as an honor.
7
光王琚，開元十三年始王，與儀、潁、永、壽、延、盛、濟七王同封。 俄領廣州都督。 勇力善騎射，帝愛之。 與鄂王同居，友睦甚，皆篤學。 既廢，無嗣。 初，琚名涺，儀王濰，潁王沄，永王澤，壽王清，延王洄，盛王沐，濟王溢，信王沔，義王漼，陳王沚，豐王澄，恒王潓，涼王漎，汴王滔，至二十三年，詔悉改今名。
The Prince of Guang, Ju, was first enfeoffed in Kaiyuan 13, together with the seven princes of Yi, Ying, Yong, Shou, Yan, Sheng, and Ji. Soon afterward he held the post of military governor of Guangzhou. Bold and strong, skilled in horsemanship and archery, he was beloved by the emperor. He lived together with the Prince of E in great friendship and mutual affection; both were devoted to learning. After his deposition he left no heirs. At the outset Ju was named Ti; the Prince of Ceremonial Rites, Wei; the Prince of Ying, Yun; the Prince of Yong, Ze; the Prince of Shou, Qing; the Prince of Yan, Hui; the Prince of Sheng, Mu; the Prince of Ji, Yi; the Prince of Xin, Mian; the Prince of Righteousness, Cui; the Prince of Chen, Chi; the Prince of Feng, Cheng; the Prince of Heng, Lian; the Prince of Liang, Cong; and the Prince of Bian, Tao. By year 23 an edict changed them all to their present names.
8
夏悼王一，生韶秀，以母寵，故鐘愛，命之曰一。 未免懷薨，追爵及謚。 時帝在東都，故葬龍門東岑，欲宮中望見雲。
Summer-Mourning Prince Yi was graceful and fair from birth. Because his mother was favored, he was deeply cherished, and the emperor named him One. He died before leaving the womb; posthumous rank and title were granted. At the time the emperor was in the eastern capital, so the burial was placed on the eastern slope of Longmen, where the palace might look out and see the clouds above it.
9
儀王璲，即封，授河南牧。 薨，贈太傅。 子侁王鐘陵郡，僆廣陵。
The Prince of Yi, Xun, upon enfeoffment was appointed Governor of Henan. He died and was posthumously honored as Grand Tutor. His son Shen was enfeoffed as Prince of Zhongling; Mian as Prince of Guangling.
10
懷思王敏，貌豐秀若圖畫，帝愛之。 甫晬薨，追爵及謚，祔葬敬陵。
Recalling-and-Thinking Prince Min was full-faced and handsome as if painted, and the emperor loved him. He died just after his first birthday; posthumous rank and title were granted, and he was buried beside Jingling.
11
永王璘，少失母，肅宗自養視之。 長聰敏好學。 貌陋甚，不能正視。 既封，領荊州大都督。 安祿山反，帝至扶風，詔璘即日赴鎮。 俄又領山南、江西、嶺南、黔中四道節度使，以少府監竇昭為副。 璘至江陵，募士得數萬，補署郎官、御史。
The Prince of Yong, Lin, lost his mother while young; Suzong himself raised and cared for him. Growing up he was clever and eager to learn. His appearance was extremely ugly; he could not look people straight in the face. Once enfeoffed, he held the post of grand military governor of Jingzhou. When An Lushan rebelled and the emperor reached Fufeng, an edict ordered Lin to proceed to his post that very day. Soon afterward he also received commissions over the four circuits of Shannan, Jiangxi, Lingnan, and Qianzhong, with Dou Zhao, Vice Minister of the Palace Storehouse, as his deputy. Lin reached Jiangling, recruited soldiers numbering in the tens of thousands, and appointed men to posts as court gentlemen and censors.
12
時江淮租賦巨億萬，在所山委。 璘生宮中，於事不通曉，見富且強，遂有窺江左意，以薛镠、李臺卿、韋子春、劉巨鱗、蔡F駉為謀主。 肅宗聞之，詔璘還覲上皇於蜀，璘不從。 其子襄城王亻易，剛鷙乏謀，亦樂亂，勸璘取金陵。 即引舟師東下，甲士五千趨廣陵，以渾惟明、季廣琛、高仙琦為將，然未敢顯言取江左也。
At the time the tax grain and levies of the Jiang-Huai region amounted to hundreds of millions; wherever they were stored, the piles rose like hills. Lin had been born in the palace and understood nothing of practical affairs. Seeing such wealth and strength, he conceived a design on the lands south of the Yangzi, taking Xue Liu, Li Taiqing, Wei Zichun, Liu Julin, and Cai Bing as his chief advisers. When Suzong learned of this, he ordered Lin to return and pay homage to the Retired Emperor in Shu; Lin refused to obey. His son, the Prince of Xiangcheng, Ti, was fierce and overbearing but lacked strategy; he too delighted in disorder and urged Lin to seize Jinling. Lin thereupon led his fleet eastward with five thousand armored soldiers hurrying toward Guangling, appointing Hun Weiming, Ji Guangchen, and Gao Xianqi as his generals—yet he still did not dare openly speak of seizing the lands south of the Yangzi.
13
會吳郡采訪使李希言平牒璘，璘因發怒曰：「寡人上皇子，皇帝弟，地尊禮絕。 今希言乃平牒抗威，落筆署字，何邪？ 」乃使惟明襲希言，而令廣琛趨廣陵，攻采訪使李成式。 璘至當塗，希言已屯丹楊，遣將元景曜等拒戰，不勝，降於璘，江淮震動。
Just then Li Xiyan, the surveillance commissioner of Wu commandery, addressed Prince Lin in a letter on equal stationery—as though they were peers. Lin flew into a rage. "I am a prince of the Retired Emperor, the emperor's own younger brother," he said. "My rank is exalted and my ritual standing without equal. And now Xiyan dares send me a letter on equal footing, signing his name at the bottom as though he were my equal. What is the meaning of this?" He thereupon sent Hun Weiming to attack Xiyan and ordered Ji Guangchen to hurry to Guangling and assault the surveillance commissioner Li Chengshi. Lin reached Dangtu. Xiyan had already encamped at Danyang and sent generals including Yuan Jingyao to resist, but they were defeated and surrendered to Lin. The entire Jiang-Huai region was shaken.

Under Tang institutions, princes of the blood received eight hundred enfeoffment households, increased in some cases to one thousand; princesses received three hundred, and senior princesses no more than six hundred. In Gaozong's time the three princes of Pei, Ying, and Yu and Princess Taiping, all born of Empress Wu, first exceeded the prescribed quotas. In the Zhonggu reign Princess Taiping's allotment reached twelve hundred households. In early Shengli the Prince of Xiang and Princess Taiping each received three thousand households, while the five princes including Shouchun received three hundred each. In early Shenlong the Prince of Xiang and Princess Taiping reached five thousand households; Prince Wei three thousand; Prince Wen two thousand; the Shouchun princes seven hundred each; the princes of Siyong, Hengyang, Linzi, Baling, and Zhongshan five hundred each; Princess Anle two thousand; Changning fifteen hundred; Xuancheng, Yicheng, and Xu'an a thousand each; and the Prince of Xiang's daughters, enfeoffed as chieflets, three hundred each. The Prince of Xiang was later increased to seven thousand households; Princess Anle to three thousand; Changning to twenty-five hundred; and Yicheng and the rest to two thousand. For the Prince of Xiang, Taiping, Changning, and Anle the limit was seven retainers per household. Even in years of flood or drought this quota was not remitted; it was made up from state land tax and corvée payments. In Zhongzong's death edict the princes of Yong and Shouchun were advanced to princes of the first rank, each with one thousand households. After Kaiyuan the Son of Heaven cultivated brotherly harmony among the princes, so the Prince of Ning reached fifty-five hundred households, Qi and Xue five thousand each, Prince Shen four thousand on account of his mother's humble birth, Prince Bin eighteen hundred, the emperor's sisters a thousand each, and Zhongzong's daughters likewise—all subject to a general limit of three retainers per household. When imperial sons were enfeoffed as princes they received two thousand households; princesses received five hundred. Princess Xianyi, because her mother was Consort Hui, received one thousand households. Thereafter princesses were as a rule capped at one thousand. At first, after Empress Wende died, the Prince of Jin was the youngest child. Taizong pitied him and would not allow him to leave the Inner Quarters. Prince Yu likewise, as Empress Wu's youngest son, did not leave the Inner Quarters. In early Sisheng he ascended the throne, and only when he was later demoted to Prince of Xiang did he leave the Inner Quarters. In Zhongzong's time Prince Qiao lost favor and was transferred to an outer fief. Prince Wen, at seventeen, still lived within the palace and was thereupon installed as emperor. After Kaiyuan, imperial sons in youth mostly lived within the Forbidden Inner. Once they had grown, an edict ordered pavilions built against the park wall as a great palace, divided into separate courtyards—the Ten Princes' Residence, housing the princes of Qing, Zhong, Di, E, Rong, Guang, Yi, Ying, Yong, Yan, Sheng, Ji, and others. The ten is a round number for the whole. Eunuchs supervised them, and through the walled passage they attended the Son of Heaven's daily movements. The Director of the Household saw to their daily meals. Literary scholars were brought in to instruct them. These tutors were called Readers-in-Attendance. The seven princes Shou, Xin, Yi, Chen, Feng, Heng, and Liang, though already enfeoffed, also dwelt in the Ten Princes' Residence. E and Guang were deposed and died. Zhong was established as crown prince. Qing and Di died in succession. Only Rong, Yi, and fourteen princes remained in the courtyards, while their staff offices were arrayed in outer wards. At the seasonal observances they sent their names and greetings. As imperial grandsons also grew numerous, a Hundred Grandsons' Courtyard was established outside the residence. When the Son of Heaven went each year to Huaqing Palace, Ten Princes' and Hundred Grandsons' courtyards were also set up beside the palace. Palace women in each courtyard numbered more than four hundred; in the Hundred Grandsons' Courtyard there were also thirty or forty. Within the Forbidden Inner a Weicheng Storehouse was established to supply the princes' monthly stipends. When grandsons took brides or daughters were given in marriage, the ceremonies were held at the Ten Princes' Residence. The crown prince did not dwell in the Eastern Palace but in whichever separate courtyard the imperial carriage happened to favor. Crown princes, princes of the blood, and princesses all had their wedding pavilions supplied at the Ch'ung-jen Ceremonial Hall. Such were the institutions of an age of peace.

Tan, posthumously honored as Emperor Chengtian, was first enfeoffed as Prince of Jianning. He was fierce, resolute, and gifted with talent and stratagem. He excelled at horsemanship and archery. When An Lushan rose in rebellion, Tan took command of the imperial guard and escorted the fleeing court. After they crossed the Wei River, the people blocked the road and begged the crown prince to remain. The crown prince sent word, 'The Son of Heaven has been driven into exile. How can I leave his side?' Tan pressed forward and said, 'This rebel has overturned the realm and shattered the empire. If we do not seize the people's will and plan restoration, then even if we follow the emperor into Shu, everything east of San Pass will be lost to the dynasty.' The highest filial duty is to secure the altars of state. Your Highness should recruit bold men, march swiftly into Hexi, and gather the frontier horse herds. The frontier garrisons field no fewer than a hundred thousand men, and Guangbi and Ziyi still hold their full strength in Hebei. Unite with them to plan restoration—that is the supreme course.' The Prince of Guangping agreed as well, and the decision was made. The crown prince pushed north beyond the Wei with wretched arms and rations. Morale crumbled. Day after day they fought battle upon battle. Tan rode behind with several hundred elite horsemen. In every clash he charged at the front, his sleeves soaked dark with blood—and never once complained of it. When the crown prince went hungry past the hour of his meal, Tan wept until he could not speak for grief. All three armies looked on. At Lingwu the crown prince took the throne. The court considered making Tan supreme commander of all armies, but his attendants pressed hard for the Prince of Guangping instead. The emperor said, 'Guangping is already heir apparent. Why make him a field marshal?' They answered, 'When the crown prince leads an army, he is said to pacify the host; when he holds the capital, he oversees the realm.' Field marshal means pacifier of armies—and no one is more fitting than the Prince of Guangping.' The emperor agreed. A new order placed Tan in command of the imperial guard, with Li Fuguo as his adjutant. At that time Lady Zhang Liangdi held the emperor's favor and plotted with Fuguo to undermine the succession. Tan was upright and blunt, and repeatedly warned the emperor. Lady Zhang and Fuguo therefore slandered him, claiming, 'Tan resents being denied command of the armies and broods with treason in his heart.' The emperor, misled by their private whispers, ordered Tan to die. Before long he awoke to what he had done—and grieved. The next year the Prince of Guangping retook the two capitals and sent Li Bi to bear the news of victory. Bi had long been close to the emperor. As they spoke easily of Tan's case, the emperor's face changed. He said, 'In those bitter days Tan truly did his part. Small men set brother against brother, and I weighed the realm against my own flesh—and cut what I loved most to preserve the throne.' Bi said, 'I was in Hexi then and knew the truth.' The Prince of Guangping loves his brothers dearly. Even now, at the name of Jianning, he weeps until he cannot stop. What Your Majesty has heard came from poison tongues alone.' The emperor wept and said, 'It is done already. What can be undone now?' Bi said, 'Has Your Majesty ever heard of the Melons on the Yellow Platform?' Emperor Gaozong had eight sons. The Empress Wu bore four of them. Among themselves they ordered their ranks by age, and Ruizong was the youngest. The eldest was Hong, made crown prince—humane, bright, filial, and devoted to his brothers. When the empress later schemed to rule in her own name, she poisoned him and set up the second son, Xian, as heir. Xian lived in daily fear. Whenever he attended his father he dared not speak plainly. Instead he wrote a song and had musicians perform it, hoping to move the emperor and the empress alike. The words ran, 'Melons planted below the Yellow Platform—when ripe, the fruit hangs heavy.' One picking and the melons grow fine; a second picking and the vine grows sparse. A third picking—and one may still say it will do; a fourth picking, and only the bare stem remains on the vine.' Yet in the end Xian was cast out by the empress and died in exile at Qianzhong. Your Majesty has already plucked once—take care not to pluck again!' The emperor started and said, 'How can you, sir, speak such words?' At that time the Prince of Guangping had won great merit and was also framed by the empress; Bi therefore raised the matter in his reply, and Guangping was secured. When Daizong took the throne, Tan was posthumously created Prince of Qi. In 768 an edict declared that Tan, in the darkest days, had first settled the great design, overrode dissent, and served the restoration—he was posthumously elevated as Emperor Chengtian. The youngest daughter Zhang of the Princess of Xingxin was made Empress Gongshun for a posthumous marriage. He was buried at Shun Mausoleum, with his spirit tablet in the temple of Emperor Fengtian, in the same hall but a separate chamber. At first Li Bi asked that Tan receive further honors. Daizong said, 'Tan was loyal and filial yet trapped by slander—if we posthumously make him emperor, what then?' The reply was, 'In the Kaiyuan period the Retired Emperor's brothers were all posthumously made crown princes.' The emperor said, 'That was merely the ancestral family's affection—how can it compare with Tan's merit?' Thereupon the posthumous imperial title was granted. Envoys were sent to welcome the coffin from Pengyuan. When it reached the city gate the funeral carriage would not move. The emperor said to Bi, 'Is there resentment?' Go and offer sacrifice, and make my intent known. And you, together with those who knew Tan's hardships and settled the plan.' Bi composed two elegiac stanzas recounting Tan's intent, ordered the pallbearers to sing them, and advanced the carriage—it then moved, and all who watched wept.

莊恪太子永，大和四年始王魯。 帝以王幼，宜得賢輔，因召見傅和元亮。 元亮以卒史進，有所問，不能答。 帝責謂宰相：「王可教，官屬應任士大夫賢者，寧元亮比邪！ 」於是劇選戶部侍郎庾敬休兼王傅，太常卿鄭肅兼長史，戶部郎中李踐方兼司馬。 六年，遂立為皇太子。 帝承寶歷荒怠，身勤儉率天下，謂晉王生謹敏，欲引為嗣，會蚤夭，故久不議東宮事。 及太子立，天下屬心焉。 開成三年，詔宮臣詣崇明門謁朔望，侍讀偶日入對。 太子稍事燕豫，不能壹循法，保傅戒告，不納。 又母愛弛，楊賢妃方幸，數譖之。 帝它日震怒，禦延英，引見群臣，詔曰：「太子多過失，不可屬天下，其議廢之。 」群臣頓首言：「太子春秋盛，雖有過，尚可改。 且天下本，不可輕動，惟陛下幸赦。 」御史中丞狄兼暮流涕固爭，帝未決，罷。 群臣又連章論救，意稍釋，詔太子還少陽院，以中人護視，誅幸昵數十人，敕侍讀竇宗直、周敬復詣院授經。 然太子終不能自白其讒，而行己亦不加修也。 是年暴薨，帝悔之。 明年，下詔以陳王為太子，置酒殿中。 有俳兒緣橦，父畏其顛，環走橦下。 帝感動，謂左右曰：「朕有天下，返不能全一兒乎！ 」因泣下。 即取坊工劉楚才等數人付京兆榜殺之，及禁中女倡十人斃永巷，皆短毀太子者。 宰相楊嗣復等不及知，因言：「楚才等罪當誅，京兆殺之，不覆奏，敢以請。 」翌日，詔京兆後有決死敕不覆者，亦許如故事以聞。
Crown Prince Zhuangke Yong was first created Prince of Lu in 830. Thinking the prince too young to govern without guidance, the emperor summoned his tutor He Yuanliang for an audience. Yuanliang had risen from the rank of clerk; when the emperor questioned him, he could not answer. The emperor rebuked the chancellors: 'The prince can be taught. His staff ought to be worthy gentlemen of the scholar-official class—is Yuanliang fit for such company? ' Thereupon Yu Jingxiu, Vice Minister of Revenue, was rigorously chosen as the prince's tutor; Zheng Su, Minister of Ceremonies, as chief administrator; and Li Jianfang, Director of Revenue, as chief secretary. In the sixth year he was thereupon established as crown prince. Succeeding the reckless neglect of the Baoli era, the emperor personally modeled thrift and led the empire by example. He had regarded the Prince of Jin as prudent and quick-witted from youth and wished to make him heir—but the prince died prematurely, and therefore he long deferred naming a crown prince. Once the crown prince was established, all under Heaven looked to him with hope. In 838 an edict ordered palace officials to attend at Chongming Gate on the new and full moon; tutors occasionally entered for audience by day. The crown prince gradually gave himself to banquets and pleasures and could not wholly follow the rules; his tutors admonished him, but he would not listen. Moreover his mother's affection waned; Consort Yang Xian, then in favor, slandered him again and again. One day the emperor, shaken with rage, held court at Yanying Hall, summoned the ministers, and proclaimed: "The crown prince has many faults and cannot be entrusted with the realm—discuss deposing him. " The ministers prostrated themselves and said: "The crown prince is still young; though he has faults, he can still reform. Moreover he is the foundation of the realm and must not be lightly displaced—we beg Your Majesty's mercy. " Vice Censor-in-Chief Di Jianmo wept and argued firmly; the emperor remained undecided and dismissed court. The ministers again memorialized in succession pleading for him; the emperor's anger softened somewhat. An edict ordered the crown prince back to the Shaoyang Quarter under eunuch guard; several dozen favored intimates were put to death; and tutors Dou Zongzhi and Zhou Jingfu were sent to the quarter to lecture on the classics. Yet the crown prince never cleared himself of slander, nor did he amend his conduct. That year he died suddenly; the emperor was filled with regret. The next year an edict made the Prince of Chen crown prince, and a feast was held in the palace hall. A performer boy walked the pole; his father, fearing a fall, ran in circles beneath it. The emperor was moved and said to those at his side: "I possess all under Heaven—yet I cannot even preserve one son! " Whereupon he wept. He immediately handed several quarters workmen, including Liu Chucai, to the Capital Magistrate for cudgeling to death, and ten female entertainers of the inner palace were beaten to death in Yong Alley—all who had slandered the crown prince. Chancellor Yang Sifu and others had not been informed beforehand; they therefore said: "Chucai and the others deserved death, but the Capital Magistrate executed them without memorial review—we venture to ask how this is to be handled. " The next day an edict declared that hereafter, when the Capital Magistrate carried out death sentences by imperial command without prior review, he might report them afterward according to precedent.

Prince Tong Zi was first created Prince of Kui in 846, enfeoffed together with Prince Qing Yi. At first the emperor ordered the Prince of Yun to reside in the Sixteen Mansions; the remaining five princes dwelt in the inner court of Daming Palace. Remonstrance Counselor Zheng Zhang and Director of the Bureau of Personnel Li Ye were made tutors; every five days they visited Qianfu Gate to lecture the princes on the classics. When the Prince of Yun was enthroned as Emperor Yizong, the arrangement was abolished. Zi was transferred to another princedom. In 896, under Emperor Zhaozong, he commanded the palace guard armies. At that time Wang Xingyu was executed, but Li Maozhen nursed a grievance and entered the capital with troops. An edict ordered Zi and the other princes each to command the Anshi, Fengchen, Baoning, and Anhua guards to defend the capital. The emperor was about to proceed to Taiyuan; Han Jian met him on the road and kept him halted at Hua Prefecture. Han Jian feared the princes' armed force and sent someone to submit an urgent report alleging that the princes intended to kill him and were coercing the emperor to go to Hezhong. The emperor was alarmed and summoned Jian to discuss it; Jian pleaded illness and refused to enter. An order directed Zi together with the Princes of Mu, Ji, Shao, Peng, Han, Yi, and Chen to visit Jian and explain themselves. Jian kept his army in the field and memorialized: "Court and camp are separate bodies—a minister cannot receive private audiences. " He also said: "The eight princes of Jin seized power at will and in the end ruined the realm. I ask that they return to the Sixteen Mansions and that all their commanded troops be disbanded. " The emperor did not consent. Jian surrounded the imperial traveling residence with troops and demanded that the great general Li Jun be executed. The emperor feared him and beheaded Jun to appease him. Jian drove out all the guard troops; from then on the emperor stood alone and weak.

At first the emperor had Acting Prince of Yan Jiepi and Acting Prince of Dan Yun go to see Li Keyong; when the two princes returned, Jian hated them; Moreover the Acting Prince of Tan had once supervised troops campaigning against Maozhen. Thereupon he impeached them: "In recent years warfare has engulfed the capital region; the princes have fallen into its disaster, forcing the imperial carriage to dwell in subordinate provinces without peace. I have already asked that their troops be dismissed. Now the three princes Yan, Tan, and Dan still secretly plot to endanger the state—I ask that they be put to death. The emperor said: "They have come to this? " Three days later, together with Liu Jishu, he forged an edict and attacked the Sixteen Mansions with troops. The princes, hair disheveled, scaled the walls and fled; some climbed onto rooftops and wailed: "Your Majesty, save us! " Jian then led eleven princes together with their households to Shidi Valley and killed them, afterward reporting them as having plotted rebellion; all under Heaven deemed it a great injustice. The nine princes Ji, Shao, Peng, Han, Yi, Chen, Yan, Tan, and Dan—the histories have lost their genealogies.

Prince De Yu was first enfeoffed in 891. When the emperor visited Hua Prefecture, Han Jian had already stripped the princes of their troops and felt ill at ease. He therefore asked that unenfeoffed imperial sons be made princes; afterward he killed the princes and then asked that Yu be made crown prince, declaring this to the four directions—it was 897. Liu Jishu and others confined the emperor in the Eastern Inner Palace and enthroned Yu as emperor. When Jishu was executed Yu hid in the Right Army; some asked that he be killed. The emperor said: "The crown prince is young and innocent—the rebels forcibly enthroned him; what crime has he committed? " An edict ordered him back to the Shaoyang Quarter, restoring him as a prince. When Zhu Quanzhong returned from Fengxiang he saw that the prince was in the prime of youth, tall and handsome, and envied him. He said secretly to Cui Yin: "The prince has already usurped the throne once; in great righteousness one destroys kin—how can he be kept? You serve as chancellor—why not raise it with the emperor? " Yin spoke at his ease along the lines Quanzhong wished; the emperor would not consent. Another day he relayed this to Quanzhong. Quanzhong said: "This is a great matter of state—how dare a subject take part? Yin must have betrayed me. " Thereupon Yu was spared. When the emperor moved the capital to Luoyang, one day he said to Jiang Xuanhui: "Prince De is a beloved son of mine—how can Quanzhong wish to kill him? " Having spoken he wept, biting his finger until it bled. Xuanhui immediately reported this speech to Quanzhong, and Quanzhong was furious. After the emperor was killed, Xuanhui set out wine and invited the princes to the Nine-Bend Pool; when they were drunk he killed them all and cast their bodies into the water.

157
贊曰：唐自中葉，宗室子孫多在京師，幼者或不出閤，雖以國王之，實與匹區夫不異，故無赫赫過惡，亦不能為王室軒輊，運極不還，與唐俱殫。 然則歷數短長，自有底止。 彼漢七國、晉八王，不得其效，愈速禍雲。
The commentator says: From the mid-Tang onward, imperial clansmen mostly dwelt in the capital; the younger ones often never left the palace confines. Though titled as kings of states, in truth they differed little from common men of the lanes. Thus they performed no grand evil, yet neither could they lend weight to the royal house; when fortune reached its limit there was no return—they perished together with Tang. Thus the span of dynastic fortune, long or short, has its own terminus. The seven kingdoms of Han and the eight princes of Jin sought this effect but did not attain it—their ruin came all the swifter.

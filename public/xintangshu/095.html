1
高檢子:履行審行真行五世孫:重竇威侄:軌琮抗侄孫:靜誕侄:璡從孫:德玄
The sons of Gao Shilian were Lüxing, Shenxing, and Zhenxing; a fifth-generation descendant was Zhong; Dou Wei's nephews were Gui, Cong, and Kang; his grandnephews were Jing and Dan; his nephew was Jin; and his great-grandson was Dexuan.
2
高儉，字士廉，以字顯，齊清河王岳之孫，父勵樂安王，入隋為洮州刺史。 士廉敏惠有度量，狀貌若畫，觀書一見輒誦，敏於占對。 隋司隸大夫薛道衡、起居舍人崔祖浚皆宿臣顯重，與為忘年友，繇是有名。 自以齊宗室，不欲廣交，屏居終南山下。 吏部侍郎高孝基勸之仕，仁壽中，舉文才甲科，補治禮郎。 斛斯政奔高麗，坐與善，貶為硃鳶主簿，以母老不可居瘴癘地，乃留妻鮮于奉養而行。 會世大亂，京師阻絕，交趾太守丘和署司法書佐。 時欽州俚帥寧長真以兵侵交趾，和懼，欲出迎，士廉曰：「長真兵雖多，縣軍遠客，勢不得久，城中勝兵尚可戰，奈何受制於人？ 」和因命為行軍司馬，逆擊破之。
Gao Shilian, courtesy name Shilian, was known by that name. He was the grandson of Prince Yue of Qinghe under Northern Qi; his father Li held the title Prince of Le'an and, after the dynasty changed, served the Sui as prefect of Taozhou. Shilian was quick of mind, perceptive, and magnanimous. His features were finely formed, as though painted; he could recite a text after a single reading and was sharp in impromptu debate. Xue Daohang of the Office of the Censorate and Cui Zujun, attendant of the Secretariat, were both senior ministers of standing who became his friends across the gap in years. Through them his name spread. As a member of the Qi imperial house, he did not wish to cultivate a wide circle of acquaintance and withdrew to live in seclusion at the foot of Mount Zhongnan. Gao Xiaoji, vice minister of the Ministry of Personnel, urged him to take office. In the Renshou reign he passed the civil examination in the top grade for literary talent and was appointed director of rites. When Husizheng fled to Goguryeo, Shilian was punished for association with him and demoted to chief clerk at Zhuyuan. His mother was too old to endure a malarial posting, so he left his wife Lady Xianyu to care for her and set out alone. The realm then fell into chaos and the capital was cut off from the south. Qiu He, prefect of Jiaozhi, appointed him judicial secretary. At that time the Li chieftain Ning Changzhen of Qinzhou invaded Jiaozhi with his forces. Qiu He was afraid and wanted to go out and surrender. Shilian said, "Changzhen may have many soldiers, but his army is far from home and his men are strangers in this land—they cannot stay long. The city still has troops enough to fight. Why place ourselves at another man's mercy? Qiu He then appointed him marching marshal, and Shilian met the invaders and routed them.
3
高祖遣使徇嶺南，武德五年與和來降，於是秦王領雍州牧，薦士廉為治中，親重之。 隱太子與王隙已熾，乃與長孫無忌密計計定，是日率吏卒釋囚授甲，趨芳林門助戰。 王為皇太子，授右庶子。 進侍中，封義興郡公。 坐匿王珪奏不時上，左授安州都督。
When Gaozu sent envoys to bring Lingnan under control, Shilian came north with Qiu He to surrender in the fifth year of Wude. The Prince of Qin, then governor of Yong Province, recommended Shilian as chief administrator and held him in high personal esteem. By then the breach between the Hidden Crown Prince and the Prince of Qin had grown bitter. Shilian joined Long Sun Wuji in a secret plan, and when all was settled he led clerks and soldiers that day, freed prisoners and armed them, and rushed to the Fanglin Gate to join the battle. When the prince became crown prince, Shilian was appointed right vice tutor. He was promoted to attendant-in-ordinary and enfeoffed as Duke of Yixing Commandery. He was punished for withholding Wang Gui's memorial and failing to submit it in time, and was demoted to military commissioner of Anzhou.
4
進益州大都督府長史。 蜀人畏鬼而惡疾，雖父母病皆委去，望舍投餌哺之，昆弟不相假財。 士廉為設條教，辯告督勵，風俗翕然為變。 又引諸生講授經藝，學校復興。 秦時李冰導汶江水灌田，瀕水者頃千金，民相侵冒。 士廉附故渠廝引旁出，以廣溉道，人以富饒。
He was promoted to chief administrator of the Yizhou metropolitan command. The people of Shu feared ghosts and shunned the sick. Even when their own parents fell ill they would abandon them, leave home, and lodge elsewhere to take medicine and food; brothers would not lend one another so much as property. Shilian issued regulations, explained them publicly, and supervised and encouraged compliance, and local custom changed almost at once. He also invited scholars to lecture on the classics, and the schools were revived. In Qin times Li Bing had diverted the Min River to irrigate the fields. Land along the water fetched a thousand gold pieces per plot, and people encroached on one another's holdings. Shilian added side channels branching from the old canal to widen the irrigation network, and the people grew prosperous.
5
入為吏部尚書，進封許國公。 雅負裁鑒，又詳氏譜，所署州，人地無不當者。 高祖崩，攝司空，營山陵； 加特進，遷尚書右僕射。 士廉三世居此官，世榮其貴。
He entered the capital as minister of the Ministry of Personnel and was further enfeoffed as Duke of Xu. He had a natural gift for judgment and selection and was also deeply versed in clan genealogies; in every prefecture where he assigned men to office, none was misplaced. When Gaozu died, Shilian served as acting minister of works and oversaw construction of the imperial tomb. He was granted special advance rank and transferred to vice director of the Department of State Affairs on the right. Shilian's family had held this office for three generations, and the age counted it a mark of honor.
6
太宗幸洛陽，太子監國，命攝少師。 手詔曰：「端拱三川，不憂關中者，以屬卿也。 」久之，請致仕，聽解僕射，加開府儀同三司、同中書門下三品，知政事。 帝伐高麗，皇太子監國駐定州，又攝太傅，同掌機務。 太子令曰：「寡人資公訓道，而比聽政，據桉對公，情所未安，所司宜別設桉奉太傅。 」士廉固辭。
When Emperor Taizong went to Luoyang, the crown prince remained in the capital as regent, and Shilian was ordered to serve as acting junior preceptor. The emperor wrote in his own hand, "I can hold court in peace in the Luoyang region and need not worry over Guanzhong, for I have entrusted it to you. After some time he asked to retire. He was allowed to resign the vice directorship, was given the rank of grand master with privilege to open an office equal to the Three Excellencies and the third rank of co-signer at the Secretariat and Chancellery, and continued to take part in governance. When the emperor campaigned against Goguryeo, the crown prince supervised the realm from Dingzhou, and Shilian again served as acting grand preceptor and shared in the handling of state affairs. The crown prince issued an order: "I rely on the Grand Preceptor's guidance in governance, yet when I hear affairs of state and sit facing him across the desk, I am ill at ease. The relevant offices should set up a separate desk for the Grand Preceptor. Shilian firmly declined.
7
士廉進止詳華，凡有獻納，搢紳皆屬以目。 奏議未嘗不焚稿，家人無見者。 士廉少識太宗非常人，以所出女歸之，是為文德皇后。 及遺令墓不得它藏，惟置衣一襲與平生所好書示先王典訓可用終始者。
Shilian's bearing in court was measured and dignified; whenever he offered remonstrance or counsel, the gentry all looked to him. After each memorial or proposal he always burned the draft, and no one in his household ever saw it. In his youth Shilian had seen that the Prince of Qin was no ordinary man and gave him his daughter in marriage; she became Empress Wende. In his final instructions he ordered that his tomb hold no other treasures—only one set of clothes and the books he had loved in life that embodied the enduring worth of the ancient kings' teachings.
8
初，太宗嘗以山東士人尚閥閱，後雖衰，子孫猶負世望，嫁娶必多取貲，故人謂之賣昏。 由是詔士廉與韋挺、岑文本、令狐德棻責天下譜諜，參考史傳，檢正真偽，進忠賢，退悖惡，先宗室，後外戚，退新門，進舊望，右膏粱，左寒畯，合二百九十三姓，千六百五十一家，為九等，號曰《氏族志》，而崔幹仍居第一。 帝曰：「我于崔、盧、李、鄭無嫌，顧其世衰，不復冠冕，猶恃舊地以取貲，不肖子偃然自高，販鬻松檟，不解人間何為貴之？ 齊據河北，梁、陳在江南，雖有人物，偏方下國，無可貴者，故以崔、盧、王、謝為重。 今謀士勞臣以忠孝學藝從我定天下者，何容納貨舊門，向聲背實，買昏為榮耶？ 太上有立德，其次有立功，其次有立言，其次有爵為公、卿、大夫，世世不絕，此謂之門戶。 今皆反是，豈不惑邪？ 朕以今日冠冕為等級高下。 」遂以崔幹為第三姓，班其書天下。
Earlier, Emperor Taizong had observed that the gentry of Shandong prized pedigree and lineage; though their houses had declined, their descendants still traded on old prestige, and in marriage they always demanded heavy bride-prices—so people spoke of it as selling marriage. Accordingly he ordered Shilian, together with Wei Ting, Cen Wenben, and Linghu Defen, to compile genealogies throughout the realm, consult historical records, verify truth and falsehood, advance the loyal and worthy and remove the perverse and base—imperial clan first, consort kin next, new families demoted and old standing elevated, the well-born favored and the humble set aside. They listed two hundred ninety-three surnames and one thousand six hundred fifty-one households in nine grades under the title Records of Clans and Families, yet the Cui clan of Gan still ranked first. The emperor said, "I bear no grudge against the Cui, Lu, Li, and Zheng clans, but their houses have declined and no longer fill high office. They still trade on old standing to extract bride-prices, and unworthy sons hold themselves proudly aloft, selling off ancestral graves—do they not understand why the world once held them in esteem? Qi held Hebei, and Liang and Chen held the lands south of the Yangtze; though they had men of talent, they were regional states on the margins with nothing truly worth esteeming, so the Cui, Lu, Wang, and Xie were held in honor. Now the strategists and laboring ministers who followed me in settling the realm through loyalty, filial piety, learning, and skill—how can they be ranked below old houses that accept payment, chase reputation over reality, and take pride in buying marriage alliances? The highest is to establish virtue, next to establish merit, next to establish words, next to hold noble rank as duke, minister, or grandee generation after generation without break—this is what is called a house of standing. Now all is reversed—is this not confusion? I shall take those who wear the cap and regalia today as the measure of high and low rank. He then ranked the Cui clan of Gan as the third surname and had the book promulgated throughout the realm.
9
高宗時，許敬宗以不敘武后世，又李義府恥其家無名，更以孔志約、楊仁卿、史玄道、呂才等十二人刊定之，裁廣類例，合二百三十五姓，二千二百八十七家，帝自敘所以然。 以四後姓、酅公、介公及三公、太子三師、開府儀同三司、尚書僕射為第一姓，文武二品及知政事三品為第二姓，各以品位高下敘之，凡九等，取身及昆弟子孫，余屬不入，改為《姓氏錄》。 當時軍功入五品者，皆升譜限，搢紳恥焉，目為「勳格」。 義府奏悉索《氏族志》燒之。 又詔後魏隴西李寶，太原王瓊，滎陽鄭溫，范陽盧子遷、盧澤、盧輔，清河崔宗伯、崔元孫，前燕博陵崔懿，晉趙郡李楷，凡七姓十家，不得自為昏； 三品以上納幣不得過三百匹，四品五品二百，六品七品百，悉為歸裝，夫氏禁受陪門財。 先是，後魏太和中，定四海望族，以寶等為冠。 其後矜尚門地，故《氏族志》一切降之。 王妃、主婿皆取當世勳貴名臣家，未嘗尚山東舊族。 後房玄齡、魏徵、李勣復與昏，故望不減，然每姓第其房望，雖一姓中，高下縣隔。 李義府為子求昏不得，始奏禁焉。 其後天下衰宗落譜，昭穆所不齒者，皆稱「禁昏家」，益自貴，凡男女皆潛相聘娶，天子不能禁，世以為敝雲。 士廉六子，履行、審行、真行有名。
Under Emperor Gaozong, Xu Jingzong—because Empress Wu's lineage was not entered—and Li Yifu, ashamed that his own house had no name, again had Kong Zhiyue, Yang Renqing, Shi Xuandao, Lü Cai, and twelve others revise the work. They trimmed and broadened the categories to two hundred thirty-five surnames and two thousand two hundred eighty-seven households, and the emperor himself wrote the preface explaining why. The four empresses' surnames, the Duke of Xi, the Duke of Jie, the Three Excellencies, the crown prince's three preceptors, grand masters with privilege to open an office equal to the Three Excellencies, and vice directors of the Department of State Affairs formed the first surname-grade; civil and military officials of the second rank and third-rank participants in governance formed the second; each grade was ordered by rank in nine levels, listing only oneself and brothers, sons, and grandsons while excluding other relatives. The work was retitled Records of Surnames. At the time all who entered the fifth rank through military merit were raised into the genealogical limits; the gentry were ashamed of this and called it the "merit register." Li Yifu memorialized that every copy of Records of Clans and Families be seized and burned. An edict also declared that the seven surnames and ten households—Li Bao of Longxi in Later Wei, Wang Qiong of Taiyuan, Zheng Wen of Xingyang, Lu Ziqian, Lu Ze, and Lu Fu of Fanyang, Cui Zongbo and Cui Yuansun of Qinghe, Cui Yi of Boling in Former Yan, and Li Kai of Zhaojun in Jin—might not marry among themselves. Officials of the third rank and above might offer no more than three hundred bolts of betrothal gifts; those of the fourth and fifth ranks two hundred; those of the sixth and seventh ranks one hundred—all to serve as the bride's outfit—and the wife's clan was forbidden to accept door-accompanying wealth. Earlier, in the Taihe era of Later Wei, the eminent clans of the four seas were fixed, with Bao and the others at the head. Afterward, because pedigree was prized, Records of Clans and Families demoted them all. Imperial princesses and the consorts of imperial sons-in-law were all taken from the houses of meritorious ministers and famous officials of the age; they never married into the old clans of Shandong. Later Fang Xuanling, Wei Zheng, and Li Ji again intermarried with them, so their prestige did not decline; yet within each surname the branches were ranked by standing, and even within one surname high and low were widely separated. When Li Yifu sought a marriage alliance for his son and could not obtain one, he first memorialized to forbid such marriages. Afterward declining clans fell from the registers; those whom the ancestral line would not acknowledge all called themselves "forbidden-marriage families" and held themselves all the more precious. Men and women alike secretly arranged betrothals, and even the Son of Heaven could not forbid it—later ages regarded this as a corruption. Shilian had six sons; Lüxing, Shenxing, and Zhenxing were the ones of note.

子履行
Son: Lüxing.
11
履行居母喪毀甚，太宗諭使強食。 尚東陽公主，襲爵。 繇戶部尚書為益州大都督府長史，政有名。 坐長孫無忌，左授洪州都督，改永州刺史。
While observing mourning for his mother, Lüxing grieved so severely that Emperor Taizong admonished him and ordered him to force himself to eat. He married Princess Dongyang and inherited the title. From minister of the Ministry of Revenue he became chief administrator of the Yizhou metropolitan command, and his governance won renown. He was punished in connection with Long Sun Wuji, demoted to military commissioner of Hongzhou, and then transferred to military commissioner of Yongzhou.

真行至左衛將軍。 其子岐連章懷太子事，詔令自誡切，真行以佩刀刺殺之，斷首棄道上，高宗鄙其為，貶睦州刺史。
Zhenxing rose to general of the Left Guard. His son Qi, implicated in the affair of Crown Prince Zhanghuai, was ordered to reprove himself severely. Zhenxing stabbed him with the sword at his belt, cut off his head, and cast it in the road. Emperor Gaozong despised the deed and demoted Zhenxing to prefect of Muzhou.

五世孫重
Fifth-generation descendant: Zhong.
17
士廉五世孫重，字文明，以明經中第，李巽表鹽鐵轉運巡官，善職，凡十年，進累司門郎中。
Zhong, Shilian's fifth-generation descendant, courtesy name Wenming, passed the classics examination. Li Xun recommended him as patrol officer of the Salt and Iron Transport Office; he performed his duties well for ten years in all and was promoted in succession to director in the Ministry of Justice.
18
敬宗慎置侍講學士，重以簡厚惇正，與崔郾偕選，再擢國子祭酒。 文宗好《左氏春秋》，命分列國各為書，成四十篇。 與鄭覃刊定《九經》于石。 出為鄂嶽觀察使，以美政被褒。 久之，拜太子賓客，分司東都。 卒，贈太子少保。
When Jingzong carefully selected lecturers for the crown prince, Zhong—for his simplicity, steadiness, and upright honesty—was chosen together with Cui Yan and was twice promoted to director of the Directorate of Education. Emperor Wenzong favored the Zuo Commentary to the Spring and Autumn Annals and ordered that the various states be divided, each made into a separate book, completing forty chapters. Together with Zheng Tan he collated and fixed the Nine Classics on stone. He went out as observation commissioner of E and Yue and was praised for excellent governance. After some time he was appointed crown prince's guest and served at the Eastern Capital in a nominal post. When he died he was posthumously granted junior guardian of the crown prince.
19
贊曰：古者受姓受氏以旌有功，是時人皆土著，故名宗望姓，舉郡國自表，而譜系興焉，所以推敘昭穆，使百代不得相亂也。 遭晉播遷，胡醜亂華，百宗蕩析，士去墳墓，子孫猶挾系錄，以示所承，而閥閥顯者，至賣昏求財，汨喪廉恥。 唐初流弊仍甚，天子屢抑不為衰。 至中葉，風教又薄，譜錄都廢，公靡常產之拘，士亡舊德之傳，言李悉出隴西，言劉悉出彭城，悠悠世詐，訖無考按，冠冕皁隸，混為一區，可太息哉！
The commentary says: In antiquity one received surname and clan-name to honor merit. At that time all people were rooted to the soil, so clans were named by ancestral temple and surname, and each commandery and state set forth its own record—thus genealogies arose, to arrange the ancestral line in order and keep the hundred generations from confusion. When Jin was driven into exile and barbarian invasions threw China into chaos, the hundred clans were scattered and broken. Though scholars left their ancestral graves, their descendants still carried lineage records to show what they inherited—and among illustrious clans of standing, some went so far as to sell marriage for wealth, drowning integrity and shame. In early Tang the abuses still ran deep, yet the Son of Heaven repeatedly tried to suppress them without lasting success. By the mid-dynasty, custom and teaching had again grown thin and genealogical records were altogether abandoned. Public office no longer bound men to fixed estates, and scholars lost the transmission of old virtue; whoever was surnamed Li was said to come from Longxi, whoever was surnamed Liu from Pengcheng—the generations drifted in falsehood until nothing could be verified, and cap and regalia and common clerk were mixed in one mass. Alas!
20
竇威，字文蔚，岐州平陸人。 父熾，在周為上柱國，入隋為太傅，太穆皇后，其從兄弟女也。
Dou Wei, courtesy name Wenwei, was a native of Pinglu in Qizhou. His father Chi, in Zhou served as pillar of state and after entering Sui became grand preceptor; Empress Taimu was the daughter of his father's younger male cousin.
21
威沈邃有器局，貫覽群言，家世貴，子弟皆喜武力，獨威尚文，諸兄詆為書癡。 內史令李德林舉秀異，授秘書郎，當遷不肯調者十年，故其學益博。 而諸兄以軍功位通顯矣，薄威職閑冗，更謂曰：「昔仲尼積學成聖，猶棲遲不偶，汝尚何求耶？ 」威笑不答。 蜀王秀辟為記室，威以秀多不法，謝疾去。 秀廢，府屬皆得罪，威獨免。 大業中，累遷內史舍人，數諫忤旨，轉考功郎中，後坐事免。
Wei was deep and far-sighted, with breadth of capacity, and read widely among all books. His family had long been eminent, and the younger men all delighted in martial prowess; Wei alone honored letters, and his elder brothers mocked him as a book-addict. Li Delin, director of the Secretariat, recommended him as outstanding talent, and he was appointed secretary. When he was due for transfer he refused to accept appointment for ten years, and thereby his learning grew ever broader. His elder brothers by then had risen to prominence through military merit, and finding Wei's post idle and trivial, they again said to him, "Even Confucius, through long study, became a sage, yet he still lingered in misfortune—what more do you seek? Wei smiled and did not reply. Prince Xiu of Shu recruited him as recorder; Wei, because the prince often acted unlawfully, pleaded illness and withdrew. When the prince was deposed, all members of his staff were punished; Wei alone was spared. In the Daye era he was promoted in succession to attendant of the Secretariat; he remonstrated several times and offended the emperor's will, was transferred to director in the Ministry of Personnel, and later was dismissed for an offense.
22
後寢疾，帝臨問，及卒，哭之慟。 贈同州刺史，追封延安郡公，諡曰靖。 威性儉素，家不樹產，比喪，無餘貲，遺令薄葬。 詔皇太子、百官臨送。
Later he fell gravely ill; the emperor visited him in person, and when he died the emperor wept bitterly for him. He was posthumously granted prefect of Tongzhou, further enfeoffed as Duke of Yan'an Commandery, and given the posthumous name Jing. Wei was by nature frugal and plain; his household accumulated no property, and when he died there was no surplus wealth. His final instructions ordered a simple burial. An edict ordered the crown prince and all officials to attend his funeral.

In the Daye era he served as eastern bureau aide of Ziyang Commandery, then left office and returned home. When Gaozu raised his army, Gui recruited more than a thousand men and went to meet him at Changchun Palace. The emperor was greatly pleased, bestowed ten fine horses, and sent him to pacify the lands south of the Wei River. When fifty thousand Ji-Hu rebels pillaged Yichun, the emperor ordered Dou Gui to lead a punitive campaign against them. At Mount Huangqin, the rebels rained arrows from the high ground, and Gui's men fell back under the barrage. Dou Gui executed fourteen of his officers and promoted their deputies to take their places; then, at the head of several hundred horsemen in the rear, he proclaimed: "Any man who hears the drum and does not advance shall die." At the beat of the drum, his soldiers surged forward; the rebels' arrows could not stop them. Gui routed the enemy utterly, taking a thousand heads and twenty thousand captives, men and women alike. He was promoted to Grand Mentor of the Crown Prince. When the Chipa Qiang joined with Zhong Juqiu, a defector from Xue Ju's ranks, to raid Hanzhong, Gui was appointed Prefect-General of Qinzhou. He fought the rebels in successive engagements with repeated success, until the last of their bands surrendered. His old enfeoffment as Duke of the State of Zan was restored, and he was appointed Left Vice Director of the Yizhou Circuit Executive. When the Tangut incited the Tuyuhun to attack Songzhou, the court ordered Gui and Jiang Shanhe, prefect of Fuzhou, to go to its aid. Shanhe arrived first and routed the enemy at Qianchuan. Gui pressed on to Lin Tao, attacked Zuofeng, and drove his followers into flight. Convinced that the Qiang would remain a lasting threat, he established military farming colonies at Songzhou. He was ordered to take his command and join the Prince of Qin in the campaign against Wang Shichong. The next year he returned to Shu.

As Gui's rank rose, so did his severity. Yet he drove himself as hard as he drove others: on campaign he never once removed his armor. Any subordinate who failed to obey was executed; even trifling offenses earned the lash until the blood ran. Men who saw it walked as though on thin ice, hearts quaking—and in time every bandit in Shu was brought to heel. He had once made a nephew his closest confidant. One night he went out and called for the man; when the nephew failed to come at once, Gui had him beheaded. He had forbidden his household slaves to leave the premises, yet one day he sent a slave to fetch gruel from the official kitchen. Regretting the breach of his own rule, he said, "I must borrow your head to uphold the law." He ordered the slave executed. The slave protested his innocence; the executioner hesitated—and Gui had the executioner beheaded too. Later, when he came to court and was granted a seat on the imperial gallery, his demeanor was disrespectful; he remained seated even while an edict was being read aloud. The emperor, enraged, said: "When you went into Shu, twenty of my Longzhong cavalry officers went with you—and you killed nearly every one. Before long he was released and sent back to his post as military governor of Yizhou.

That year the circuit executive was abolished. Gui was appointed Military Governor of Yizhou, with six hundred additional households added to his fief.

Gui's younger brother Cong was a man of martial ability. At the end of the Daye era, after breaking the law he fled to Taiyuan and placed himself in Gaozu's service. He had an old grievance against the Prince of Qin and lived in unease. The Prince was then gathering the finest men in the realm, receiving them with courtesy and warm familiarity, and bringing Cong in and out of his inner chambers. Cong's anxieties gradually eased. When the Grand General's headquarters was formed, Cong was appointed a commander-in-chief. He took part in the pacification of Xihe and helped break the enemy at Huoyi. Under Liu Wenjing he attacked Qu Tutong at Tong Pass, routed Qu's general Sang Xianhe, and when Qu fled, Cong pursued with light cavalry and captured him at Chousang. He pressed forward, captured Shaan County, and took the Taiyuan granary. He was promoted to Grand General of the Left Leading Army and rewarded with five hundred bolts of silk and goods. Dugu Wu, the Sui garrison commander at Heyang, secretly sought to surrender. Cong was ordered to lead ten thousand horsemen to meet him from Baiya, but he delayed and failed to advance. Wu was killed, and Cong was stripped of his rank for the offense. At the beginning of the Wude reign he served as Grand General of the Right Garrison Guard. When the court prepared to move against Luoyang, Cong was ordered to hold Shaan and protect the supply lines. Dou Wei's nephew Kang, whose courtesy name was Daosheng, His father Rongding had served as Prefect-General of Mingzhou under the Sui and as Duke of the State of Chen; his posthumous name was Virtuous. His mother was the Princess of Ancheng, a sister of Emperor Wen of Sui. Kang was handsome in face and bearing, open and direct in temperament, and well versed in geography and history. As the emperor's nephew by marriage he rose to prominence early, entered the Imperial Academy, and upon taking his first office became a Thousand-Ox Guard Attendant with the honorary rank of Director-in-chief of the Third Rank. While nursing his father through illness, he kept his sash tightly bound for fifty days without once loosening it; In mourning he grieved so deeply that he wasted away beyond what custom required. He inherited his father's title and was eventually appointed Governor of Liangzhou. When he was about to depart for his post, Emperor Wen visited his home and they feasted together with the easy intimacy of kin. At his mother's death he wept until he repeatedly lost consciousness. The emperor issued an edict directing the palace women to moderate their lamentation. More than a year later he was made Governor of Qizhou, then Prefect-General of Youzhou; in every post he was known for his clemency and benevolence. When Prince Han Liang rose in rebellion, Emperor Yang suspected Kang might join him and dispatched Li Zixiong posthaste to relieve him of command. Li Zixiong then falsely charged that Kang had received a letter from Liang and concealed it. Though the inquiry found no proof, Kang was dismissed on that pretext.

The house of Li is named in the books of destiny—Heaven itself has opened the way." Gaozu replied: "To speak of calamity before it comes is ill-omened. Do not talk so recklessly." Emperor Yang sent Kang to Lingwu to guard the distant reaches of the Great Wall. When he learned that Gaozu had taken the capital, he rejoiced and said: "That son-in-law of our house—generous and broad-minded—is a true ruler to set the realm right." He thereupon returned to Chang'an. Gaozu welcomed him with delight, clasped his hand, and said: "The house of Li has indeed taken the throne—was I not right?" Jing, whose courtesy name was Yuanxiu, served in the Sui Imperial Guard. Because his father had fallen afoul of Emperor Yang, he languished for years without advancement. When Gaozu entered the capital, Jing was promoted to chief administrator of the Taiyuan Grand Prefect-General's headquarters. At that time the Turks repeatedly harassed the frontier and supply lines were unreliable. Jing submitted a memorial proposing military farming colonies at Taiyuan to reduce the cost of transport. Critics argued that refugees had not yet returned home and ought not be taxed further. Jing was summoned to debate the matter at court with Pei Ji, Xiao Yu, and Feng Lun. None of them could overturn his case; the emperor approved the plan, and the colonies yielded one hundred thousand hu of grain each year. He was appointed acting Grand Prefect-General of Bingzhou. He also proposed closing Shiling Pass and fortifying it as a barrier to check Turkic raids. When Taizong came to the throne, Jing was appointed Minister of Agriculture and enfeoffed as Baron of Xindu County. Zhao Yuankai served as vice minister, and Jing despised his habit of squeezing revenue. At a meeting of his staff he declared aloud: "If the Son of Heaven were as extravagant as Emperor Yang of Sui, draining the four seas to feed his pleasures, then the Ministry of Agriculture would indeed need you." But today's emperor practices austerity himself, bending one man's pride so that the myriad people may live in peace—what need have we of you?" Yuankai was utterly humiliated. Jing was reassigned as Military Governor of Xizhou. When the Turks grew divided in loyalty, generals setting out on campaign passed through Jing's domain. He briefed them on the true state of affairs among the barbarians, and on that intelligence they won great victories and rich spoils. He also set factions against one another among their tribes, until nine tribal chiefs under Yüshe's command—including Yuguni—all submitted to the court. The emperor commended his work and rewarded him with a hundred horses and a thousand sheep. After Jieli Khan was taken captive, the court ordered his people resettled south of the Yellow River. Jing submitted a memorial: "When the barbarians are desperate they turn to plunder; when they are full they herd together. Neither penal codes nor the teaching of benevolence and righteousness can hold them in check." They live on what others provide for food and clothing and do not depend on plowing fields or raising silkworms. To burden our productive people in order to sustain these unknowing barbarians is to gain nothing for good government and lose nothing for the civilizing order if they are cast off. Moreover, so long as they remember the mounds of their homeland, rebellion may break out at any moment and violate the imperial domain. Better to take advantage of their present ruin: invest one of them with the title of a worthy king, marry him to a princess of the imperial clan, and break up their lands and tribes so that authority is weakened and power scattered, making them easier to control—then for generations they will serve as vassals of the court. Though the emperor did not adopt his plan, he valued Jing's loyalty and answered with a gracious edict: "All affairs of the north I entrust entirely to you. With you as Grand Ambassador for Pacifying the North, I shall have no need to look over my shoulder toward the frontier." He was promoted again, to Minister of the Household. He died and was given the posthumous name Solemn. His son Kui married the Princess of Suian and inherited the title.

Kang's younger brother Jin, courtesy name Zhitui, was grave and steady by nature. At the end of the Sui Daye era he served as prefect of Fufeng. When the Tang armies rose, he surrendered his commandery and later served as Minister of the Household. He followed the Prince of Qin in the pacification of Xue Rengao and was rewarded with a brocade robe. Great-grandnephew: Dou Dexuan

威從孫德玄，隋大業中，起家國學生。 祖照，尚周文帝義陽公主，封钜鹿郡公。 父彥，襲爵，終隋西平太守。 兄德明，師事陳留王孝逸，通知文史。 漢王諒反，遣將綦良攻黎州。 德明年十八，募士五千，號令嚴整，倍道擊賊，破之。 以功擢累齊王府屬。 坐事免。 高祖兵叩長安，而宗室孝基、神符、道宗及竇誕、趙慈景等並系獄，隋將衛文升、陰世師欲殺之，德明諫曰：「罪不在此，殺之無傷於彼，祗取怨焉，不如縱之。 」乃止。 長安平，謁高祖，終不自言，時稱長者。 拜考功郎中。 從秦王擊王世充。 封顯武男，曆常、愛二州刺史，卒。 德玄始為高祖丞相府千牛，曆太宗時不甚顯，高宗以舊臣，自殿中少監為御史大夫，歲中遷司元太常伯。 時帝又以源直心為奉常正卿，劉祥道為司刑太常伯，上官儀為西台侍極，郝處俊為太子左中護，凡十餘人，皆帝自擇，以示宰相李勣等，皆頓首謝。 麟德初，進檢校左相，勤職約己，天子嘗臨朝，咨其清素，加以賜賚。 居位數年，贊圖封禪事，與李勣皆為使。 帝次濮陽，問古謂帝丘，德玄不能對，許敬宗具道其然，帝稱善。 敬宗自矜於人，德玄知，不為忤，眾服其量。 禮成，進爵二級。 以弟德遠未及爵，願分封，詔可，故德玄封钜鹿男，德遠樂安男。 德玄迎時取合，未嘗有過，然無它補益。 卒，年六十九，贈光祿大夫，幽州都督，諡曰恭。
Dou Wei's great-grandnephew Dexuan entered the Imperial Academy at the beginning of his career during the Sui Daye era. His grandfather Zhao married Emperor Wen of Zhou's daughter, the Princess of Yiyang, and was enfeoffed as Duke of Julu. His father Yan inherited the title and ended his career as governor of Xiping under the Sui. His elder brother Deming studied under Wang Xiaoyi of Chenliu and mastered literature and history. When Prince Han Liang rebelled, he sent the general Qi Liang to attack Lizhou. Deming, at eighteen, raised five thousand men, drilled them to strict order, forced a rapid march, and routed the rebels. For this service he rose through successive appointments on the staff of the Prince of Qi's household. He was later dismissed for an offense. When Gaozu's army reached Chang'an, Xiaoji, Shenfu, Daozong, Dou Dan, Zhao Cijing, and other members of the imperial clan were imprisoned. The Sui generals Wei Wensheng and Yin Shishi planned to execute them. Deming urged: "These men are not guilty of the rebellion. Killing them will not weaken the enemy—only breed hatred. Better to set them free." The execution was halted. After Chang'an fell he went to see Gaozu but never mentioned his intervention; contemporaries praised him as a man of true integrity. He was appointed Director of the Bureau of Evaluations. He followed the Prince of Qin in the campaign against Wang Shichong. He was created Baron of Xianwu, served successively as governor of Changzhou and Aizhou, and died. Dexuan began as a Thousand-Ox Guard on Gaozu's chancellor's staff and remained relatively obscure through Taizong's reign. Gaozong, honoring him as an old servant, promoted him from vice director of the palace department to censor-in-chief and, within a year, to grand master of the bureaus of state and rites. The emperor at the same time personally selected Yuan Zhiyuan as director of the Bureau of Rites, Liu Xiangdao as grand master of the bureaus of punishments and rites, Shangguan Yi as attendant of the western terrace, Hao Chujun as left protector of the heir apparent, and more than ten others besides. He presented the list to Chancellor Li Jing and his colleagues, who all bowed in gratitude. Early in the Linde reign he was promoted to acting left chancellor. Diligent and self-restrained, he was questioned at court about his plain living, and the emperor rewarded him. After several years in office he helped plan the Feng and Shan sacrifices and, together with Li Jing, served as ritual envoy. When the emperor paused at Puyang and asked what the ancients had called Diqu, Dexuan could not answer; Xu Jingzong explained the matter fully, and the emperor commended him. Jingzong was vain before others, but Dexuan knew it and did not quarrel with him; the court admired his forbearance. After the rites were completed, his noble rank was raised two grades. Because his younger brother Deyuan had received no title, Dexuan asked that the enfeoffment be split between them. The emperor agreed: Dexuan became Baron of Julu and Deyuan Baron of Le'an. Dexuan kept pace with the times and pleased his superiors without ever giving offense, yet contributed nothing of lasting substance. He died at sixty-nine and was posthumously honored as Grand Master of Splendid Happiness and military governor of Youzhou, with the posthumous name Respectful.
42
贊曰：高、竇雖緣外戚姻家，然自以才猷結天子，廁跡名臣，垂榮無窮，時有遇合，故見諸事業。 古來賢豪，不遭與運，埋光鏟采，與草木俱腐者，可勝吒哉！ 竇宗自魏訖唐，支胄扶疏數百年，所馮厚矣。
The historian remarks: The Gao and Dou clans rose through imperial marriage, yet it was their own ability that won the throne's trust. They took their places among the great ministers of the age and enjoyed glory that did not fade—when talent met opportunity, their deeds deserved to be recorded. How many gifted men of old never found their moment, whose brilliance was buried and left to rot with the grass and trees? It is enough to make one sigh. From the Northern Wei through the Tang, the Dou clan's branches spread across centuries on the strength of deep imperial favor.

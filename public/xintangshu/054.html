1
25%唐有鹽池十八，井六百四十，皆隸度支。 蒲州安邑、解縣有池五，總曰「兩池」，歲得鹽萬斛，以供京師。 鹽州五原有烏池、白池、瓦池、細項池，靈州有溫泉池、兩井池、長尾池、五泉池、紅桃池、回樂池、弘靜池、會州有河池，三州皆輸米以代鹽。 安北都護府有胡落池，歲得鹽萬四千斛，以給振武、天德。 黔州有井四十一，成州、巂州井各一，果、閬、開、通井百二十三，山南西院領之。 邛、眉、嘉有井十三，劍南西川院領之。 梓、遂、緜、合、昌、渝、瀘、資、榮、陵、簡有井四百六十，劍南東川院領之。 皆隨月督課。 幽州、大同橫野軍有鹽屯，每屯有丁有兵，歲得鹽二千八百斛，下者千五百斛。 負海州歲免租為鹽二萬斛以輸司農。 青、楚、海、滄、棣、杭、蘇等州，以鹽價市輕貨，亦輸司農。
Under the Tang there were eighteen salt ponds and six hundred forty salt wells, all administered by the Department of Revenue. Puzhou's Anyi and Jie counties had five ponds, known together as the "Two Pools," which produced ten thousand hu of salt each year for the capital. At Wuyuan in Yan Prefecture were the Black, White, Tile, and Fine-Item ponds; Ling Prefecture had the Hot Spring, Two Wells, Long Tail, Five Springs, Red Peach, Huile, and Hongjing ponds; and Huiz Prefecture had the River Pond—all three prefectures paid grain taxes in place of salt tribute. The Anbei Protectorate's Huluo Pond yielded fourteen thousand hu of salt a year, earmarked for the Zhenwu and Tiande garrisons. Qian Prefecture had forty-one wells; Cheng and Xi each had one; and Guo, Lang, Kai, and Tong together had one hundred twenty-three—all overseen by the Southwestern Shannan salt office. Qiong, Mei, and Jia prefectures maintained thirteen wells under the Western Sichuan salt office. Zi, Sui, Mian, He, Chang, Yu, Lu, Zi, Rong, Ling, and Jian had four hundred sixty wells combined, all under the Eastern Sichuan salt office. Each site was monitored month by month for production quotas. You Prefecture and Datong's Hengye Army maintained salt farms; every farm had civilian laborers and soldiers, producing up to twenty-eight hundred hu a year and no less than fifteen hundred. Coastal prefectures were excused from grain rents each year on condition that they delivered twenty thousand hu of salt to the Commissioner's office. Qing, Chu, Hai, Cang, Di, Hang, Su, and other prefectures converted salt revenues into lightweight goods for transport, likewise forwarding them to the Commissioner.
2
天寶、至德間，鹽每斗十錢。 乾元元年，鹽鐵、鑄錢使第五琦初變鹽法，就山海井竈近利之地置監院，游民業鹽者為亭戶，免雜傜。 盜鬻者論以法。 及琦為諸州榷鹽鐵使，盡榷天下鹽，斗加時價百錢而出之，為錢一百一十。
From the Tianbao through the Zhide reigns, salt sold for ten cash per dou. In 758, Salt and Iron Commissioner Diwu Qi overhauled the salt law: he placed inspectorates wherever mountains, seas, wells, or brine furnaces offered easy profit; itinerant laborers who took up salt-making were registered as pavilion households and freed from miscellaneous corvée. Illegal salt trading was prosecuted under the full force of the law. Once Qi became national salt-and-iron monopolist, he brought all salt under state control, adding one hundred cash to the market price per dou and selling it for one hundred ten cash.
3
自兵起，流庸未復，稅賦不足供費，鹽鐵使劉晏以為因民所急而稅之，則國足用。 於是上鹽法輕重之宜，以鹽吏多則州縣擾，出鹽鄉因舊監置吏，亭戶糶商人，縱其所之。 江、嶺去鹽遠者，有常平鹽，每商人不至，則減價以糶民，官收厚利而人不知貴。 晏又以鹽生霖潦則鹵薄，暵旱則土溜墳，乃隨時為令，遣吏曉導，倍於勸農。 吳、越、揚、楚鹽廩至數千，積鹽二萬餘石。 有漣水、湖州、越州、杭州四場，嘉興、海陵、鹽城、新亭、臨平、蘭亭、永嘉、大昌、候官、富都十監，歲得錢百餘萬緡，以當百餘州之賦。 自淮北置巡院十三，曰揚州、陳許、汴州、廬壽、白沙、淮西、甬橋、浙西、宋州、泗州、嶺南、兗鄆、鄭滑，捕私鹽者，姦盜為之衰息。 然諸道加榷鹽錢，商人舟所過有稅。 晏奏罷州縣率稅，禁堰埭邀以利者。 晏之始至也，鹽利歲纔四十萬緡，至大曆末，六百餘萬緡。 天下之賦，鹽利居半，宮闈服御、軍饟、百官祿俸皆仰給焉。 明年而晏罷。
After the rebellion erupted, refugees had still not returned home and ordinary taxes could not cover expenses. Salt Commissioner Liu Yan argued that if the state taxed what people could not do without, the treasury would be adequately supplied. Accordingly he memorialized on the proper balance of the salt levies: too many salt officials meant trouble for local government, so in producing districts he kept the old inspectorates; pavilion households sold to merchants, who could ship salt wherever demand lay. In the Yangzi basin and Lingnan, far from producing areas, the state maintained ever-normal salt reserves; when merchants failed to arrive, prices were cut to sell to common people—the treasury still took a heavy profit while the public scarcely felt the burden. Yan also knew that heavy rains diluted the brine and drought washed away the soil, so he issued seasonal orders and sent officials to instruct producers—devoting twice the zeal he would have shown in promoting agriculture. The Wu, Yue, Yang, and Chu circuits maintained thousands of salt storehouses holding more than twenty thousand shi of salt. Four production fields—Lianshui, Huzhou, Yuezhou, and Hangzhou—and ten salt offices—Jiaxing, Hailing, Yancheng, Xinting, Linping, Lanting, Yongjia, Dachang, Houguan, and Fudu—together yielded more than a million strings of cash annually, equal to the tax revenue of over a hundred prefectures. North of the Huai, thirteen patrol stations—from Yangzhou to Zhengzhou-Hua Prefecture—were established to hunt smugglers, and illegal traffic accordingly subsided. Even so, regional circuits piled on salt surcharges, taxing merchant vessels at every stop along the waterways. Yan petitioned to end ad hoc local surcharges and to outlaw dams and ferry barriers erected purely for extortion. When Yan took office, annual salt revenues barely reached four hundred thousand strings; by the close of the Dali reign they exceeded six million. Salt revenues accounted for half the empire's tax intake; the palace wardrobe, army rations, and official salaries all depended on them. The following year Yan was removed from office.
4
貞元四年，淮南節度使陳少游奏加民賦，自此江淮鹽每斗亦增二百，為錢三百一十，其後復增六十，河中兩池鹽每斗為錢三百七十。 江淮豪賈射利，或時倍之，官收不能過半，民始怨矣。
In 788, Huainan governor Chen Shaoyou won approval to raise civilian levies, and Jiang-Huai salt jumped two hundred cash per dou to three hundred ten; later it rose another sixty, while the Two Pools salt in Hezhong sold for three hundred seventy per dou. Wealthy Jiang-Huai merchants hoarded profits, sometimes doubling the price; the state collected less than half the proceeds, and popular resentment began to stir.
5
劉晏鹽法既成，商人納絹以代鹽利者，每緡加錢二百，以備將士春服。 包佶為汴東水陸運、兩稅、鹽鐵使，許以漆器、瑇瑁、綾綺代鹽價，雖不可用者亦高估而售之，廣虛數以罔上。 亭戶冒法，私鬻不絕，巡捕之卒，遍于州縣。 鹽估益貴，商人乘時射利，遠鄉貧民困高估，至有淡食者。 巡吏既多，官冗傷財，當時病之。 其後軍費日增，鹽價寖貴，有以穀數斗易鹽一升。 私糴犯法，未嘗少息。
Once Liu Yan's system was in place, merchants paying silk in lieu of salt dues paid an extra two hundred cash per string to fund soldiers' spring uniforms. Commissioner Bao Ji allowed merchants to pay salt dues in lacquerware, tortoise shell, and brocades—even worthless goods were assigned inflated prices and accepted, swelling the reported totals to mislead the court. Pavilion households broke the law and private sales never stopped; patrol troops fanned out through every prefecture and county. Salt grew steadily dearer as merchants timed their profits; poor villagers in remote districts, crushed by inflated prices, sometimes went without salt altogether. The proliferation of patrol officers meant wasteful bureaucracy—a grievance widely noted at the time. As military costs mounted, salt prices kept climbing until several dou of grain might buy no more than a sheng of salt. Illicit private buying never slackened.
6
順宗時始減江淮鹽價，每斗為錢二百五十，河中兩池鹽，斗錢三百。 增雲安、渙陽、塗𣉯三監。 其後鹽鐵使李錡奏江淮鹽斗減錢十以便民，未幾復舊。 方是時，錡盛貢獻以固寵，朝廷大臣，皆餌以厚貨，鹽鐵之利，積于私室，而國用耗屈，榷鹽法大壞，多為虛估，率千錢不滿百三十而已。 兵部侍郎李巽為使，以鹽利皆歸度支，物無虛估，天下糶鹽稅茶，其贏六百六十五萬緡。 初歲之利，如劉晏之季年，其後則三倍晏時矣。 兩池鹽利，歲收百五十餘萬緡。 四方豪商猾賈，雜處解縣，主以郎官，其佐貳皆御史。 鹽民田園籍於縣，而令不得以縣民治之。
Under Emperor Shunzong, Jiang-Huai salt was cut to two hundred fifty cash per dou and the Two Pools salt in Hezhong to three hundred. Three new salt offices were added at Yun'an, Huanyang, and Tujin. Later Commissioner Li Kai proposed cutting Jiang-Huai salt by ten cash per dou to ease the burden on the people, but the reduction was soon withdrawn. Li Kai showered the throne with tribute to secure favor and bought off senior ministers with rich gifts; salt profits pooled in his private coffers while the treasury ran dry; the monopoly system collapsed into hollow valuations, so a thousand-cash assessment might yield barely one hundred thirty in real revenue. When Vice Minister Li Xun took charge, salt revenues reverted to the Department of Revenue and fictitious valuations ended; nationwide salt sales and tea taxes together yielded six million six hundred fifty thousand strings in net profit. First-year profits matched Liu Yan's final year; thereafter they tripled what Yan had achieved. The Two Pools alone yielded more than one million five hundred thousand strings a year. Powerful and unscrupulous merchants from every quarter congregated in Jie County; a court gentleman headed the office and censors served as deputies. Salt workers' land remained on county registers, yet magistrates were barred from governing them as ordinary local subjects.
7
憲宗之討淮西也，度支使皇甫鎛加劍南東西兩川、山南西道鹽估以供軍。 貞元中，盜鬻兩池鹽一石者死，至元和中，減死流天德五城，鎛奏論死如初。 一斗以上杖背，沒其車驢，能捕斗鹽者賞千錢； 節度觀察使以判官、州以司錄錄事參軍察私鹽，漏一石以上罰課料； 鬻兩池鹽者，坊市居邸主人、市儈皆論坐； 盜刮鹻土一斗，比鹽一升。 州縣團保相察，比於貞元加酷矣。 自兵興，河北鹽法羈縻而已。 至皇甫鎛又奏置榷鹽使，如江淮榷法，犯禁歲多。 及田弘正舉魏博歸朝廷，穆宗命河北罷榷鹽。 戶部侍郎張平叔議榷鹽法弊，請糶鹽可以富國，詔公卿議其可否。 中書舍人韋處厚、兵部侍郎韓愈條詰之，以為不可，平叔屈服。
During Xianzong's campaign against Huaixi, Fiscal Commissioner Huangfu Bo raised salt assessments in both Sichuan circuits and Southwest Shannan to fund the war. Under Zhenyuan, smuggling a shi of Two Pools salt meant death; in Yuanhe the penalty was reduced to exile at Tiande and Wucheng until Bo petitioned to restore the death penalty. For a dou or more, offenders were flogged and their carts and donkeys confiscated; informants who seized a dou of illicit salt received one thousand cash in reward. Governors and observation commissioners assigned aides, and prefectures assigned registrars, to hunt private salt; failing to detect a shi or more cost officials their salary supplements. Anyone dealing in Two Pools salt—landlords of ward inns, lodging-house masters, and brokers—was subject to prosecution. Scraping a dou of salty soil was punished as if one had smuggled a sheng of salt. Prefectures and counties enforced mutual surveillance through neighborhood responsibility groups—harsher even than under Zhenyuan. Since the rebellion, Hebei's salt regime had been only loosely enforced. When Huangfu Bo petitioned to establish salt monopolists in Hebei on the Jiang-Huai model, violations multiplied year by year. After Tian Hongzheng returned Weibo to imperial control, Muzong abolished the salt monopoly in Hebei. Vice Minister Zhang Pingshu criticized the monopoly system's abuses and argued that state salt sales could enrich the country; the throne ordered senior officials to debate the proposal. Secretariat Drafter Wei Chuhou and Vice Minister Han Yu rebutted the plan point by point and declared it unworkable; Pingshu conceded.
8
是時奉天鹵池生水柏，以灰一斛得鹽十二斤，利倍鹻鹵。 文宗時，采灰一斗，比鹽一斤論罪。 開成末，詔私鹽月再犯者，易縣令，罰刺史俸； 十犯，則罰觀察、判官課料。
At that time the Fengtian brine ponds grew water cypress; a hu of burnt ash yielded twelve jin of salt—twice the profit from ordinary brine. Under Wenzong, collecting a dou of ash was penalized as if one had smuggled a jin of salt. Late in Kaicheng, an edict declared that two private-salt violations within a month would cost a county magistrate his post and dock a prefect's salary. Ten violations would forfeit salary supplements from the observation commissioner and his aides.
9
宣宗即位，茶、鹽之法益密，糶鹽少、私盜多者，讁觀察、判官，不計十犯。 戶部侍郎、判度支盧弘止以兩池鹽法敝，遣巡院官司空輿更立新法，其課倍入，遷榷鹽使。 以壕籬者，鹽池之隄禁，有盜壞與鬻鹻皆死，鹽盜持弓矢者亦皆死刑。 兵部侍郎、判度支周墀又言：「兩池鹽盜販者，迹其居處，保、社按罪。 鬻五石，市二石，亭戶盜糶二石，皆死。 」是時江、吳羣盜，以所剽物易茶鹽，不受者焚其室廬，吏不敢枝梧，鎮戍、場鋪、堰埭以關通致富。 宣宗乃擇嘗更兩畿輔望縣令者為監院官。 戶部侍郎裴休為鹽鐵使，上鹽法八事，其法皆施行，兩池榷課大增。
At Xuanzong's accession tea and salt controls tightened; if official sales lagged while smuggling flourished, observation commissioners and aides were censured without waiting for ten violations. Fiscal Vice Minister Lu Hongzhi, finding the Two Pools system broken, sent inspection-office official Sikong Yu to draft a new law; revenues doubled and Lu was promoted to Salt Commissioner. The moat-fences guarding the salt ponds were sacrosanct: breaking them or selling brine salt meant death, and smugglers caught armed with bow and arrow likewise faced execution. Fiscal Vice Minister Zhou Chi added: "When Two Pools salt is smuggled, track the culprits' dwellings and hold ward and community groups liable according to the offense." Selling five shi, retailing two shi, or pavilion households illicitly selling two shi—all carried the death penalty. At that time Jiang and Wu bandits traded loot for tea and salt; those who refused saw their homes burned, and officials dared not resist. Garrison posts, salt stations, dams, and ferry barriers all grew wealthy charging transit fees. Xuanzong then appointed former magistrates of prominent counties in the capital regions to head the salt inspectorates. When Vice Minister Pei Xiu became Salt Commissioner he submitted an eight-point salt reform; every measure took effect and Two Pools revenues surged.
10
其後兵遍天下，諸鎮擅利，兩池為河中節度使王重榮所有，歲貢鹽三千車。 中官田令孜募新軍五十四都，餫轉不足，乃倡議兩池復歸鹽鐵使，而重榮不奉詔，至舉兵反，僖宗為再出，然而卒不能奪。
Later, as war engulfed the empire and regional armies seized revenues for themselves, the Two Pools passed to Hezhong governor Wang Chongrong, who sent three thousand cartloads of salt in annual tribute. Eunuch Tian Lingzi raised fifty-four new army companies but could not supply them, so he proposed restoring the Two Pools to central control; Wang Chongrong defied the edict, took up arms, and even after Emperor Xizong marched against him twice, the court never regained the ponds.
11
唐初無酒禁。 乾元元年，京師酒貴，肅宗以稟食方屈，乃禁京城酤酒，期以麥熟如初。 二年，饑，復禁酤，非光祿祭祀、燕蕃客，不御酒。
Early Tang imposed no restrictions on wine. In 758, as wine prices soared in the capital and grain stores ran low, Suzong banned wine-selling in the capital, promising to lift the ban once the wheat harvest came in. The next year, famine returned and the ban was reimposed; aside from state sacrifices and entertaining foreign envoys, the court itself abstained from wine.
12
廣德二年，定天下酤戶以月收稅。 建中元年，罷之。 三年，復禁民酤，以佐軍費，置肆釀酒，斛收直三千，州縣總領，醨薄私釀者論其罪。 尋以京師四方所湊，罷榷。 貞元二年，復禁京城、畿縣酒，天下置肆以酤者，斗錢百五十，免其傜役，獨淮南、忠武、宣武、河東榷麴而已。 元和六年，罷京師酤肆，以榷酒錢隨兩稅青苗斂之。 大和八年，遂罷京師榷酤。 凡天下榷酒為錢百五十六萬餘緡，而釀費居三之一，貧戶逃酤不在焉。 昭宗世，以用度不足，易京畿近鎮麴法，復榷酒以贍軍，鳳翔節度使李茂貞方顓其利，按兵請入奏利害，天子遽罷之。
In 763, wine shops empire-wide were registered to pay a monthly tax. In 780 the tax was abolished. In 782 private wine-selling was banned again to fund the army; government shops brewed wine at three thousand cash per hu under prefectural oversight, and weak or illicit home brewing was prosecuted. Soon afterward, since the capital drew goods from every direction, the monopoly was lifted. In 786 wine was again restricted in the capital and surrounding counties; licensed shops elsewhere paid one hundred fifty cash per dou and were freed from corvée; only Huainan, Zhongwu, Xuanwu, and Hedong retained yeast monopolies. In 811 capital wine shops were closed and wine tax was folded into the two-tax and green-sprout assessments. In 834 the capital wine monopoly was abolished entirely. Empire-wide wine revenues totaled one million five hundred sixty thousand strings, though brewing costs consumed a third—and poor households who evaded licensed selling were not included in the tally. Under Zhaozong, when funds ran short the court swapped yeast regulations in nearby capital districts and revived the wine monopoly to pay the army; Fengxiang governor Li Maozhen, who controlled the profits, marched troops to the capital to argue the policy's merits; the emperor immediately canceled it.
13
初，德宗納戶部侍郎趙贊議，稅天下茶、漆、竹、木，十取一，以為常平本錢。 及出奉天，乃悼悔，下詔亟罷之。 及朱泚平，佞臣希意興利者益進。 貞元八年，以水災減稅，明年，諸道鹽鐵使張滂奏：出茶州縣若山及商人要路，以三等定估，十稅其一。 自是歲得錢四十萬緡，然水旱亦未嘗拯之也。
Early on, Dezong adopted Vice Minister Zhao Zan's plan to levy a ten-percent tax on tea, lacquer, bamboo, and timber empire-wide to fund the Ever-Normal system. After fleeing to Fengtian he regretted the policy and urgently abolished the levies by edict. Once Zhu Ci was defeated, flatterers who curried favor by inventing new revenues gained further influence. In 792 flood relief cut taxes; the next year Commissioner Zhang Pang proposed grading tea by three price tiers at producing counties, mountain districts, and merchant routes, with a ten-percent levy on each. Thereafter the tax yielded four hundred thousand strings annually—yet none of it was ever spent on flood or drought relief.
14
穆宗即位，兩鎮用兵，帑藏空虛，禁中起百尺樓，費不可勝計。 鹽鐵使王播圖寵以自幸，乃增天下茶稅，率百錢增五十。 江淮、浙東西、嶺南、福建、荊襄茶，播自領之，兩川以戶部領之。 天下茶加斤至二十兩，播又奏加取焉。 右拾遺李珏上疏諫曰：「榷率起於養兵，今邊境無虞，而厚斂傷民，不可一也。 茗飲，人之所資，重稅則價必增，貧弱益困，不可二也。 山澤之饒，其出不訾，論稅以售多為利，價騰踊則市者稀，不可三也。 」其後王涯判二使，置榷茶使，徙民茶樹於官場，焚其舊積者，天下大怨。 令狐楚代為鹽鐵使兼榷茶使，復令納榷，加價而已。 李石為相，以茶稅皆歸鹽鐵，復貞元之制。
At Muzong's accession two military districts were at war, the treasury stood empty, and the palace began constructing a hundred-foot tower at immeasurable cost. Seeking imperial favor, Salt Commissioner Wang Bo raised the tea tax nationwide, adding fifty cash to every hundred owed. Wang Bo personally oversaw tea in Jiang-Huai, Zhe East and West, Lingnan, Fujian, and Jing-Xiang; the Ministry of Revenue handled the Two Sichuan circuits. Producers were required to add up to twenty liang of weight per jin of tea nationwide, and Bo petitioned for further surcharges. Right Reminder Li Jue memorialized in protest: "These monopoly rates were meant to fund the army, yet the frontiers are quiet while heavy levies still burden the people—that alone is reason enough to stop." "Tea is a daily necessity; steep taxes will push prices up and squeeze the poor still harder—that is a second reason." "Mountain and marsh produce costs little to harvest; taxing by volume maximizes revenue on paper, but inflated prices will drive buyers away—that is a third reason." Later Wang Ya took charge of both commissions, created a tea monopolist, uprooted private tea trees to state fields, and burned old stockpiles—the empire boiled with resentment. Linghu Chu succeeded him as Salt and Tea Commissioner, restoring payment of monopoly dues with nothing more than a price surcharge. When Li Shi became chief minister, tea taxes reverted to the Salt Commission and the Zhenyuan system was restored.
15
武宗即位，鹽鐵轉運使崔珙又增江淮茶稅。 是時茶商所過州縣有重稅，或掠奪舟車，露積雨中，諸道置邸以收稅，謂之「搨地錢」，故私販益起。 大中初，鹽鐵轉運使裴休著條約：私鬻三犯皆三百斤，乃論死； 長行羣旅，茶雖少皆死； 雇載三犯至五百斤、居舍儈保四犯至千斤者，皆死； 園戶私鬻百斤以上，杖背，三犯，加重傜； 伐園失業者，刺史、縣令以縱私鹽論。 廬、壽、淮南皆加半稅，私商給自首之帖，天下稅茶增倍貞元。 江淮茶為大摸，一斤至五十兩。 諸道鹽鐵使于悰每斤增稅錢五，謂之「剩茶錢」，自是斤兩復舊。
At Wuzong's accession, Transport Commissioner Cui Gong raised Jiang-Huai tea taxes again. Tea merchants then faced crushing local levies at every stop; some had boats and carts seized, goods left piled in the rain; circuits set up relay inns to collect what was called "ground-flattening money"—so smuggling only increased. Early in the Dazhong reign, Salt and Iron Transport Commissioner Pei Xiu issued a code of rules: anyone caught selling tea privately three times, with each offense involving three hundred jin or more, would be sentenced to death; Merchants traveling in large caravans over long routes faced the death penalty even when they carried only a small amount of tea; Hired haulers convicted three times for amounts up to five hundred jin, and lodging-house keepers and guarantors convicted four times for amounts up to one thousand jin, were all executed; Tea growers who sold more than one hundred jin on the black market were flogged; after three convictions, they were subjected to heavier forced labor duties; If tea gardens were destroyed and growers left without work, the local prefect and county magistrate were held accountable under the same penalties applied for tolerating illicit salt trade. Lu, Shou, and Huainan each saw their tea levy raised by fifty percent; unauthorized dealers who turned themselves in received official amnesty slips; and nationwide tea revenue came to twice what it had been under the Zhenyuan reign. The finest Jianghuai tea came in large molded cakes, with a single jin selling for as much as fifty taels of silver. Circuit salt-and-iron commissioner Yu Cong imposed an extra five-cash tax on each jin, dubbed the "leftover tea fee," after which the official weight measure was restored to its former standard.
16
凡銀、銅、鐵、錫之冶一百六十八。 陝、宣、潤、饒、衢、信五州，銀冶五十八，銅冶九十六，鐵山五，錫山二，鉛山四。 汾州礬山七。 麟德二年，廢陝州銅冶四十八。
The empire maintained one hundred sixty-eight smelting works for silver, copper, iron, and tin combined. Shaan, Xuan, Run, Rao, Qu, and Xin—the five prefectures together accounted for fifty-eight silver works, ninety-six copper works, five iron mines, two tin mines, and four lead mines. Fen Prefecture alone had seven alum-mining sites. In 665, forty-eight copper smelting operations in Shaan Prefecture were shut down.
17
開元十五年，初稅伊陽五重山銀、錫。 德宗時戶部侍郎韓洄建議，山澤之利宜歸王者，自是皆隸鹽鐵使。
In 727, the government began levying taxes on silver and tin mined at Wuzhong Mountain in Yiyang. Under Emperor Dezong, Vice Minister of Revenue Han Hui argued that revenue from mountains and waterways should accrue to the throne; thereafter every such operation fell under the Salt and Iron Commissioner's jurisdiction.
18
元和初，天下銀冶廢者四十，歲采銀萬二千兩，銅二十六萬六千斤，鐵二百七萬斤，錫五萬斤，鉛無常數。
Early in the Yuanhe reign, forty silver works nationwide had already been closed; yearly production stood at twelve thousand taels of silver, two hundred sixty-six thousand jin of copper, two million seven hundred thousand jin of iron, and fifty thousand jin of tin, while lead output was not regularly recorded.
19
開成元年，復以山澤之利歸州縣，刺史選吏主之。 其後諸州牟利以自殖，舉天下不過七萬餘緡，不能當一縣之茶稅。 及宣宗增河湟戍兵衣絹五十二萬餘匹，鹽鐵轉運使裴休請復歸鹽鐵使以供國用，增銀冶二、鐵山七十一，廢銅冶二十七、鉛山一。 天下歲率銀二萬五千兩、銅六十五萬五千斤、鉛十一萬四千斤、錫萬七千斤、鐵五十三萬二千斤。
In 836, control of mountain and marsh resources was devolved back to local prefectures and counties, with each prefect appointing officials to oversee operations. Local prefectures then kept the proceeds for their own benefit, so that empire-wide revenue from these sources never exceeded seventy thousand strings of cash—less than what a single county brought in from tea taxes alone. After Emperor Xuanzong raised Hexi frontier garrisons' cloth and silk allotments by more than five hundred twenty thousand bolts, Salt and Iron Transport Commissioner Pei Xiu persuaded the court to recentralize mining under the Salt and Iron Commission to fund state expenses—adding two silver works and seventy-one iron mines while closing twenty-seven copper works and one lead mine. Under this arrangement the empire annually produced on average twenty-five thousand taels of silver, six hundred fifty-five thousand jin of copper, one hundred fourteen thousand jin of lead, seventeen thousand jin of tin, and five hundred thirty-two thousand jin of iron.
20
隋末行五銖白錢，天下盜起，私鑄錢行。 千錢初重二斤，其後愈輕，不及一斤，鐵葉、皮紙皆以為錢。 高祖入長安，民間行綫環錢，其製輕小，凡八九萬纔滿半斛。
In the final years of the Sui dynasty, lightweight five-zhu coins were in use; rebellion spread across the land, and illicit coinage flourished. A thousand coins at first weighed two jin, but over time they grew so debased that the same sum weighed less than one jin; people even passed iron scraps and paper as currency. When the founding emperor entered Chang'an, the populace traded in flimsy ring-linked coins so tiny that eighty or ninety thousand of them barely filled half a bushel measure.
21
武德四年，鑄「開元通寶」，徑八分，重二銖四參，積十錢重一兩，得輕重大小之中，其文以八分、篆、隸三體。 洛、幷、幽、益、桂等州皆置監。 賜秦王、齊王三鑪，右僕射裴寂一鑪以鑄。 盜鑄者論死，沒其家屬。 其後盜鑄漸起。
In 621 the court minted the Kaiyuan Tongbao coin, eight fen in diameter and two zhu four can in weight, so that ten coins equaled one liang—a carefully calibrated standard—with the legend written in regular, seal, and clerical script forms. Mints were established at Luoyang, Bing, You, Yi, Gui, and other major prefectures. The Prince of Qin and the Prince of Qi each received three minting furnaces, and Right Vice Director Pei Ji was given one furnace of his own. Anyone caught counterfeiting coins was put to death, and the offender's entire household was seized by the state. Before long, illicit coinage began to spread again.
22
顯慶五年，以惡錢多，官為市之，以一善錢售五惡錢，民間藏惡錢以待禁弛。 乾封元年，改鑄「乾封泉寶」錢，徑寸，重二銖六分，以一當舊錢之十。 踰年而舊錢多廢，明年，以商賈不通，米帛踊貴，復行開元通寶錢，天下皆鑄之。 然私錢犯法日蕃，有以舟筏鑄江中者。 詔所在納惡錢，而姦亦不息。 儀鳳中，瀕江民多私鑄錢為業，詔巡江官督捕，載銅、錫、鑞過百斤者沒官。 四年，命東都糶米粟，斗別納惡錢百，少府、司農毀之。 是時鑄多錢賤，米粟踊貴，乃罷少府鑄，尋復舊。 永淳元年，私鑄者抵死，鄰、保、里、坊、村正皆從坐。 武后時，錢非穿穴及鐵錫銅液，皆得用之，熟銅、排斗、沙澀之錢皆售，自是盜鑄蜂起，江淮游民依大山陂海以鑄，吏莫能捕。
In 660, faced with a flood of debased currency, the government offered to buy it up at a rate of one good coin for five bad ones—yet people simply hoarded the inferior coins, betting that enforcement would eventually ease. In 666 the court replaced the old currency with new Qianfeng Quanbao coins, one cun across and two zhu six fen in weight, each valued at ten of the previous coins. Within a year most of the old coinage had fallen out of use; the following year commerce ground to a halt and grain and cloth prices spiked, forcing the government to reinstate the Kaiyuan Tongbao and order mints throughout the empire to produce it again. Private minting violations nonetheless multiplied daily, with some counterfeiters operating from boats and rafts moored mid-river. The throne ordered every region to collect and turn in debased coins, but counterfeiting continued unabated. During the Yifeng reign, riverside communities turned illicit minting into a livelihood; the court dispatched river patrol officers to hunt down offenders and confiscated any shipment of copper, tin, or lead exceeding one hundred jin. In the fourth year of the era, the Eastern Capital was instructed to sell grain and accept one hundred debased coins per dou as payment, which the Court of the Treasury and the Directorate of Grain then melted down. Coin production had become so excessive that money lost value while grain prices climbed; the court briefly shut down Treasury mints, only to reopen them shortly afterward. In 682 anyone who minted coins privately faced the death penalty, and neighbors, guarantors, block wardens, lane overseers, and village heads were all held liable as accomplices. Under Empress Wu, even unholed coins and pieces cast from iron, tin, and copper slag circulated freely, and worn copper of every inferior grade found buyers; counterfeiting then exploded, as Jianghuai drifters set up hidden mints in remote mountains, marshes, and along the coast, beyond the reach of local officials.
23
先天之際，兩京錢益濫，郴、衡錢纔有輪郭，鐵錫五銖之屬皆可用之。 或鎔錫摸錢，須臾百十。 開元初，宰相宋璟請禁惡錢，行二銖四參錢，毀舊錢不可用者。 江淮有官鑪錢、偏鑪錢、稜錢、時錢，遣監察御史蕭隱之使江淮，率戶出惡錢，捕責甚峻，上青錢皆輸官，小惡者沈江湖，市井不通，物價益貴，隱之坐貶官。 宋璟又請出米十萬斛收惡錢，少府毀之。 十一年，詔所在加鑄，禁賣銅錫及造銅器者。 二十年，千錢以重六斤四兩為率，每錢重二銖四參，禁缺頓、沙澀、盪染、白彊、黑彊之錢。 首者，官為市之。 銅一斤為錢八十。
By the Xiantian era, currency in the two capitals had become severely debased; coins from Chen and Heng were so crude they barely had rims, and iron, tin, and five-zhu pieces all passed as legal tender. Some counterfeiters simply melted tin and cast coins from molds, turning out a hundred or more in moments. Early in the Kaiyuan reign, Chancellor Song Jing proposed outlawing debased currency, enforcing the two-zhu four-can standard, and recalling and melting down all unusable old coins. The Jianghuai region was awash in official-mint coins, off-spec furnace pieces, rimmed coins, and assorted contemporary issues; the court sent Censor Xiao Yinzhí to compel every household to surrender debased currency under harsh enforcement—fine greenish coins went to the treasury while inferior ones were dumped in rivers and lakes; commerce seized up, prices soared, and Yinzhí himself was demoted for the fiasco. Song Jing then proposed releasing one hundred thousand hu of grain to buy up debased coins for the Treasury to melt down. In the eleventh year of Kaiyuan, the throne ordered every region to step up coin production while prohibiting the sale of copper and tin and the making of copper goods. In 732 the standard was fixed at six jin four liang per thousand coins, or two zhu four can each, and chipped, gritty, tampered, white-streaked, and black-streaked coins were outlawed. Those who reported violations had their seized coins bought up by the government. Each jin of copper was allotted to produce eighty coins.
24
二十二年，宰相張九齡建議：「古者以布帛菽粟不可尺寸抄勺而均，乃為錢以通貿易。 官鑄所入無幾，而工費多，宜縱民鑄。 」議下百官，宰相裴耀卿、黃門侍郎李林甫、河南少尹蕭炅、祕書監崔沔皆以為「嚴斷惡錢則人知禁，稅銅折役則官冶可成，計估度庸則私錢以利薄而自息。 若許私鑄，則下皆棄農而競利矣」。 左監門衞錄事參軍事劉秩曰：「今之錢，古之下幣也。 若捨之任人，則上無以御下，下無以事上，不可一也。 物賤傷農，錢輕傷賈，物重則錢輕，錢輕由乎物多，多則作法收之使少，物少則作法布之使輕，奈何假人？ 不可二也。 鑄錢不雜鉛鐵則無利，雜則錢惡。 今塞私鑄之路，人猶冒死，況設陷穽誘之？ 不可三也。 鑄錢無利則人不鑄，有利則去南畝者眾，不可四也。 人富則不可以賞勸，貧則不可以威禁。 法不行，人不理，繇貧富不齊。 若得鑄錢，貧者服役於富室，富室乘而益恣，不可五也。 夫錢重繇人日滋於前，而鑪不加舊。 公錢與銅價頗等，故破重錢為輕錢，銅之不贍，在採用者眾也。 銅之為兵不如鐵，為器不如漆。 禁銅則人無所用，盜鑄者少，公錢不破，人不犯死，錢又日增，是一舉而四美兼也。 」是時公卿皆以縱民鑄為不便，於是下詔禁惡錢而已。 信安郡王禕復言國用不足，請縱私鑄，議者皆畏禕帝弟之貴，莫敢與抗，獨倉部郎中韋伯陽以為不可，禕議亦格。
In 734 Chancellor Zhang Jiuling argued: "In ancient times cloth, silk, grain, and beans could not be apportioned precisely by measure, so coinage was invented to grease the wheels of commerce. State mints bring in little while costing a fortune to run—the better course is to let the people mint coins themselves. " The proposal went before the full bureaucracy. Chancellor Pei Yaoji, Vice Director of the Yellow Gate Li Linfu, Vice Prefect of Henan Xiao Jiong, and Secretariat Director Cui Hong all countered that strict suppression of debased coinage would teach people to obey the law; taxing copper and converting labor dues would make state smelting viable; and setting valuation standards for corvée would starve private minting of its profit margin. Allow private minting, and the common people will abandon the plow for the lure of easy coin." Liu Zhi, a records officer in the Left Gate Guard Regiment, said: "Money today is what the ancients called the lowest grade of currency." If the state renounces control and leaves minting to private hands, the throne loses leverage over its subjects and subjects lose their basis for serving the throne. That alone is sufficient reason to forbid it. Cheap produce ruins farmers; debased coinage ruins traders. When goods are plentiful, money loses value—and the remedy is to absorb surplus goods, not flood the market with coin. When goods are scarce, the state should release money to ease prices. Why would we hand that power to private minters? That is the second reason it must be rejected. Coin minting turns a profit only by adulterating the metal, and adulteration produces bad money. Even with private minting outlawed, people still risk execution to do it—how much worse would it be if the law openly baited them with profit? That is the third objection. If minting pays nothing, no one will do it; if it pays well, peasants will abandon the fields in droves. That is the fourth reason to forbid it. The rich cannot be moved by reward, and the poor cannot be governed by fear of punishment. When wealth and poverty diverge too sharply, law loses force and society falls out of order. If minting were permitted, the poor would become bondsmen to the rich, and the wealthy would exploit the power of coin to act with even greater impunity. That is the fifth reason. Full-weight coin kept piling up in private hands, yet the number of official mints had not grown since earlier times. Because official coin was worth nearly its weight in raw copper, people melted good coins into bad ones—and copper ran short simply because too many hands competed for it. Copper makes inferior weapons compared with iron, and inferior utensils compared with lacquerware. Ban private use of copper, and copper stops draining into illicit mints; official coin stops being melted down, counterfeiting drops, and the supply of sound money grows—all from a single policy yielding four gains at once. At that point the court overwhelmingly agreed that private minting was a bad idea, and the emperor issued an edict banning debased coin—nothing more. Prince Xin'an, Li Yi, raised the shortage of revenue again and asked to legalize private minting. Debates fell silent—few dared contest an imperial prince—until Wei Boyang of the Granaries Bureau alone objected, and Li Yi's plan was set aside.
25
二十六年，宣、潤等州初置錢監，兩京用錢稍善，米粟價益下。 其後錢又漸惡，詔出銅所在置監，鑄開元通寶錢，京師庫藏皆滿。 天下盜鑄益起，廣陵、丹楊、宣城尤甚。 京師權豪，歲歲取之，舟車相屬。 江淮偏鑪錢數十種，雜以鐵錫，輕漫無復錢形。 公鑄者號官鑪錢，一以當偏鑪錢七八，富商往往藏之，以易江淮私鑄者。 兩京錢有鵝眼、古文、綫環之別，每貫重不過三四斤，至翦鐵而緡之。
In Kaiyuan 26, new mint supervisors were established in Xuancheng, Runzhou, and elsewhere; currency in both capitals improved, and grain prices dropped. Debasement returned; the throne ordered mints wherever copper was mined to cast Kaiyuan Tongbao, until the capital treasuries overflowed. Counterfeiting spread empire-wide, worst in Yangzhou, Danyang, and Xuancheng. Capital elites bought up debased coin year after year, with convoys of boats and carts rolling in without pause. Private mints along the Yangzi-Huai corridor produced dozens of varieties adulterated with iron and tin—so thin and crude they barely looked like money. Official coin bought seven or eight private coins; merchants hoarded the good stuff and traded it for cheap Yangzi counterfeits. Capitals circulated goose-eye, ancient-script, and thread-ring coin—each thousand-coin string weighing barely three or four pounds—until people were stringing clipped iron as cash.
26
宰相李林甫請出絹布三百萬匹，平估收錢，物價踊貴，訴者日萬人。 兵部侍郎楊國忠欲招權以市恩，揚鞭市門曰：「行當復之。 」明日，詔復行舊錢。 天寶十一載，又出錢三十萬緡易兩市惡錢，出左藏庫排斗錢，許民易之。 國忠又言錢非鐵錫、銅沙、穿穴、古文，皆得用之。
Chancellor Li Linfu proposed releasing three million bolts of silk and cloth to buy up coin at assessed rates, but prices spiked and ten thousand petitioners poured in daily. Yang Guozhong, Vice Minister of War, sought to seize favor by playing savior; whip in hand at the market gate he promised: "The old currency will be restored." The next day an edict restored the previous coin standard. In Tianbao 11, the court spent three hundred thousand strings to swap out bad money in the markets, issuing sorted coin from the Left Treasury for public exchange. Yang Guozhong went further, declaring that coin adulterated with iron, tin, copper slag, holes, or worn ancient inscriptions should all remain legal tender.
27
是時增調農人鑄錢，既非所習，皆不聊生。 內作判官韋倫請厚價募工，繇是役用減而鼓鑄多。 天下鑪九十九：絳州三十，揚、潤、宣、鄂、蔚皆十，益、郴皆五，洋州三，定州一。 每鑪歲鑄錢三千三百緡，役丁匠三十，費銅二萬一千二百斤、鑞三千七百斤、錫五百斤。 每千錢費錢七百五十。 天下歲鑄三十二萬七千緡。
Farmers conscripted into mint labor knew nothing of the craft and could barely survive. Wei Lun of the Palace Workshops proposed hiring skilled workers at premium wages, cutting forced peasant labor while boosting output. Ninety-nine mints operated empire-wide: thirty at Jiangzhou; ten each at Yangzhou, Runzhou, Xuancheng, Ezhou, and Yuzhou; five each at Yizhou and Chenzhou; three at Yang Prefecture; one at Dingzhou. Each mint produced 3,300 strings a year with thirty workers, consuming 21,200 jin of copper, 3,700 jin of lead, and 500 jin of tin. Minting cost seven hundred fifty cash per thousand produced. Empire-wide annual output reached 327,000 strings.
28
肅宗乾元元年，經費不給，鑄錢使第五琦鑄「乾元重寶」錢，徑一寸，每緡重十斤，與開元通寶參用，以一當十，亦號「乾元十當錢」。 先是諸鑪鑄錢窳薄，鎔破錢及佛像，謂之「盤陀」，皆鑄為私錢，犯者杖死。 第五琦為相，復命絳州諸鑪鑄重輪乾元錢，徑一寸二分，其文亦曰「乾元重寶」，背之外郭為重輪，每緡重十二斤，與開元通寶錢並行，以一當五十。 是時民間行三錢，大而重稜者亦號「重稜錢」。 法既屢易，物價騰踊，米斗錢至七千，餓死者滿道。 初有「虛錢」，京師人人私鑄，併小錢，壞鍾、像，犯禁者愈眾。 鄭叔清為京兆尹，數月榜死者八百餘人。 肅宗以新錢不便，命百官集議，不能改。 上元元年，減重輪錢以一當三十，開元舊錢與乾元十當錢，皆以一當十，碾磑鬻受，得為實錢，虛錢交易皆用十當錢，由是錢有虛實之名。
In Qianyuan 1, with the treasury empty, Coinage Commissioner Diwu Qi introduced the Qianyuan Heavy Treasure—one inch across, ten pounds per string—valued at ten Kaiyuan coins and nicknamed the "ten-for-one Qianyuan." Official mints had already been producing thin coin; people melted scrap coin and bronze Buddhas—"pántuó"—into counterfeits, a capital offense. As chancellor, Diwu Qi ordered Jiangzhou mints to produce double-rim Qianyuan Heavy Treasure coin—slightly larger, twelve pounds per string—worth fifty Kaiyuan coins apiece. Three coin types circulated side by side; the large double-rim pieces were known as "heavy-rim money." Constant currency reversals sent prices soaring—a peck of rice hit seven thousand cash, and corpses of the starved lined the streets. "Nominal money" appeared first; everyone in the capital counterfeited small coin by melting bells and statues, and prosecutions multiplied. As Jingzhao governor, Zheng Shuqing had more than eight hundred offenders beaten to death in just a few months. Suzong, acknowledging the new currency's failure, convened the court for reform—and achieved nothing. In Shangyuan 1, double-rim coin was cut to thirty-for-one, while old Kaiyuan and ten-count Qianyuan were pegged at ten-for-one; worn coin ground down could still pass as "real money," but nominal transactions ran on ten-count pieces—thus "real" and "nominal" coin entered the language.
29
史思明據東都，亦鑄「得一元寶」錢，徑一寸四分，以一當開元通寶之百。 既而惡「得一」非長祚之兆，改其文曰「順天元寶」。
Rebels under Shi Siming, holding Luoyang, minted the "De Yi Yuanbao"—large coins worth a hundred Kaiyuan each. Siming soon scrapped the name—the character for "obtain" seemed an ill omen for a lasting dynasty—and renamed the coin "Shuntian Yuanbao" (Obedient to Heaven).
30
代宗即位，乾元重寶錢以一當二，重輪錢以一當三，凡三日而大小錢皆以一當一。 自第五琦更鑄，犯法者日數百，州縣不能禁止，至是人甚便之。 其後民間乾元、重稜二錢鑄為器，不復出矣。
Upon Daizong's accession, Qianyuan was devalued to two-for-one and double-rim to three-for-one; within three days every denomination returned to face value. From Diwu Qi's reforms onward, hundreds broke the coin laws daily and local officials could not stop them—yet when denominations unified, the public welcomed the relief. Qianyuan and heavy-rim coin soon disappeared from circulation, melted down for household ware.
31
當時議者以為「自天寶至今，戶九百餘萬。 王制：上農夫食九人，中農夫七人。 以中農夫計之，為六千三百萬人。 少壯相均，人食米二升，日費米百二十六萬斛，歲費四萬五千三百六十萬斛，而衣倍之，吉凶之禮再倍，餘三年之儲以備水旱凶災，當米十三萬六千八十萬斛，以貴賤豐儉相當，則米之直與錢鈞也。 田以高下肥瘠豐耗為率，一頃出米五十餘斛，當田二千七百二十一萬六千頃。 而錢亦歲毀於棺瓶埋藏焚溺，其間銅貴錢賤，有鑄以為器者，不出十年錢幾盡，不足周當世之用」。 諸道鹽鐵轉運使劉晏以江、嶺諸州，任土所出，皆重粗賤弱之貨，輸京師不足以供道路之直。 於是積之江淮，易銅鉛薪炭，廣鑄錢，歲得十餘萬緡，輸京師及荊、揚二州，自是錢日增矣。
Policy critics observed: "From the Tianbao era to the present, registered households exceed nine million." As the Royal Regulations put it, a first-rate cultivator can support nine people and a middling one seven. Figuring by the middling cultivator's yield, that works out to sixty-three million people. Assuming a balanced mix of adults in their prime, at two sheng of rice per person per day, daily consumption would run to 1.26 million hu and yearly to 453.6 million hu. Double that for clothing, double it again for weddings and funerals, and set aside enough grain for three years against flood, drought, and famine—1.368 billion hu in all. When rank and wealth, abundance and want are properly aligned, the value of grain and cash should stand in equilibrium. Taking fields by their grade of elevation, soil, and output, and assuming roughly fifty hu of rice per qing, the empire would need some 27.2 million qing under the plow. Coin is also lost every year to coffins, urns, burial hoards, fire, and water. With copper dear and cash cheap, people melt coin to cast utensils; within a decade the currency will be all but gone, unable to sustain the economy of the day." Liu Yan, commissioner for salt-and-iron transport across the circuits, observed that the Jiang and Ling regions shipped only bulky, low-grade local goods to the capital—goods so worthless that freight alone consumed their value. He therefore accumulated those goods along the Yangzi and Huai, traded them for copper, lead, and fuel, and stepped up minting, producing more than a hundred thousand strings a year for the capital and for Jing and Yang. From that point the money supply steadily grew.
32
大曆七年，禁天下鑄銅器。 建中初，戶部侍郎韓洄以商州紅崖冶銅多，請復洛源廢監，起十鑪，歲鑄錢七萬二千緡，每千錢費九百。 德宗從之。
In Dali 7, a ban on casting copper utensils was imposed throughout the realm. Early in the Jianzhong era, Han Hui of the Ministry of Revenue, citing the rich copper deposits at Shangzhou's Hongya works, petitioned to reopen the defunct Luoyuan mint with ten furnaces, aiming to coin seventy-two thousand strings a year at a minting cost of nine hundred cash per thousand. Emperor Dezong approved the plan.
33
江淮多鉛錫錢，以銅盪外，不盈斤兩，帛價益貴。 銷千錢為銅六斤，鑄器則斤得錢六百，故銷鑄者多，而錢益耗。 判度支趙贊採連州白銅鑄大錢，一當十，以權輕重。 貞元初，駱谷、散關禁行人以一錢出者。 諸道鹽鐵使張滂奏禁江淮鑄銅為器，惟鑄鑑而已。 十年，詔天下鑄銅器，每器一斤，其直不得過百六十，銷錢者以盜鑄論。 然而民間錢益少，繒帛價輕，州縣禁錢不出境，商賈皆絕。 浙西觀察使李若初請通錢往來，而京師商賈齎錢四方貿易者，不可勝計。 詔復禁之。 二十年，命市井交易，以綾、羅、絹、布、雜貨與錢兼用。 憲宗以錢少復禁用銅器。
Lead-tin coins flooded the Jiang-Huai region—thin copper wash on the surface, barely a full ounce of metal in each—and the price of silk kept climbing. A thousand coins melted down gave six pounds of copper, but that same copper cast into ware could pass for six hundred coins' worth—so melting flourished and the currency bled away. Zhao Zan, who oversaw the budget, drew on Lianzhou's white copper to mint heavy coins worth ten standard cash apiece, hoping to stabilize the skew between light and full-weight currency. Early in the Zhenyuan era, travelers at the Luogu and San'guan passes were barred from carrying so much as one coin beyond the frontier. Zhang Pang, the salt-and-iron commissioner, urged a ban on copperware in the Jiang-Huai basin, allowing only the casting of mirrors. In year ten an edict regulated copperware empire-wide: no piece weighing one jin might sell for more than 160 cash, and anyone who melted coin to cast it would be treated as a counterfeiter. All the same, cash in circulation dwindled, silk and cloth lost value, local governments blocked coin from crossing their borders, and trade ground to a halt. Li Ruochu, commissioner of Zhexi, asked that coin be allowed to move freely again; meanwhile merchants in the capital were exporting cash in every direction in numbers too great to reckon. The throne ordered the restrictions reimposed. In year twenty the government required marketplace dealings to accept damask, gauze, silk, cloth, and other goods alongside cash. With the currency still too thin, Emperor Xianzong once more banned copper utensils.
34
時商賈至京師，委錢諸道進奏院及諸軍、諸使富家，以輕裝趨四方，合券乃取之，號「飛錢」。 京兆尹裴武請禁與商賈飛錢者，廋索諸坊，十人為保。
Merchants bound for the capital would leave their coin with circuit memorial bureaus or with the treasuries of armies and commissioners, travel unburdened across the empire, and collect payment only when their vouchers matched—a practice known as "flying cash." Pei Wu, governor of the capital district, urged a crackdown on flying-cash dealings with merchants, proposing ward-by-ward searches and mutual-responsibility groups of ten.
35
鹽鐵使李巽以郴州平陽銅坑二百八十餘，復置桂陽監，以兩鑪日鑄錢二十萬。 天下歲鑄錢十三萬五千緡。
Li Xun, commissioner of salt and iron, reopened the Guiyang mint to exploit more than 280 copper pits at Pingyang in Chenzhou, running two furnaces that turned out two hundred thousand coins a day. Annual mint output across the realm reached 135,000 strings of cash.
36
命商賈蓄錢者，皆出以市貨； 天下有銀之山必有銅，唯銀無益於人，五嶺以北，採銀一兩者流他州，官吏論罪。 元和四年，京師用錢緡少二十及有鉛錫錢者，捕之； 非交易而錢行衢路者，不問。 復詔采五嶺銀坑，禁錢出嶺。 六年，貿易錢十緡以上者，參用布帛。
Merchants sitting on stockpiled cash were commanded to put it back into circulation by buying goods; Wherever a mountain yielded silver it also held copper, but silver itself served no public need; north of the Five Ridges, even one liang of silver mined meant exile to another province, and complicit officials faced prosecution. In Yuanhe 4 the capital authorities seized anyone paying with short-weight strings—twenty cash light per thousand—or with debased lead-and-tin coin; People who simply carried cash on the street without conducting a transaction were left alone. The court also revived work in the Five Ridges' silver pits and barred cash from being taken beyond the mountains. In year six, any transaction of ten strings or more had to include payment in cloth and silk as well as cash.
37
蔚州三河冶距飛狐故監二十里而近，河東節度使王鍔置鑪，疏拒馬河水鑄錢，工費尤省，以刺史李聽為使，以五鑪鑄，每鑪月鑄錢三十萬，自是河東錫錢皆廢。
At Weizhou the Sanhe foundry stood barely twenty li from the former Feihu mint. Wang E, governor of Hedong, set up furnaces there, routed the Juma River to power them, and cut minting costs sharply. He put Prefect Li Ting in charge of five furnaces, each producing three hundred thousand coins a month—and from that point every debased tin coin in Hedong was withdrawn.
38
自京師禁飛錢，家有滯藏，物價寖輕。 判度支盧坦、兵部尚書判戶部事王紹、鹽鐵使王播請許商人於戶部、度支、鹽鐵三司飛錢，每千錢增給百錢，然商人無至者。 復許與商人敵貫而易之，然錢重帛輕如故。 憲宗為之出內庫錢五十萬緡市布帛，每匹加舊估十之一。
Once the capital banned flying money, cash piled up in private hoards and prices slowly sank. Revenue Commissioner Lu Tan, Minister of War Wang Shao in his concurrent post over the Ministry of Revenue, and Salt and Iron Commissioner Wang Bo petitioned to let merchants remit flying money through those three fiscal offices, offering a bonus of one hundred cash on every thousand—yet no merchant would use the scheme. They again allowed merchants to swap coin at full nominal strings, but cash stayed scarce and cloth cheap as ever. Emperor Xianzong drew five hundred thousand strings from the palace treasury to buy up cloth and silk, paying one-tenth above the old official price per bolt.
39
會吳元濟、王承宗連衡拒命，以七道兵討之，經費屈竭。 皇甫鎛建議，內外用錢每緡墊二十外，復抽五十送度支以贍軍。 十二年，復給京兆府錢五十萬緡市布帛，而富家錢過五千貫者死，王公重貶，沒入於官，以五之一賞告者。 京師區肆所積，皆方鎮錢，少亦五十萬緡，乃爭市第宅。 然富賈倚左右神策軍官錢為名，府縣不敢劾問。 民間墊陌有至七十者，鉛錫錢益多，吏捕犯者，多屬諸軍、諸使，謼集市人彊奪，毆傷吏卒。 京兆尹崔元略請犯者本軍、本使涖決，帝不能用，詔送本軍、本使，而京兆府遣人涖決。 穆宗即位，京師鬻金銀十兩亦墊一兩，糴米鹽百錢墊七八。 京兆尹柳公綽以嚴法禁止之。 尋以所在用錢墊陌不一，詔從俗所宜，內外給用，每緡墊八十。
Then Wu Yuanji and Wang Chengzong joined forces in open rebellion, and as seven military circuits marched against them, war funds ran dry. Huangfu Bo proposed that on top of the usual twenty-cash short count per string, another fifty be withheld from every string spent inside or outside the capital and forwarded to the Revenue Bureau to feed the armies. In the twelfth year the court again gave the capital prefecture five hundred thousand strings to buy cloth and silk, while families hoarding more than five thousand strings were sentenced to death, princes and nobles were harshly penalized, their wealth was seized, and informants received a fifth of the confiscated sum. Cash piled up in the capital's shops was almost all money from the regional commands; even the smallest hoards ran to five hundred thousand strings, and merchants competed to buy grand houses. Wealthy traders shielded their hoards by claiming them as official funds of the Left and Right Divine Strategy Armies, and local officials did not dare probe them. Short counts in the marketplace ran as high as seventy cash per hundred, debased lead-and-tin coin proliferated, and when officers arrested violators—often men attached to armies or commissioner offices—their allies rallied market crowds to snatch the prisoners back and assault the arresting clerks and troops. Metropolitan Prefect Cui Yuanlue asked that offenders be returned to their own army or commissioner for trial, but the emperor would not agree; he ordered them sent back to those units while still requiring capital officials to be present at the hearing. When Muzong came to the throne, even gold and silver trades in the capital shaved one tael off every ten, and a nominal hundred-cash purchase of rice or salt paid out only ninety-two or ninety-three. Metropolitan Prefect Liu Gongchuo cracked down on the practice with harsh penalties. Before long, with short counts varying from region to region, the court ruled that official payments at home and abroad should follow local practice—eighty cash short on every string.
40
寶曆初，河南尹王起請銷錢為佛像者以盜鑄錢論。 大和三年，詔佛像以鉛、錫、土、木為之，飾帶以金銀、鍮石、烏油、藍鐵，唯鑑、磬、釘、鐶、鈕得用銅，餘皆禁之，盜鑄者死。 是時峻鉛錫錢之禁，告千錢者賞以五千。
Early in the Baoli reign, Henan Prefect Wang Qi petitioned that anyone who melted coin into Buddha images be punished as an illicit minter. In Dahe 3 an edict required Buddha images to be cast from lead, tin, clay, or wood, with gold, silver, brass, black iron, or blue iron allowed only for ornament; copper was permitted solely for mirrors, chime-stones, nails, rings, and buttons. Everything else was banned, and illicit casting carried the death penalty. The ban on debased lead-and-tin coin was enforced with special rigor, and informants who turned in a thousand cash of it were paid five thousand.
41
四年，詔積錢以七千緡為率，十萬緡者期以一年出之，二十萬以二年。 凡交易百緡以上者，匹帛米粟居半。 河南府、揚州、江陵府以都會之劇，約束如京師。 未幾皆罷。
In the fourth year the court fixed seven thousand strings as the hoarding threshold: anyone sitting on one hundred thousand had to spend it within a year, and anyone with two hundred thousand within two. Any deal above one hundred strings had to be settled half in coin and half in cloth, rice, or grain. Because Henan, Yangzhou, and Jiangling were major commercial hubs, they were subjected to the same coin controls as the capital. The measures were soon dropped altogether.
42
八年，河東錫錢復起，鹽鐵使王涯置飛狐鑄錢院於蔚州，天下歲鑄錢不及十萬緡。 文宗病幣輕錢重，詔方鎮縱錢穀交易。 時雖禁銅為器，而江淮、嶺南列肆鬻之，鑄千錢為器，售利數倍。 宰相李珏請加鑪鑄錢，於是禁銅器，官一切為市之。 天下銅坑五十，歲采銅二十六萬六千斤。
In the eighth year debased pewter coin reappeared in Hedong; Salt and Iron Commissioner Wang Ya opened a mint at Flying Fox in Weizhou, yet annual output empire-wide did not reach one hundred thousand strings. Distressed that goods were cheap while coin remained scarce, Emperor Wenzong ordered the regional commands to loosen restrictions on cash-and-grain trade. Copper vessels were officially banned, yet shops in the Jiang-Huai and Lingnan regions sold them openly; melting a thousand cash into ware could fetch several times the metal's face value. Chief Minister Li Jue urged more mint furnaces; the court then outlawed copper ware and bought up every piece on the market. Fifty copper mines operated across the empire, producing two hundred sixty-six thousand jin of copper each year.
43
及武宗廢浮屠法，永平監官李郁彥請以銅像、鍾、磬、鑪、鐸皆歸巡院，州縣銅益多矣。 鹽鐵使以工有常力，不足以加鑄，許諸道觀察使皆得置錢坊。 淮南節度使李紳請天下以州名鑄錢，京師為京錢，大小徑寸，如開元通寶，交易禁用舊錢。 會宣宗即位，盡黜會昌之政，新錢以字可辨，復鑄為像。
After Wuzong suppressed Buddhism, Yongping Directorate officer Li Yuyan proposed that copper statues, bells, chime-stones, censers, and clappers be surrendered to the inspection commission, and copper supplies in the provinces swelled. The Salt and Iron Commissioner, finding fixed mint labor too limited for increased output, allowed every regional observation commissioner to open coin workshops. Huainan governor Li Shen proposed minting coins stamped with each prefecture's name—Capital Coin for the capital—sized one inch across like Kaiyuan tongbao, and outlawing older coin in commerce. When Xuanzong came to the throne he rolled back the Huichang reforms; the distinctive new coins were identified by their inscriptions and melted back into Buddhist images.
44
昭宗末，京師用錢八百五十為貫，每百纔八十五，河南府以八十為百云。
In the last years of Zhaozong's reign the capital counted only eight hundred fifty coins as a full string and paid out eighty-five per nominal hundred; Henan Prefecture, by report, treated eighty as a hundred.

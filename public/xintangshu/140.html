3
崔圓，字有裕，貝州武城人，後魏尚書左仆射亮八世孫。 少孤貧，志向卓邁，喜學兵家。 開元中，詔舉遺逸，以鈐謀對策甲科，歷京兆府參軍，尹蕭炅薦之，遷會昌丞。 楊國忠遙領劍南節度，引圓為左司馬，知留後。 玄宗西出，次撫風，遷御史中丞、劍南節度副大使。 圓銳功名，初聞難，刺國忠意，乃治城浚隍，列館宇，儲什具。 帝次河池，圓疏具陳「蜀土腴穀羨，儲供易辦」。 帝省書泣下曰：「世亂識忠臣。 」即日拜中書侍郎、同中書門下平章事，仍兼劍南節度使。 天子至，朝廷百司殿宇帷幔皆具，益嗟賞之。 肅宗立，命與房琯、韋見素赴行在所，帝為製遺愛碑於蜀以寵之。
Cui Yuan, courtesy name Youyu, came from Wucheng in Beizhou and was the eighth-generation descendant of Liang, who had served as Left Vice Director of the Imperial Secretariat under the Northern Wei. Orphaned and poor in his youth, he harbored lofty ambitions and took pleasure in studying the art of war. During the Kaiyuan reign an edict called up recluses of outstanding talent; Yuan passed the military-strategy examination in the top grade, served as an aide in the Jingzhao metropolitan prefecture, was recommended by the prefect Xiao Jiong, and was promoted to assistant magistrate of Huichang. When Yang Guozhong held the Jiannan commission in name only, he brought Yuan in as left major and put him in charge of the rear administration. When Emperor Xuanzong fled west and paused at Fufeng, Yuan was promoted to censor-in-chief and deputy commissioner of the Jiannan military commission. Ambitious for fame and advancement, Yuan took the first news of disaster as a cue to Yang Guozhong's intentions; he fortified the city, deepened the moats, set up lodging, and laid in stores of every kind. When the emperor stopped at Hechi, Yuan submitted a detailed memorial arguing that Shu was fertile and grain-rich, and that supplies there would be easy to arrange. The emperor read the memorial and wept, saying, "In times of chaos one learns who the loyal ministers are. " That same day Yuan was appointed vice director of the Secretariat and associate director of the Chancellery, while continuing to hold the Jiannan military commission. When the emperor arrived, the halls of government and their furnishings were all ready, and his admiration for Yuan only grew. After Emperor Suzong took the throne, Yuan was ordered to join Fang Guan and Wei Jiansu at the mobile court; the former emperor had a stele of enduring affection set up in Shu to honor him.
4
至德二載，遷中書令，封趙國公，實封戶五百。 乾元元年，罷為太子少師，留守東都。 於是上皇所置宰相無在者。 王師之敗相州也，軍所過，皆縱剽，圓懼，委東都，奔襄陽，詔削階、封。 尋召拜濟王傅。 李光弼表為懷州刺史，改汾州，以治行稱。 徙淮南節度使，在鎮六年，請朝京師，吏民乞留，詔檢校尚書右仆射，還之。 久乃檢校左仆射，入知省事。 大歷中卒，年六十四，贈太子太師，謚曰昭襄。
In the second year of the Zhide era he was promoted to director of the Secretariat, enfeoffed as Duke of Zhao with a substantive fief of five hundred households. In the first year of Qianyuan he was dismissed to the post of junior tutor to the heir apparent and left to guard the Eastern Capital. By then none of the chancellors the retired emperor had appointed still held office. After the imperial army's defeat at Xiangzhou, the troops looted wherever they marched; fearing for himself, Yuan abandoned the Eastern Capital and fled to Xiangyang, and an edict stripped him of rank and fief. He was soon recalled and appointed tutor to the Prince of Ji. Li Guangbi recommended him for prefect of Huaizhou; he was later moved to Fenzhou, where his administration won renown. He was transferred to military commissioner of Huainan; after six years in the region he asked to attend court at the capital, but officials and commoners pleaded that he stay, so an edict named him acting right vice director of the Imperial Secretariat and sent him back. Only after some time was he made acting left vice director and brought in to handle Secretariat business. He died during the Dali era at the age of sixty-four and was posthumously honored as grand tutor to the heir apparent with the temple name Zhaoxiang.

苗晉卿
Miao Jinqing.
6
苗晉卿，字元輔，潞州壺關人，世以儒素稱。 擢進士第，調為修武尉，累進吏部郎中、中書舍人，知吏部選事。 選人訴索好官，厲言倨色紛於前，晉卿與相對，終日無慍顏。 久之，進侍郎，積寬縱，而吏下因緣作奸。 方時承平，選常萬人，李林甫為尚書，專國政，以銓事委晉卿及宋遙，然歲命它官同較書判，核才實。 天寶二年，判入等者凡六十四人，分甲、乙、丙三科，以張奭為第一。 奭，御史中丞倚之子，倚新得幸於帝，晉卿欲附之，奭本無學，故議者囂然不平。 安祿山因間言之，帝為禦花萼樓覆實，中裁十一二，奭持紙終日，筆不下，人謂之「曳白」。 帝大怒，貶倚淮陽太守，遙武當太守，晉卿安康太守。 明年，徙魏郡，即充河北采訪使。 居三年，政化大行。 嘗入計，謁歸壺關，望縣門輒步，吏諫止，晉卿以「公門當下，況父母邦乎」？ 郡太守迎犒，使所屬令行酒，酒至，必立飲白釂，侍老有獻，降西階拜而飲，時美其恭。 改河東郡，兼河東采訪使。 徙撫風郡，封高平縣男。 遷工部尚書、東都留守。 召為憲部，兼左丞。 安祿山反，竇廷芝棄陜郡不守，楊國忠本忌其有望，即奏「東道賊沖，非大臣不可鎮遏」，授陜郡太守、陜虢防禦使，晉卿見帝，以老辭，忤旨，聽致仕於家。 車駕入蜀，搢紳多陷賊，晉卿間道走金州。
Miao Jinqing, courtesy name Yuanfu, was from Huguan in Luzhou; his family had long been known for scholarly simplicity. After taking the jinshi degree he was appointed magistrate of Xiuwu and rose through the Ministry of Personnel to drafting secretary, with responsibility for civil-service selection. Candidates clamored for desirable posts, heaping harsh words and arrogant demands upon him, yet Jinqing faced them from morning to night without showing irritation. In time he was promoted to vice director; his indulgence deepened, and the clerks beneath him seized the chance for corruption. In those peaceful years selections often involved ten thousand candidates; Li Linfu, as director of the ministry, controlled the government and left the evaluations to Jinqing and Song Yao, but each year other officials were also assigned to review the papers and test candidates' real ability. In the second year of Tianbao sixty-four candidates were judged to have passed, ranked in three grades; Zhang Shi was placed first. Shi was the son of Censor-in-Chief Yi, who had lately won the emperor's favor; Jinqing sought to curry favor with the father, but Shi had no learning to speak of, and public opinion seethed with outrage. An Lushan brought the matter to the emperor's attention; the emperor went to the Hua'e Tower to verify the results in person; only one or two in ten of those re-tested passed; Shi held his examination paper all day without writing a word, and people dubbed it "dragging a blank sheet." The emperor was furious: Yi was demoted to prefect of Huaiyang, Yao to prefect of Wudang, and Jinqing to prefect of Ankang. The following year he was moved to Weijun and appointed investigating commissioner for Hebei. After three years in office his reforms had taken deep hold. Once, coming to the capital on official business, he returned to pay respects in Huguan; at sight of the county gate he would dismount and walk, and when clerks tried to stop him he said, "One should walk before the public gate—how much more in one's native district?" The prefect welcomed him with a feast and had the local magistrate pass the wine; whenever a cup reached him he stood and drained it in one; when elders offered wine he went down the west steps, bowed, and drank; people at the time admired his deference. He was transferred to Hedong commandery and concurrently made investigating commissioner for Hedong. He was moved to Fufeng commandery and enfeoffed as Baron of Gaoping. He was promoted to minister of works and defender of the Eastern Capital. He was recalled to the Ministry of Justice and concurrently made left assistant director. When An Lushan rebelled, Dou Tingzhi abandoned Shaan commandery without a fight; Yang Guozhong, who had long envied Jinqing's standing, immediately memorialized that the eastern route was overrun and only a senior minister could contain the rebels, and appointed him prefect of Shaan and defense commissioner of Shan-Guo; Jinqing appeared before the emperor, pleaded old age, offended the throne, and was allowed to retire at home. When the court fled into Shu, many officials were trapped by the rebels; Jinqing escaped by back roads to Jinzhou.
7
晉卿寬厚，所至以惠化稱。 魏人為營生祠，立石頌美。 再秉政，出入七年，小心謹畏，不甚斥是非得失，故能安保寵名。 然練達事體，百官簿最，一省無遺，議者比漢胡廣。 肅宗欲以李輔國為常侍，奏曰：「常侍近密，非賢不可居，豈宜任等輩？ 」罷之。 朝廷欲論陳希烈等死，晉卿曰：「陛下得張通儒、安守忠、孫孝哲等，何以加罪？ 」帝不從。 俄而史思明亂，持是以誘眾。 嘗自為父碑文，有鵲巢碑上。 賊入上黨，焚蕩略盡，而苗氏松槚獨無傷。 大歷七年，配享肅宗廟廷。
Jinqing was generous and mild; wherever he served he was known for benevolent rule. The people of Wei built him a living shrine and set up a stone inscription in his praise. He held high office twice over seven years in all; careful and restrained, he rarely spoke out on rights and wrongs, and thus preserved both favor and reputation. Yet he was thoroughly versed in administrative detail; the records of every office lay open to him at a glance, and commentators compared him to Hu Guang of the Han. When Emperor Suzong wished to make Li Fuguo a palace attendant, Jinqing memorialized, "That post is close to the throne and demands a man of talent—how can someone of his sort hold it?" " The appointment was dropped. When the court debated executing Chen Xilie and others, Jinqing said, "Your Majesty already holds Zhang Tongru, An Shouzhong, Sun Xiaozhe, and the like—how can you pile on more punishments?" " The emperor would not listen. Soon afterward Shi Siming rebelled and used this very argument to win men to his side. He once wrote an inscription for his father's tomb, and a magpie nested on the stone. When rebels entered Shangdang they burned and ravaged almost everything, yet the pines at the Miao family graves alone were untouched. In the seventh year of Dali he was granted a place in the sacrifices at Emperor Suzong's temple.

十子：發、丕、堅、粲、垂、向、呂、稷、望、咸。
He had ten sons: Fa, Pi, Jian, Can, Chui, Xiang, Lü, Ji, Wang, and Xian.
9
粲，德宗時官至郎中，陸贄欲進粲官，帝不許，曰：「晉卿往攝政，有不臣之言。 又名其子，皆與帝王同，粲等宜與外官。 」贄奏：「王者爵人必於朝，刑人必於市，言與眾共之。 獎而不言其善，斯謂曲貸； 罰而不書其惡，斯謂中傷。 曲貸，則授受不明，而私幸之門啟； 中傷，則枉直無辨，而讒間之道行。 可不慎哉！ 若陛下以晉卿奸邪，粲等應坐，則當公議其罪； 若知見誣，亦宜擢粲等以示天下。 且晉卿起文儒，致位臺輔，謙柔敦厚，為三朝所推，安肯為族滅計？ 雖甚狂險猶不為之，況老臣乎？ 」帝然之，而粲官終不顯。
Can rose to director under Emperor Dezong; Lu Zhi wished to promote him, but the emperor refused, saying, "When Jinqing once held regency he spoke words unbecoming a subject. He even gave his sons names that echoed imperial usage; Can and his brothers should receive only peripheral appointments." Zhi replied in a memorial, "A true king ennobles men only in court and punishes them only in the marketplace, because he shares such decisions with the people. To reward without stating the merit is crooked favor; to punish without recording the fault is slander by omission. Crooked favor blurs who gives and who receives and opens the door to private patronage; slander by omission leaves right and wrong undistinguished and lets calumny flourish. Can Your Majesty not be cautious in this? If Your Majesty believes Jinqing was treacherous and that Can and his brothers should share the guilt, then their crimes ought to be debated openly; if you know they were slandered, you should promote Can and his brothers to show the empire where you stand. Besides, Jinqing rose from the world of letters to the highest office; humble, gentle, and sincere, he was trusted through three reigns—why would he ever plot the destruction of his own house? Even the wildest and most reckless man would not do such a thing—how much less a veteran minister? " The emperor assented, yet Can never attained high rank.
10
裴冕，字章甫，河中河東人，本冠族仕家，以蔭再調渭南尉。 王鉷為京畿采訪使，表署判官，歷殿中侍御史。 冕少學術，然明銳，果於事，眾呈稱職，鉷雅任之。 及鉷得罪，有詔廷辨，冕位甚下，而抗言其誣。 鉷死，李林甫方用事，僚屬懼，皆引去，獨冕為斂葬，由是浸知名。 河西節度使哥舒翰辟行軍司馬。
Pei Mian, courtesy name Zhangfu, was from Hedong in Hezhong; his family was of distinguished official lineage, and through yin privilege he was twice appointed magistrate of Weinan. When Wang Hong was investigating commissioner for the capital region he had Mian appointed as his aide; Mian later served as palace attendant censor. Mian had studied the classics in his youth, but he was sharp and decisive in action; everyone said he did his job well, and Hong relied on him heavily. When Hong was accused, an edict ordered a court debate; though Mian's rank was low, he spoke up boldly that Hong had been framed. After Hong died, with Li Linfu in power his former subordinates fled in fear; Mian alone arranged the burial, and from that time his name slowly spread. Geshu Han, military commissioner of Hexi, recruited him as marching major.
11
玄宗入蜀，詔皇太子為天下兵馬元帥，拜冕御史中丞兼左庶子副之。 初，冕在河西，方召還，而道遇太子平涼，遂從至靈武，與杜鴻漸、崔漪同辭進曰：「主上厭於勤，且南狩蜀，宗社神器，要須有歸。 今天意人事，屬在殿下，宜正位號。 有如逡巡，失億兆心，則大事去矣。 」太子曰：「我平寇逆，奉迎乘輿還京師，退居涼貳，以侍膳左右，豈不樂哉！ 公等何言之過！ 」對曰：「殿下居東宮二十年，今多難啟聖，以安社稷，而所從將士皆關輔人，日夜思歸，大眾一騷，不可復集，不如因而撫之，以就大功。 臣等昧死請。 」太子固讓，凡五請，卒見聽。 太子即位，進冕中書侍郎、同中書門下平章事。 乃建言賣官、度僧道士，收貲濟軍興。 時取償既賤，眾不為宜。
When Emperor Xuanzong fled into Shu, an edict named the crown prince commander-in-chief of all armies and appointed Mian censor-in-chief and left subordinate of the heir to assist him. Mian had been in Hexi and was on his way back when he met the crown prince at Pingliang; he followed him to Lingwu and, with Du Hongjian and Cui Yi, jointly urged, "The emperor is weary of rule and has gone south into Shu; the altars of state and the imperial regalia must find a home. Heaven's intent and the people's will now rest with Your Highness; you should take the throne." If you hesitate and lose the hearts of the people, the great cause will be lost. The crown prince said, "I mean to crush the rebels, welcome the emperor back to the capital, and withdraw to a secondary role, attending him at table—what could be happier than that! Why do you speak so extravagantly!" They answered, "Your Highness has been heir for twenty years; now in this time of calamity you are called to save the realm. Yet the soldiers who follow you are all men of the capital region, longing day and night to go home; once the army is unsettled it cannot be rallied again. Better to win them over now and achieve the great work. We beg this at the risk of our lives." The crown prince refused firmly; they pleaded five times before he at last consented. When the crown prince took the throne, Mian was promoted to vice director of the Secretariat and associate director of the Chancellery. He then proposed selling offices and ordaining monks and Daoist priests, using the proceeds to fund the armies. The fees demanded were so low that many thought the policy improper.
12
肅宗至鳳翔，罷冕政事，拜尚書右僕射。 兩京平，封冀國公，實封五百戶，出為劍南西川節度使。 復為右僕射，待制集賢院。 俄充山陵使。 於是，中書舍人劉烜為李輔國所昵，冕表為判官。 烜抵法，坐降施州刺史，徙澧州。
When Emperor Suzong reached Fengxiang, Mian was removed from active government and made right vice director of the Imperial Secretariat. After the two capitals were recovered he was enfeoffed as Duke of Ji with a substantive fief of five hundred households and sent out as military commissioner of western Sichuan. He was again made right vice director and attendant drafting at the Hall of Assembled Worthies. Soon he was appointed commissioner for the imperial tombs. At that time the drafting secretary Liu Xuan was a favorite of Li Fuguo; Mian recommended him as his administrative aide. When Xuan ran afoul of the law, Mian was demoted to prefect of Shi and then moved to Lizhou.
13
大歷中，郭子儀言於代宗曰：「冕首佐先帝，馳驅靈武，有社稷勛，程元振忌其賢，遂加誣構，海內冤之。 陛下宜還冕於朝，復俾輔相，必能致治成化。 」時元載秉政，冕早所甄引，載德之，又貪其衰瘵，且下己，遂拜左僕射、同中書門下平章事。 入見，拜不能興，載自扶之，代為贊謝。 俄兼河南江淮副元帥、東都留守。 不逾月卒，有詔贈太尉。
During the Dali era Guo Ziyi told Emperor Daizong, "Mian was among the first to support the late emperor, riding hard to Lingwu; he earned merit for the realm. Cheng Yuanzhen envied his talent and framed him, and the whole empire regarded it as an injustice." Your Majesty should bring Mian back to court and restore him as chief minister; he will surely help restore good government. Yuan Zai then held power; Mian had long ago been the man who advanced him, and Zai was grateful, but he also counted on Mian's age and infirmity and on being his superior—so Mian was appointed left vice director and associate director of the Chancellery. When he came to audience he could not rise to bow; Zai himself helped him up and answered the emperor's thanks on his behalf. Soon he was also made deputy commander-in-chief for Henan and the Jiang-Huai region and defender of the Eastern Capital. Within a month he died; an edict posthumously honored him as grand marshal.
14
冕以忠勤自將，然不知宰相大體。 性豪侈，既素貴，輿服食飲皆光麗珍豐，櫪馬直數百金者常十數，每廣會賓客，不能名其饌，自製巾子工甚，人爭效之，號「僕射巾」。 領使既眾，吏白俸簿月二千緡，冕顧視，喜見顏間，世訾其嗜利云。 始，肅宗廟惟苗晉卿配享，冕卒後二十餘年，有蘇正元者奏言：「肅宗為元帥時，師才一旅，冕於草創中，甄大義以勸進，收募驍勇幾十餘萬。 既逾月，房琯來； 又一年，而晉卿至。 今晉卿從祀，而冕乃不與。 」有詔冕配享肅宗廟。
Mian led with loyalty and diligence, yet he did not grasp the larger duties of a chief minister. By nature he was lavish; born to high rank, his carriages, dress, food, and drink were all splendid and costly; he routinely kept a dozen horses in his stable worth several hundred strings of cash each; at his grand banquets he could not even name every dish served; the headcloth he designed was so finely made that people rushed to copy it, and it was nicknamed the "Vice Director's cap." He held so many commissions that a clerk reported his monthly salary register at two thousand strings of cash; Mian looked it over with visible pleasure, and later generations condemned his greed. At first only Miao Jinqing shared sacrifice in Emperor Suzong's temple; more than twenty years after Mian's death a man named Su Zhengyuan memorialized, "When Suzong was commander-in-chief his forces numbered barely a brigade; Mian, in those founding days, grasped the larger cause and urged him to take the throne, raising nearly a hundred thousand fierce troops. More than a month passed before Fang Guan arrived; and another year passed before Jinqing came. Jinqing now shares the sacrifices, yet Mian does not. " An edict then granted Mian a place in the sacrifices at Emperor Suzong's temple.

裴遵慶
Pei Zunqing.
16
裴遵慶，字少良，絳州聞喜人。 幼強學，該綜圖傳，外晦內明，不幹當世。 年既長，始以仕家推蔭為興寧陵丞，調大理丞。 邊將蕭克濟督役苛暴，役者有醜言，有司以大逆論，遵慶曰：「財不足聚人，力不足加眾，焉能反？ 」由是全救數十族。 頻擢吏部員外郎，判南曹。 天寶時，選者歲萬計，遵慶性強敏，視簿牒，詳而不苛，世稱吏事第一。 肅宗時，為吏部侍郎。 蕭華輔政，屢薦之，拜黃門侍郎、同中書門下平章事。 代宗初，仆固懷恩反，帝以遵慶忠厚大臣，故奉詔宣慰，懷恩聽命將入朝，既而為其將范志誠沮止。 時帝在陜，遵慶脫身赴行在。 帝還，遷太子少傅。 罷為集賢院待制，改吏部尚書，以尚書右僕射復知選事，朝廷優其老，聽就第註官，時以為榮。
Pei Zunqing, courtesy name Shaoliang, was from Wenxi in Jiangzhou. As a boy he studied hard and mastered historical and geographical texts; outwardly unassuming but inwardly sharp, he kept aloof from worldly entanglements. Only in maturity did he enter office through family privilege as assistant at the Xingning imperial tomb, then was transferred to assistant in the Court of Judicial Review. The frontier general Xiao Keji drove corvée labor with brutal severity; when the laborers spoke rebelliously the authorities charged them with treason; Zunqing said, "Without wealth you cannot rally men, without power you cannot overcome the multitude—how could they rebel?" " By this argument he saved dozens of families from execution. He rose repeatedly to outer director in the Ministry of Personnel with charge of the Southern Bureau. During the Tianbao era annual selections ran to ten thousand candidates; Zunqing was forceful and quick-witted, thorough but not harsh in reviewing records, and his contemporaries ranked him first in administrative skill. Under Emperor Suzong he served as vice director of the Ministry of Personnel. When Xiao Hua held power he recommended Zunqing repeatedly; Zunqing was appointed vice director of the Palace Secretariat and associate director of the Chancellery. Early in Emperor Daizong's reign Pugu Huai'en rebelled; the emperor sent the loyal and seasoned Zunqing on an edict of reassurance; Huai'en agreed to come to court but was then blocked by his general Fan Zhicheng. The emperor was then at Shan; Zunqing broke free and made his way to the mobile court. After the emperor returned, Zunqing was made junior tutor to the heir apparent. He was dismissed to attendant drafting at the Hall of Assembled Worthies, then made minister of personnel; as right vice director of the Imperial Secretariat he again oversaw civil-service selection; the court indulged his age and allowed him to register appointments from home—a mark of honor at the time.

Once a kinsman by marriage, deranged by illness, accused him of plotting rebellion; the emperor saw through the charge and ignored it. Sincere and upright by nature, he grew only more careful in old age. Whenever he recommended a worthy man, he felt ashamed if the man came to thank him. When the emperor accepted his remonstrance, he grew only more inwardly wary. Even his intimates knew only how many draft memorials he revised, not what they contained. He died in the tenth year of Dali at more than ninety years of age. Early in his career as a court gentleman he wrote Records of Royal Government, surveying the principles of government ancient and modern; discerning readers saw in it the makings of a chief minister. His son was Xiang.

Xiang, courtesy name Suren, entered office through family privilege. Early in the Jianzhong era Li Shu, as prefect of Tongzhou, had him appointed administrative aide. When Li Huai'guang rebelled in Hezhong he sent his general Zhao Guixian to build fortifications at Tongzhou; Shu fled to Fengtian while Xiang remained to run the prefecture. Guixian forced officials to drive the labor gangs; when they missed the deadline he was about to execute someone as an example and the people fled in panic; Xiang alone went to Guixian's camp and talked him down, and Guixian surrendered. Tongzhou did not fall—and that was Xiang's doing. He served successively as magistrate of Liyang and Weinan, ranked first in every performance review, and was promoted to outer director in the Ministry of Revenue. Late in Dezong's reign deputies for the regional commands were often chosen at court and held in reserve until needed; Xiang was thus selected as junior administrator of Taiyuan and marching major, later served as investigating commissioner of Shan-Guo, and retired as minister of personnel. Xiang upheld his house through learning and moral conduct; more than a hundred kin inside and outside the family shared his income equally, and his age praised his filial harmony. He died at eighty and was posthumously honored as junior guardian to the heir apparent. His son Yin rose to censor-in-chief. Yin's son was Shu.

Shu, courtesy name Jisheng, passed the jinshi examination during the Xiantong reign. When Du Shenquan governed Hezhong he had Shu join his staff; Shu was twice promoted to magistrate of Lantian. The chancellor Wang Duo took notice of him and had him posted to the Hongwen Hall. After Duo left office, Shu went long without a new appointment. He followed Emperor Xizong into Shu and was promoted to palace attendant censor. Early in the Zhonghe era, when Duo was commander-in-chief, he appointed Shu secretary in charge of documents for Zheng-Hua. At the beginning of the Longji era he was promoted to drafting attendant and made prefect of Jingzhao. He was close to Kong Wei; when Wei was demoted for a crime, Shu was shifted to right subordinate of the heir and sent out as prefect of Shezhou. He was promoted to right regular attendant of the imperial stud and made proclaiming and consoling commissioner for Bianzhou.

Shu had long been allied with Zhu Quanzhong, who therefore obeyed the court and kept tribute flowing without interruption. Emperor Zhaozong was pleased and promoted him to vice minister of war. Cui Yin then also relied on Quanzhong to dominate the court, and he and Shu were on good terms. Soon he was made vice minister of revenue and associate director of the Chancellery. When the emperor was at Fengxiang, Yin was demoted and Shu was dismissed to the post of minister of works. After the emperor returned to the palace, Shu was appointed acting right vice director of the Imperial Secretariat and associate director of the Chancellery. He was sent out as military commissioner of Qinghai. Quanzhong argued that Shu had the talent to govern the age and should not be cast out to the provinces; he was recalled as vice director of the Chancellery and associate director, with charge of compiling the national history. He rose in turn to right vice director and commissioner for salt and iron transport in all circuits. When Emperor Aidi succeeded, Liu Can was in power; Quanzhong wanted his officer Zhang Tingfan made director of the Imperial Ancestral Temple; Shu argued that a meritorious general belonged in the regions, not in such a court post, and fearing this was not the emperor's wish, he withheld approval. Quanzhong angrily told his staff, "I always thought Shu was no lightweight—now look at him." " When Can heard, he immediately stripped Shu of his political duties and named him left vice director. Soon he was demoted to prefect of Dengzhou, then again to revenue aide of Longzhou. At Huazhou, Quanzhong had him killed at Baima Post Station and his body thrown into the river; he was sixty-five. Earlier Quanzhong's aide Li Zhen had said, "These men call themselves the pure stream—they ought to be thrown into the Yellow River and become the muddy stream forever." " Quanzhong laughed and agreed.

Lü Yin was from Hedong in Hezhong. In youth he studied hard; his aims and conduct were disciplined and upright. Orphaned and poor, he could not support himself; a wealthy local man of the Cheng clan gave him his daughter in marriage, believing Yin's talent would not long go unrewarded, and generously supplied his needs, so Yin's reputation steadily grew. Late in the Kaiyuan reign he went to the capital, passed the jinshi examination, was appointed magistrate of Ningling, and was made branch commissioner by the investigating commissioner Wei Zhi. When Geshu Han was military commissioner of Hexi he had Yin appointed administrative aide for expenditures. He served as attendant master of ceremonies to the heir apparent. Quiet and cautious by nature, he was diligent in administrative work; while colleagues went out on pleasure outings Yin alone bent over his desk auditing the records, and Han grew all the closer to him. He also served cumulatively as palace attendant censor. After Han's defeat at Tong Pass, Yin hurried west to Lingwu; recommended by the eunuch Wei, Emperor Suzong recognized his ability and made him censor-in-chief; everything he proposed was accepted. He followed the court to Fengxiang and was made vice minister of war.

帝復兩京，詔盡系群臣之汙賊者，以御史中丞崔器、憲部侍郎韓擇木、大理卿嚴向為三司使處其罪，又詔御史大夫李峴及諲領使。 諲於權宜知大體不及峴，而援律傅經過之，當時憚其持法，然以峴故，多所平反。
When the two capitals were recovered, an edict ordered all officials who had served the rebels imprisoned; Censor-in-Chief Cui Qi, Vice Minister of Justice Han Zemu, and chief justice Yan Xiang were made commissioners of the Three Offices to judge their crimes; another edict named Censor-in-Chief Li Xian and Yin to lead the commission. Yin was less adept than Xian at flexible policy and the larger view, but he applied the law and the classics with excessive rigor; contemporaries feared his severity, yet thanks to Xian many verdicts were overturned.

In the second year of Qianyuan the armies of the nine commissioners were defeated, and the emperor was deeply troubled. Yin was promoted to associate director of the Chancellery with charge of the Chancellery office; the next day Li Xian, Li Kui, and Diwu Qi were again made chancellors, while Miao Jinqing and Wang Yu were dismissed. He resigned on his mother's death; three months later he was recalled to oversee Chancellery affairs and concurrently to judge the Department of Public Works, and returned to power. He was enfeoffed as Baron of Xuchang and promoted to vice director of the Palace Secretariat. Early in the Shangyuan era he was raised to third-grade associate director of the Chancellery; when court halberds were to be bestowed, some urged that accepting such an honor in mourning dress was improper; Yin removed his mourning garments and bowed to receive them, and people mocked the breach of ritual.

諲引妻之父楚賓為衛尉少卿，楚賓子震為郎官。 中人馬尚言者，素匿於諲，為人求官，諲奏為藍田尉。 事覺，帝怒，命敬羽窮治，殺尚言，以其肉賜從官，罷諲為太子賓客。 數月，拜荊州長史、澧朗峽忠等五州節度使。 諲始建請荊州置南都，詔可。 於是更號江陵府，以諲為尹，置永平軍萬人，遏吳、蜀之沖，以湖南之嶽、潭、郴、道、邵、連，黔中之涪凡七州，隸其道。 初，荊州長史張惟一以衡州蠻酋陳希昂為司馬，督家兵千人自防，惟一親將牟遂金與相忤，希昂率兵至惟一所捕之，惟一懼，斬其首以謝，悉以遂金兵屬之，乃退，自是政一出希昂，後入朝，遷常州刺史，過江陵入謁，諲伏甲擊殺之，誅黨偶數十人，積屍府門，內外震服。
Yin installed his father-in-law Chubin as vice director of the Imperial Ancestral Temple and Chubin's son Zhen as a palace gentleman. The eunuch Ma Shangyan, whom Yin had long sheltered, solicited offices for others; Yin memorialized to make him magistrate of Lantian. When the affair came to light the emperor was furious, ordered Jing Yu to investigate thoroughly, executed Shangyan and had his flesh given to attending officials, and demoted Yin to guest of the heir apparent. Several months later he was appointed chief administrator of Jingzhou and military commissioner over five prefectures including Li, Lang, Xia, and Zhong. Yin was the first to propose making Jingzhou a southern capital; the emperor approved. The district was then renamed Jiangling prefecture with Yin as prefect; a Yongping army of ten thousand men was established to guard the route between Wu and Shu; seven prefectures in Hunan and Fu in Qianzhong were placed under his jurisdiction. Earlier Chief Administrator Zhang Weiyi of Jingzhou had made the Hengzhou Man chieftain Chen Xi'ang his major with a thousand household troops for defense; Weiyi's officer Mou Suijin quarreled with Xi'ang, who marched on Weiyi's headquarters and seized him; Weiyi, in fear, beheaded Suijin to appease Xi'ang and handed over all his troops, after which Xi'ang controlled the prefecture; later Xi'ang went to court and was made prefect of Changzhou; passing Jiangling he paid a courtesy call; Yin set an ambush and killed him, executed dozens of his followers, and piled the bodies at the prefectural gate until the region was cowed into submission.

妖人申泰芝用左道事李輔國，擢諫議大夫，置軍邵、道二州間，以泰芝總之，納群蠻金，賞以緋紫，出褚中詔書賜衣示之，群蠻怵於賞，而財不足，更為剽掠，吏不敢制。 潭州刺史龐承鼎疾其奸，因泰芝過潭，縛付吏，劾贓鉅萬，得左道讖記，並奏之。 輔國矯追泰芝還京，既召見，反譖承鼎陷不辜，詔諲按罪。 諲使判官嚴郢具獄，暴泰芝惡。 帝不省，賜承鼎死，流郢建州。 後泰芝終以贓徙死，承鼎追原其誣。
The sorcerer Shen Taizhi practiced heterodox rites in Li Fuguo's service and was made remonstrance grand master; troops were stationed between Shao and Dao with Taizhi in command; he extorted gold from the Man tribes, rewarded them with crimson and purple robes, and produced edicts from his pouch to prove imperial favor; tempted by rewards but still poor, the tribes turned again to raiding, and officials dared not stop them. Tanzhou prefect Pang Chengdong detested his crimes; when Taizhi passed through Tan he had him bound and handed over to the authorities, impeached him for bribes in the tens of thousands, seized heterodox prophetic writings, and memorialized the whole case. Fuguo forged a recall bringing Taizhi to the capital; after an audience Taizhi slandered Chengdong for framing an innocent man, and an edict ordered Yin to investigate. Yin sent his aide Yan Ying to compile the case and expose Taizhi's crimes. The emperor would not listen; Chengdong was sentenced to death and Ying exiled to Jianzhou. Taizhi later died in exile for corruption; Chengdong was posthumously cleared of the false charge.

諲為治，不急細務，決大事剛果不撓。 始在河西，悉知諸將能否，及為尹，奏取材者數十人總牙兵，故威惠兩行。 諲之相，與李揆不平，既斥，乃用善治聞。 揆恐帝復用，即妄奏置軍湖南非便，又陰遣人刺諲過失。 諲上疏訟其事，帝怒，逐揆出之，顯條其罪。 諲苦羸疾，卒，年五十一，贈吏部尚書。
In administration Yin did not fuss over small matters, but in great decisions he was firm and unyielding. In Hexi he had learned which generals were capable; as prefect he selected dozens of able men to command the guard troops, and so combined authority with kindness. As chancellor he had been at odds with Li Kui; after his dismissal he won renown for effective rule. Kui, fearing the emperor might recall him, falsely memorialized that stationing troops in Hunan was imprudent and secretly sent men to dig up Yin's faults. Yin memorialized to expose the plot; the emperor was furious, expelled Kui, and publicly listed his crimes. Worn down by illness, Yin died at fifty-one and was posthumously honored as minister of personnel.

諲在朝不稱任職相，及為荊州，號令明，賦斂均一。 其治尚威信，故軍士用命，闔境無盜賊，民歌詠之。 自至德以來，處方面數十人，諲最有名。 荊人生構房祠，及歿，吏裒錢十萬徙祠府西。 始，諲知杜鴻漸、元載才，薦於朝，後皆為宰相。
Yin did not distinguish himself as chancellor at court, but as prefect of Jingzhou his orders were clear and his taxes even-handed. He ruled through authority and trust; the troops obeyed, banditry vanished throughout the region, and the people sang his praise. From the Zhide era onward, among dozens who held regional commands, none was more celebrated than Yin. The people of Jing built a shrine to him on their own initiative; after his death officials raised a hundred thousand cash and moved the shrine to the west of the prefectural seat. Early on Yin recognized the talents of Du Hongjian and Yuan Zai and recommended them to court; both later became chancellors.

永泰中，嚴郢以故吏請謚有司，博士獨孤及謚曰「肅」，郢以故事宰相謚皆二名，請益曰「忠肅」。 及執奏，謂：「謚在義美惡，不在多名。 文王伐崇，周公殺三監、淮夷，重耳一戰而霸，而謚曰文。 冀缺之恪，寧俞之忠，隨會不忘其君，而謚曰武。 故知稱其大、略其細也。 且二名謚，非古也。 漢興，蕭何、張良、霍去病、霍光以文武大略，佐漢致太平，一名不盡其善，乃有文終、文成、景桓、宣成之謚。 唐興，參用漢制，魏徵以王道佐時近『文』，愛君忘身近『貞』，二者並優，廢一莫可，故曰文貞。 蕭瑀端直近『貞』，性多猜近『褊』，言『褊』則失『貞』，稱『貞』則遺『褊』，故曰貞褊。 蓋有為為之也。 若跡無異稱，則易以一字。 故杜如晦曰成，封德彜曰明，王珪曰懿，陳叔達曰忠，溫彥博曰恭，岑文本曰憲，韋世源曰昭，皆當時赫赫居宰相位者，謚不過一名。 而言故事宰相必以二名，固所未聞。 宜如前謚。 」遂不改。
During the Yongtai era Yan Ying, as a former subordinate, petitioned the authorities for a posthumous title; the erudite Dugu Ji proposed Su; Ying, citing precedent that chancellors received two-character posthumous titles, asked that it be expanded to Zhongsu. When he presented the memorial he argued, "A posthumous title lies in praising virtue and condemning vice, not in multiplying names. King Wen attacked Chong, the Duke of Zhou executed the Three Overseers and the Huaiyi, and Chong'er became hegemon after a single battle—yet all were posthumously titled Wen. Ji Que was reverent, Ning Yu loyal, and Sui Hui never forgot his lord—yet they were posthumously titled Wu. This shows that a title names what is great and passes over what is small. Moreover, two-character posthumous titles are not ancient practice. When the Han arose, Xiao He, Zhang Liang, Huo Qubing, and Huo Guang, with grand civil and military designs, helped bring the dynasty to peace; a single character could not exhaust their merit, and so they received compound titles such as Wenzong, Wencheng, Jinghuan, and Xuancheng. When Tang arose it followed the Han model: Wei Zheng's kingly service to the age approached Wen, and his love of the ruler at the cost of self approached Zhen; both virtues were outstanding and neither could be dropped, hence Wenzhen. Xiao Yu's uprightness approached Zhen, but his suspicious nature approached Pian; to say Pian would lose Zhen, to say Zhen would omit Pian—hence Zhenpian. Such cases were deliberate exceptions. Where the record offered nothing exceptional, a single character was enough. Thus Du Ruhui was titled Cheng, Feng Deyi Ming, Wang Gui Yi, Chen Shuda Zhong, Wen Yanbo Gong, Cen Wende Xian, and Wei Shiyuan Zhao—all celebrated chancellors of their day—yet each received only a single-character posthumous title. The claim that chancellors must by precedent receive two-character titles is something never heard of before. The earlier title should stand. " It was therefore not changed.
29
贊曰：孔子稱才難。 然人之才有限，不得皆善。 觀圓之銳，而失守出奔； 晉卿雅厚，而少風采臧否； 冕明強，嗜利不知大體； 諲輔政，功名不及治郡。 然各以所長顯於時。 故聖人使人也器之，不窮所不能而後為治也。 遵慶寡疵，中人之賢與。
The encomium says: Confucius said that talent is hard to find. Yet human talent has its limits, and no one can excel in everything. Cui Yuan was sharp yet lost his command and fled; Miao Jinqing was gracious and mild, yet lacked the force to judge right from wrong; Pei Mian was capable and forceful, yet greedy and blind to larger duties; Lü Yin as chancellor achieved less than he did governing a prefecture. Yet each made his particular strength count in his own day. That is why the sage employs men according to their capacities, not demanding what they cannot give, and only then achieves good government. Zunqing had few faults—perhaps the worth of a capable but unremarkable man.

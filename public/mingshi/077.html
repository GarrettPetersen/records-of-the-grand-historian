1
《記》曰：「取財於地，而取法於天。 富國之本，在於農桑。 」明初，沿元之舊，錢法不通而用鈔，又禁民間以銀交易，宜若不便於民。 而洪、永、熙、宣之際，百姓充實，府藏衍溢。 蓋是時，劭農務墾闢，土無萊蕪，人敦本業，又開屯田、中鹽以給邊軍，餫餉不仰藉於縣官，故上下交足，軍民胥裕。 其後，屯田壞於豪強之兼併，計臣變鹽法。 於是邊兵悉仰食太倉，轉輸住往不給。 世宗以後，耗財之道廣，府庫匱竭。 神宗乃加賦重徵，礦稅四出，移正供以實左藏。 中涓羣小，橫斂侵漁。 民多逐末，田卒汙萊。 吏不能拊循，而覆侵刻之。 海內困敝，而儲積益以空乏。 昧者多言復通鈔法可以富國，不知國初之充裕在勤農桑，而不在行鈔法也。 夫韁本節用，為理財之要。 明一代理財之道，始所以得，終所以失，條其本末，著於篇。
The Classic says: "Draw wealth from the land, and take your standards from Heaven." The foundation of a prosperous state is agriculture and sericulture. " In the early Ming, the dynasty retained Yuan currency practices: coin did not circulate and paper notes were used instead, while private trade in silver was forbidden — conditions that would seem plainly burdensome for ordinary people. Yet under Hongwu, Yongle, Hongxi, and Xuande, the people were well provided for and the state coffers overflowed. At that time the state vigorously promoted farming and land reclamation; fields lay uncultivated nowhere, and people kept to their primary occupations. Military colonies and salt-transport schemes supplied the frontier armies, so that grain rations no longer depended on county officials. Both court and countryside were fully supplied, and soldiers and civilians alike prospered. Later, military colonies were ruined by encroachment from powerful local magnates, and fiscal officials rewrote the salt laws. Frontier troops then came to depend entirely on grain from the Grand Granary, and transport supply often failed to keep pace. After the Jiajing Emperor, channels of expenditure multiplied and the treasuries were drained. The Wanli Emperor responded with supplemental levies and heavier taxation, dispatched mining taxes in every direction, and diverted regular tribute revenue to fill the Left Treasury. Eunuch attendants and petty officials extorted and plundered at will. The people turned increasingly to trade and secondary pursuits, and fields were left to run wild. Officials failed to comfort and guide them, and instead added further oppression and exactions. The empire grew exhausted and impoverished, while stored reserves dwindled ever further. The uninformed often claimed that reviving paper currency would enrich the state, not understanding that the dynasty's early abundance came from diligent farming and sericulture, not from the circulation of paper notes. Strengthening the economic foundation and restraining expenditure are the essentials of fiscal management. The Ming dynasty's fiscal methods — how they were first established and how they were ultimately lost — are here traced from root to branch in this chapter.

○戶口田制屯田莊田
○ Household Registers, Land System, Military Colonies, and Estate Lands
3
太祖籍天下戶口，置戶帖、戶籍，具書名、歲、居地。 籍上戶部，帖給之民。 有司歲計其登耗以聞。 及郊祀，中書省以戶籍陳壇下，薦之天，祭畢而藏之。 洪武十四年詔天下編賦役黃冊，以一百十戶為一里，推丁糧多者十戶為長，餘百戶為十甲，甲凡十人。 歲役裏長一人，甲首一人，董一里一甲之事。 先後以丁糧多寡為序，凡十年一週，曰排年。 在城曰坊，近城曰廂，鄉都曰裏。 裏編為冊，冊首總為一圖。 鰥寡孤獨不任役者，附十甲後為畸零。 僧道給度牒，有田者編冊如民科，無田者亦為畸零。 每十年有司更定其冊，以丁糧增減而升降之。 冊凡四：一上戶部，其三則布政司、府、縣各存一焉。 上戶部者，冊面黃紙，故謂之黃冊。 年終進呈，送後湖東西二庫庋藏之。 歲命戶科給事中一人、御史二人、戶部主事四人釐校訛舛。 其後黃冊只具文，有司徵稅、編徭，則自為一冊，曰白冊云。
The founding emperor registered households throughout the realm, issuing household placards and compiling household registers that recorded each person's name, age, and place of residence. The registers were filed with the Ministry of Revenue, and the placards were issued to the people. Each year local officials calculated gains and losses in the registers and reported them upward. At the suburban sacrifices, the Secretariat presented the household registers before the altar, offered them to Heaven, and stored them away once the rites were complete. In Hongwu 14, an edict ordered the compilation of yellow registers of tax and corvée obligations throughout the realm. One hundred and ten households formed a li; the ten households with the greatest adult-male and grain-tax obligations became li leaders, and the remaining hundred households were divided into ten jia of ten persons each. Each year one li chief and one jia head were appointed by rotation to manage the affairs of their li and jia respectively. Order of service was determined by the amount of adult males and grain tax owed, with a full cycle completed every ten years — this was called the rotating-year roster. In cities the units were called wards; near cities, suburban districts; in townships and rural districts, li. Each li was compiled into a register, with a summary diagram at the head of the volume. Widowers, widows, orphans, and others living alone who could not bear corvée obligations were listed after the ten jia as odd remainders. Monks and Daoist priests received ordination certificates; those who owned land were registered and taxed like commoners, while those without land were also classified as odd remainders. Every ten years local officials revised the registers, adjusting households up or down according to changes in adult males and grain-tax obligations. There were four copies of each register: one submitted to the Ministry of Revenue, and one each retained by the provincial administration commission, the prefecture, and the county. The copy sent to the Ministry of Revenue had yellow covers, hence the name yellow register. At year's end it was submitted to the throne and stored in the eastern and western archives on Rear Lake. Each year one supervising secretary from the Revenue Section, two censors, and four principal secretaries from the Ministry of Revenue were assigned to examine the registers and correct errors. Later the yellow register became a mere formality; when local officials collected taxes and compiled corvée obligations, they kept a separate working register called the white register.
4
凡戶三等：曰民，曰軍，曰匠。 民有儒，有醫，有陰陽。 軍有校尉，有力士，弓、鋪兵。 匠有廚役、裁縫、馬船之類。 瀕海有鹽竈。 寺有僧，觀有道士。 畢以其業著籍。 人戶以籍為斷，禁數姓合戶附籍。 漏口、脫戶，許自實。 裏設老人，選年高為眾所服者，導民善，平鄉里爭訟。 其人戶避徭役者曰逃戶。 年饑或避兵他徙者曰流民。 有故而出僑於外者曰附籍。 朝廷所移民曰移徙。
Households were divided into three categories: commoner, military, and artisan. Among commoners were scholars, physicians, and yin-yang diviners. Among military households were guard officers, strongmen, archers, and sentry troops. Among artisans were kitchen servants, tailors, horse-and-boat workers, and the like. Along the coast were salt-boiling households. Buddhist temples had monks; Daoist abbeys had priests. All were registered according to their occupation. Household membership was determined strictly by the register; combining several surnames into one household for registration was forbidden. Those who had omitted persons from the register or evaded registration were permitted to report themselves voluntarily. Each li appointed an elder, chosen from among those advanced in years and respected by the community, to guide people toward virtue and settle local disputes. Household members who evaded corvée and tax obligations were called fugitive households. Those who migrated elsewhere due to famine or to escape warfare were called floating populations. Those who left their native place for some reason and sojourned elsewhere were called attached registrations. Populations relocated by order of the court were called resettlement transfers.
5
凡逃戶，明初督令還本籍復業，賜復一年。 老弱不能歸及不願歸者，令在所著籍，授田輸賦。 正統時，造逃戶周知冊，核其丁糧。
As for fugitive households, in the early Ming they were compelled to return to their native registers and resume their occupations, and were granted a one-year tax remission. The aged and infirm who could not return, and those unwilling to return, were registered where they were, granted land, and required to pay taxes. During the Zhengtong reign, a comprehensive register of fugitive households was compiled to verify their adult males and grain-tax obligations.
6
凡流民，英宗令勘籍，編甲互保，屬在所裏長管轄之。 設撫民佐貳官。 歸本者，勞徠安輯，給牛、種、口糧。 又從河南、山西巡撫于謙言，免流民復業者稅。 成化初，荊、襄寇亂，流民百萬。 項忠、楊璿為湖廣巡撫，下令逐之，弗率者戍邊，死者無算。 祭酒周洪謨著《流民說》，引東晉時僑置郡縣之法，使近者附籍，遠者設州縣以撫之。 都御史李賓上其說。 憲宗命原傑出撫，招流民十二萬戶，給閒田，置鄖陽府，立上津等縣統治之。 河南巡撫張瑄亦請輯西北流民。 帝從其請。
As for floating populations, Emperor Yingzong ordered verification of registers, organization into mutual-guarantee jia, and oversight by the local li chief. Assistant officials for pacifying the people were appointed. Those returning to their native places were welcomed and settled with offers of cattle, seed, and grain rations. Following the recommendation of Yu Qian, Grand Coordinator of Henan and Shanxi, taxes were remitted for floating populations who resumed their occupations. At the beginning of the Chenghua reign, bandits ravaged Jingzhou and Xiangyang, and the floating population reached a million. Xiang Zhong and Yang Xuan, as Grand Coordinators of Huguang, ordered them expelled; those who did not comply were sent to garrison the frontier, and the dead were beyond counting. Zhou Hongmo, Grand Secretary of the National Academy, wrote "On Floating Populations," citing the Eastern Jin practice of establishing provisional commanderies and counties so that those nearby could attach to local registers while those farther away would have prefectures and counties set up to settle them. Censor-in-Chief Li Bin submitted this proposal to the throne. Emperor Xianzong dispatched Yuan Jie to pacify the region; he recruited 120,000 floating households, granted idle land, established Yunyang Prefecture, and created Shangjin and other counties to govern them. Zhang Xuan, Grand Coordinator of Henan, also requested authority to settle the floating populations of the northwest. The emperor granted his request.
7
凡附籍者，正統時，老疾致仕事故官家屬，離本籍千里者許收附，不及千里者發還。 景泰中，令民籍者收附，軍、匠、竈役冒民籍者發還。
As for attached registrations, during Zhengtong family members of officials who retired due to age or illness or who left office for cause were permitted to attach locally if they were more than a thousand li from their native register; if less than a thousand li, they were sent back. During the Jingtai reign, those with civilian registration were permitted to attach locally; those from military, artisan, or salt-boiler households who falsely claimed civilian status were sent back.
8
其移徙者，明初，當徙蘇、鬆、嘉、湖、杭民之無田者四千餘戶，往耕臨濠，給牛、種、車、糧，以資遣之，三年不徵其稅。 徐達平沙漠，徙北平山後民三萬五千八百餘戶，散處諸府衛，籍為軍者給衣糧，民給田。 又以沙漠遺民三萬二千八百餘戶屯田北平，置屯二百五十四，開地千三百四十三頃。 復徙江南民十四萬於鳳陽。 戶部郎中劉九皋言：「古狹鄉之民，聽遷之寬鄉，欲地無遺利，人無失業也。 」太祖採其議，遷山凱撒、潞民於河北。 眾屢徙浙西及山西民於滁、和、北平、山東、河南。 又徙登、萊、青民於東昌、兗州。 又徙直隸、浙江民二萬戶於京師，充倉腳夫。 太祖時徙民最多，其間有以罪徙者。 建文帝命武康伯徐理往北平度地處之。 成祖核太原、平陽、澤、潞、遼、沁、汾丁多田少及無田之家，分其丁口以實北平。 自是以後，移徙者鮮矣。
As for resettlement transfers, in the early Ming more than four thousand landless households from Suzhou, Songjiang, Jiaxing, Huzhou, and Hangzhou were relocated to farm at Linhao; cattle, seed, carts, and grain were provided to support the move, and taxes were not levied for three years. When Xu Da pacified the desert, more than 35,800 households from beyond the mountains of northern Ping were relocated and dispersed among various prefectures and guard posts; those registered as military received clothing and grain, while commoners received land. Another 32,800-odd desert remnant households were settled in military colonies in northern Ping, establishing 254 colonies and opening 1,343 qing of land. Another 140,000 people from Jiangnan were relocated to Fengyang. Liu Jiugao, a director in the Ministry of Revenue, said: "In antiquity, people from crowded districts were permitted to move to less populated ones, so that no land would lie unused and no one would be without livelihood." " The founding emperor adopted this proposal and relocated the peoples of Shankaisha and Lu to Hebei. Populations were repeatedly moved from western Zhejiang and Shanxi to Chuzhou, Hezhou, northern Ping, Shandong, and Henan. People from Dengzhou, Laizhou, and Qingzhou were also relocated to Dongchang and Yanzhou. Another 20,000 households from Zhili and Zhejiang were relocated to the capital to serve as granary porters. Relocations were most numerous under the founding emperor; among them were some moved as punishment for crimes. Emperor Jianwen ordered Xu Li, Baron of Wukang, to go to northern Ping to survey land and settle the relocated population. Emperor Chengzu examined households in Taiyuan, Pingyang, Ze, Lu, Liao, Qin, and Fen that had many adult males but little land or none at all, and divided their population to populate northern Ping. From this time onward, resettlement transfers became rare.
9
初，太祖設養濟院收無告者，月給糧。 設漏澤園葬貧民。 天下府州縣立義塚。 又行養老之政，民年八十以上賜爵。 復下詔優恤遭難兵民。 然懲元末豪強侮貧弱，立法多右貧抑富。 嘗命戶部籍浙江等九布政司、應天十八府州富民萬四千三百餘戶，以次召見，徙其家以實京師，謂之富戶。 成祖時，復選應天、浙江富民三千戶，充北京宛、大二縣廂長，附籍京師，仍應本籍徭役。 供給日久，貧乏逃竄，輒選其本籍殷實戶僉補。 宣德間定制，逃者發邊充軍，官司鄰裏隱匿者俱坐罪。 弘治五年始免解在逃富戶，每戶徵銀三兩，與廂民助役。 嘉靖中減為二兩，以充邊餉。 太祖立法之意，本仿漢徙富民實關中之制，其後事久弊生，遂為厲階。
Initially, the founding emperor established relief hospices to take in those with no one to care for them, providing monthly grain rations. Public burial grounds were established to bury the poor. Benevolent burial plots were established in prefectures, departments, and counties throughout the realm. Policies for supporting the aged were also implemented; commoners aged eighty and above were granted honorary ranks. Edicts were again issued granting generous relief to soldiers and civilians who had suffered hardship. Yet, reacting against the powerful local magnates of the late Yuan who had oppressed the poor and weak, legislation largely favored the poor and restrained the rich. He once ordered the Ministry of Revenue to register more than 14,300 wealthy households from the nine provincial commissions including Zhejiang and the eighteen prefectures and departments of Yingtian, summoning them in succession and relocating their families to populate the capital — these were called wealthy households. During Chengzu's reign, 3,000 wealthy households from Yingtian and Zhejiang were again selected to serve as suburban district chiefs of Wanping and Daxing counties in Beijing; they attached their registration to the capital but still fulfilled corvée obligations in their native registers. As support duties dragged on, many grew impoverished and fled, and wealthy households from their native registers were conscripted to fill the vacancies. During the Xuande reign regulations were fixed: fugitives were sent to the frontier as soldiers, and officials, neighbors, and li associates who concealed them were all punished. Beginning in Hongzhi 5, wealthy households in flight were no longer compelled to report in person; each household paid three taels of silver to assist corvée duties for suburban residents. During the Jiajing reign this was reduced to two taels, allocated to frontier provisions. The founding emperor's intent in legislating was originally to emulate the Han practice of relocating wealthy households to populate the Guanzhong region; over time abuses arose, and the policy became a source of harm.
10
戶口之數，增減不一，其可考者，洪武二十六年，天下戶一千六十五萬二千八百七十，口六千五十四萬五千八百十二。 弘治四年，戶九百十一萬三千四百四十六，口五千三百二十八萬一千一百五十八。 萬曆六年，戶一千六十二萬一千四百三十六，口六千六十九萬二千八百五十六。 太祖當兵燹之後，戶口顧極盛。 其後承平日久，反不及焉。 靖難兵起，淮以北鞠為茂草，其時民數反增於前。 後乃遞減，至天順間為最衰。 成、弘繼盛，正德以後又減。 戶口所以減者，周忱謂：「投倚於豪門，或冒匠竄兩京，或冒引賈四方，舉家舟居，莫可蹤跡也。 」而要之，戶口增減，由於政令張弛。 故宣宗嘗與羣臣論歷代戶口，以為「其盛也，本於休養生息，其衰也，由土木兵戎」，殆篤論云。
Household numbers rose and fell unevenly; among figures that can be verified, in Hongwu 26 there were 10,652,870 households throughout the realm and 60,545,812 persons. In Hongzhi 4: 9,113,446 households and 53,281,158 persons. In Wanli 6: 10,621,436 households and 60,692,856 persons. Remarkably, under the founding emperor, household registers reached their peak even after the devastation of warfare. In the long peace that followed, they actually fell short of this. When the Jingnan rebellion broke out, the region north of the Huai River was trampled into wild grass, yet the population count at that time actually rose above the previous figure. Afterward it declined in succession, reaching its lowest point during the Tianshun reign. Chenghua and Hongzhi saw renewed prosperity; after Zhengde there was decline again. Zhou Chen explained why household registers declined: "Some attach themselves to powerful clans; some falsely claim artisan status and slip into the two capitals; some falsely pose as merchants and travel in every direction — whole families live aboard boats with no trace to be found." " In essence, the rise and fall of registered households depended on whether government orders were enforced strictly or laxly. Thus the Xuande Emperor once debated with his ministers the rise and fall of population across the ages. His verdict was that prosperity rested on letting the people rest and recover, while decline came from grand construction and incessant war — a remark that cuts to the heart of the matter.
11
明土田之制，凡二等：曰官田，曰民田。 初，官田皆宋、元時入官田地。 厥後有還官田，沒官田，斷入官田，學田，皇莊，牧馬草場，城鵒需苜蓿地，牲地，園陵墳地，公佔隙地，諸王、公主、勳戚、大臣、內監、寺觀賜乞莊田，百官職田，邊臣養廉田，軍、民、商屯田，通謂之官田。 其餘為民田。
Under the Ming, all land fell into two categories: official land and private land. Initially, official land consisted entirely of fields that had been taken into government hands under the Song and Yuan. Later the category expanded to include land restored to the state, confiscated land, land adjudicated into government hands, school fields, imperial estates, cavalry pastures, pigeon-loft hayfields, sacrificial grounds, mausoleum plots, vacant plots seized for public use, estates granted to or solicited by princes, princesses, imperial in-laws, high ministers, eunuchs, and monasteries, salary allotments for officials, "integrity stipend" fields for frontier administrators, and military, civilian, and merchant colony farms — all of these were collectively termed official land. Everything else counted as private land.
12
元季喪亂，版籍多亡，田賦無準。 明太祖即帝位，遣周鑄等百六十四人，核浙西田畝，定其賦稅。 復命戶部核實天下土田。 而兩浙富民畏避徭役，大率以田產寄他戶，謂之鐵腳詭寄。 洪武二十年命國子生武淳等分行州縣，隨糧定區。 區設糧長四人，量度田畝方圓，次以字型大小，悉書主名及田之丈尺，編類為冊，狀如魚鱗，號曰魚鱗圖冊。 先是，詔天下編黃冊，以戶為主，詳具舊管、新收、開除、實在之數為四柱式。 而魚鱗圖冊以土田為主，諸原阪、墳衍、下隰、沃瘠、沙鹵之別畢具。 魚鱗冊為經，土田之訟質焉。 黃冊為緯，賦役之法定焉。 凡質賣田土，備書稅糧科則，官為籍記之，毋令產去稅存以為民害。 又以中原田多蕪，命省臣議，計民授田。 設司農司，開治河南，掌其事。 臨濠之田，驗其丁力，計畝給之，毋許兼併。 北方近城地多不治，召民耕，人給十五畝，蔬地二畝，免租三年。 每歲中書省奏天下墾田數，少者畝以千計，多者至二十餘萬。 官給牛及農具者，乃收其稅，額外墾荒者永不起科。 二十六年核天下土田，總八百五十萬七千六百二十三頃，蓋駸駸無棄土矣。
In the final years of the Yuan, war and turmoil destroyed most land records, leaving field taxes without any reliable basis. Upon taking the throne, the Hongwu Emperor sent Zhou Zhuo and 164 others to survey field acreage in western Zhejiang and set tax rates accordingly. He then ordered the Ministry of Revenue to verify landholding throughout the empire. Wealthy households in the Jiangnan region, seeking to dodge corvée duties, commonly registered their property under someone else's name — a practice known as "iron-foot ghost registration." In 1387, he dispatched Imperial Academy students including Wu Chun to the counties and prefectures to draw tax districts based on grain quotas. Each district appointed four grain wardens who measured every plot, arranged parcels on the map in descending size, recorded the owner's name and exact dimensions, and compiled the result into a register whose overlapping plots resembled fish scales — the famous yulin tuce, or fish-scale land register. Earlier, the throne had ordered household registers compiled empire-wide — the huangce, or yellow books — organized by household with four columns tracking prior holdings, new acquisitions, deductions, and present totals. The fish-scale register, by contrast, was organized by land rather than by household, cataloguing every distinction between upland and slope, marsh and plain, rich soil and poor, sandy and saline ground. The fish-scale register served as the authoritative record against which land disputes were judged. The yellow register fixed the basis for taxes and labor obligations. Whenever land changed hands, the tax schedule had to be fully recorded and entered in the official registers — a safeguard against the common abuse where property transferred but tax liability stayed behind, to the seller's ruin. Because much farmland in the Central Plains lay abandoned, he ordered provincial officials to devise a scheme for allocating land to settlers. A Directorate of Agriculture was set up with its headquarters in Henan to administer the program. At Linhuai, land was apportioned by measuring each household's labor capacity, acre by acre — consolidation was forbidden. Much of the land near northern cities lay idle; settlers were recruited to cultivate it, with fifteen mu of grain land plus two mu for vegetables apiece, rent-free for three years. Each year the Central Secretariat reported reclaimed acreage empire-wide — sometimes a few thousand mu, sometimes more than 200,000. Settlers who received oxen and tools from the state paid tax on their allotment, but any land they cleared beyond that quota was permanently tax-exempt. A national land survey in 1393 counted 8,507,623 qing — by then, scarcely an acre of cultivable soil remained idle.

凡田以近郭為上地，迤遠為中地、下地。 五尺為步，步二百四十為畝，畝百為頃。 太祖仍元裏社制，河北諸州縣土著者以社分裏甲，遷民分屯之地以屯分裏甲。 社民先佔畝廣，屯民新佔畝狹，故屯地謂之小畝，社地謂之廣畝。 至宣德間，墾荒田永不起科及洿下斥鹵無糧者，皆核入賦額，數溢於舊。 有司乃以大畝當小畝以符舊額，有數畝當一畝者。 步尺參差不一，人得以意贏縮，土地不均，未有如北方者。 貴州田無頃畝尺籍，悉徵之土官。 而諸處土田，日久頗淆亂，與黃冊不符。 弘治十五年，天下土田止四百二十二萬八千五十八頃，官田視民田得七之一。 嘉靖八年，霍韞奉命修會典，言：「自洪武迄弘治百四十年，天下額田已減強半，而湖廣、河南、廣東失額尤多。 非撥給於王府，則欺隱於猾民。 廣東無籓府，非欺隱即委棄於寇賊矣。 司國計者，可不究心？ 」是時，桂萼、郭弘化、唐能、簡霄先後疏請核實田畝，而顧鼎臣請履畝丈量，丈量之議由此起。 江西安福、河南裕州首行之，而法未詳具，人多疑憚。 其後福建諸州縣，為經、緯二冊，其法頗詳。 然率以地為主，田多者猶得上下其手。 神宗初，建昌知府許孚遠為歸戶冊，則以田從人，法簡而密矣。 萬曆六年，帝用大學士張居正議，天下田畝通行丈量，限三載竣事。 用開方法，以徑圍乘除，畸零截補。 於是豪猾不得欺隱，裏甲免賠累，而小民無虛糧。 總計田數七百一萬三千九百七十六頃，視弘治時贏三百萬頃。 然居正尚綜核，頗以溢額為功。 有司爭改小弓以求田多，或掊克見田以充虛額。 北直隸、湖廣、大同、宣府，遂先後按溢額田增賦云。
Fields nearest the city walls ranked as top-grade; farther out as middling, and still farther as inferior. The standard was five chi to the pace, 240 paces to the mu, and 100 mu to the qing. Hongwu preserved the Yuan li-and-she system: in Hebei, native residents were grouped into li and jia by community (she); resettled colonists were grouped by colony unit (tun). Natives who had claimed land early held plots measured by generous mu; newer colonists got cramped plots — so colony land was called "small mu" and native community land "broad mu." By the Xuande era, previously tax-exempt reclaimed land and low-lying saline plots that yielded nothing had all been folded into the tax rolls, swelling totals beyond earlier figures. Local officials compensated by counting several "small mu" as one "large mu" to match old quotas — sometimes several actual mu equaled one official mu. Measuring rods differed from place to place, leaving room for manipulation; nowhere was land distribution more skewed than in the north. Guizhou had no standardized land registers at all; taxes were collected entirely through local native chiefs. Over time, land records across the empire fell into disarray and no longer matched the yellow registers. By 1502, registered land had fallen to just 4,228,058 qing — official land comprising roughly one-seventh of private land. In 1529, Huo Yun, charged with revising the Collected Statutes, reported: "In the 140 years since Hongwu, registered land quota has been cut by more than half — with the steepest losses in Huguang, Henan, and Guangdong. Lost either to princely fief allocations or to concealment by sharp-dealing landowners. Guangdong had no princely estates to absorb the shortfall — whatever was missing had either been hidden or abandoned to bandits. Surely those responsible for the national accounts could not afford to ignore this? Gui E, Guo Honghua, Tang Neng, and Jian Xiao then petitioned in succession for a land audit; Gu Dingchen called for field-by-field surveying — and the nationwide measurement campaign was born. Anfu in Jiangxi and Yuzhou in Henan piloted the scheme first, but the procedures were incomplete and met widespread suspicion. Later, Fujian counties developed paired warp-and-weft registers with considerably more detailed procedures. But because registers were still organized by land rather than by household, wealthy landowners could still manipulate the figures. Early in Wanli's reign, Jianchang prefect Xu Fuyuan introduced the "return-to-household register," tying fields to their owners — simpler and tighter than what came before. In 1578, the emperor adopted Grand Secretary Zhang Juzheng's plan for a nationwide land survey, to be completed within three years. Surveyors used geometric formulas — multiplying and dividing diameters and perimeters — to calculate irregular plots by trimming corners and filling gaps. The wealthy could no longer hide land; village heads were no longer liable for others' shortfalls; and ordinary peasants were freed from paying tax on land they did not hold. The survey counted 7,013,976 qing — three million more than the Hongzhi figure. Zhang Juzheng, however, prized tight fiscal control and treated inflated totals as a mark of success. Officials shortened measuring rods to inflate acreage, or squeezed peasants' existing plots to meet phantom quotas. Zhili, Huguang, Datong, and Xuanfu soon raised tax rates on the newly "discovered" surplus land.

The colony-farm system had two branches: military colonies and civilian colonies. Early in Hongwu's reign, a militia headquarters was established to embed troops among farmers — the finest version of the policy. Generals were also ordered to establish farming garrisons along the Longjiang; Kang Maocai's colony outperformed all others and was publicly commended as an example to the rest. In 1370, when the Central Secretariat proposed taxing colonists at Taiyuan and Shuozhou, the emperor refused. The next year the Secretariat proposed: "For colony farms in Henan, Shandong, Beiping, Shaanxi, Shanxi, and Huai'an — those receiving oxen and seed from the state would pay a fifty-percent rate; self-supplying colonists, thirty percent. The emperor deferred collection; after three years the rate would be one dou per mu. In 1373, Vice Director of the Imperial Stud Liangye Xiantiemuer reported: "From Ningxia across eight hundred li to Chuancheng in southwest Sichuan and Tatai in the northeast, the soil is rich and fit for resettling refugees as colonists. The proposal was approved. Deng Yu, Tang He, and other generals were sent to establish colonies in Shaanxi, Zhangde, Runing, Beiping, and Yongping, while settlers from Zhending in Shanxi were relocated to farm around Fengyang. After sailors drowned on supply runs to Liaodong, colony policy was intensified until soldiers and civilians across the empire were all engaged in land reclamation.

Settlers moved to less crowded regions; recruits and convict exiles formed civilian colonies under civil administration, while military colonies fell under guard-unit command. On the frontier, thirty percent of troops garrisoned the walls and seventy percent farmed. In the interior, twenty percent garrisoned and eighty percent farmed. Each soldier received a fifty-mu allotment; the state supplied oxen and tools, taught arboriculture, reduced rents, dispatched officials to supervise deliveries, and punished corrupt local officers. The initial rate was one dou per mu. In 1402 the quota was fixed: each fifty-mu share owed twelve shi of grain to the colony granary for the unit's own use; surplus went to supplement guard-unit pay. Early in Yongle, performance standards were set: beyond the twelve shi needed for subsistence, six shi surplus was the target — exceeding it earned a cash reward, falling short meant a pay cut. Because soil quality varied, each unit was required to plant sample plots and compare annual yields. Chen Huai of Taiyuan Left Guard produced twenty-three shi surplus per soldier from his sample plots; the emperor ordered him lavishly rewarded. Ningxia commander He Fu amassed an exceptional grain surplus and received an imperial commendation. Minister of Revenue Yu Xin noted: "Huguang guard units collect grain in many varieties; I propose standardizing on rice equivalents. Two shi of millet, panicled millet, broomcorn, barley, buckwheat, or kaoliang would equal one shi of rice; two and a half shi of husked rice, sorghum, or glutinous millet; three shi of coix or barnyard millet — each equivalent to one shi of rice. Wheat, sesame, and beans counted one-for-one with rice. The proposal was approved and codified as law.

The garrison-to-farming ratios were also revised. At the most strategic frontier points, garrison duty outweighed farming. In remote or supply-starved regions, farming took priority; units of 100 colonists reported to a centurion, 300 to a chiliarch, and 500 or more to a battalion commander. Each colony posted a red placard listing the rules. Soldiers over sixty, the disabled, and minors farmed for their own subsistence outside the quota system. Colonists pulled from farming by official duties were exempt from grain levies, and guard units were barred from reassigning them arbitrarily. Colonies sprang up everywhere — from Liaodong in the east to Xuanfu and Datong in the north, Gansu in the west, Yunnan and Sichuan in the south, even Jiaozhi; throughout the Yellow River heartland, garrison farming was universal. Under the Xuande Emperor, repeated colony audits penalized units that abandoned farming for campaigns and powerful families that seized colony land — cutting their surplus-grain quota by half. Settlers arriving from the northern steppe received carts, oxen, and farm tools. Liaodong colony troops were classified in three tiers: those with both manpower and oxen ranked highest; those with one or the other, middle; those with neither, lowest. Emperor Yingzong exempted the twelve-shi subsistence quota from granary collection, taxing only six shi of surplus. Later, frontier colonists who opened new land were exempted from grain levies, and colony taxes along the borders were reduced by varying degrees. Under the Jingtai Emperor, with the frontier in turmoil, troops were split into two rotations — six days on guard, six days farming. In the early Chenghua reign, Xuanfu coordinator Ye Sheng bought 1,800 government oxen, supplied tools, and put troops to farming; grain was sold for silver to cover cavalry horse losses — to general approval on the frontier.

自正統後，屯政稍弛，而屯糧猶存三之二。 其後屯田多為內監、軍官佔奪，法盡壞。 憲宗之世頗議釐復，而視舊所入，不能什一矣。 弘治間，屯糧愈輕，有畝止三升者。 沿及正德，遼東屯田較永樂間田贏萬八千餘頃，而糧乃縮四萬六千餘石。 初，永樂時，屯田米常溢三之一，常操軍十九萬，以屯軍四萬供之。 而受供者又得自耕。 邊外軍無月糧，以是邊餉恆足。 及是，屯軍多逃死，常操軍止八萬，皆仰給於倉。 而邊外數擾，棄不耕。 劉瑾擅政，遣官分出丈田責逋。 希瑾意者，偽增田數，蒐括慘毒，戶部侍郎韓福尤急刻。 遼卒不堪，脅眾為亂，撫之乃定。
After the Zhengtong era, colony administration slackened, but colony grain revenues still stood at about two-thirds of their peak. Colony land was soon seized by eunuchs and military officers, and the system collapsed entirely. The Chenghua court debated restoration, but recovered revenues amounted to less than a tenth of the original intake. By the Hongzhi era, colony yields had shrunk so far that some plots yielded only three sheng per mu. By the Zhengde era, Liaodong registered 18,000 more qing than under Yongle — yet grain collections had fallen by over 46,000 shi. In Yongle's day, colony grain regularly ran one-third over quota; 40,000 colonists fed a standing force of 190,000. Those receiving rations could also farm on their own account. Troops beyond the passes had no monthly stipend, yet frontier provisions remained ample — thanks to colony grain. By now most colonists had fled or died; the standing force had fallen to 80,000, entirely dependent on government stores. The outer frontier, repeatedly raided, lay fallow. When Liu Jin seized power, he sent officials to survey land and collect tax arrears. Officials currying favor with Jin inflated land figures and extorted with brutal zeal; Vice Minister Han Fu was the most ruthless of all. Liaodong soldiers, driven past endurance, rose in mutiny; the disturbance was quelled only after concessions from the authorities.

In the early Ming, the court recruited salt merchants to establish transport stations along the frontiers—a system known as merchant colonies. By the Hongzhi reign, Ye Qi's reforms had broken the transport-station system. Huai salt merchants quit the trade entirely; northwestern merchants likewise relocated their households to the Huai region; frontier districts turned to ruins; rice reached five taels per shi, and border granaries stood empty. Under the Jiajing emperor, Yang Yiqing renewed the call for merchant transport stations and proposed reviving the old policy of settling civilians on the frontier, recruiting people from Longyou and western Guanzhong to garrison the border. Afterward Zhou Ze, Wang Chonggu, Lin Fu, Chen Shifu, Wang Ji, Wang Chaoyong, Tang Shunzhi, Wu Guifang, and others all pressed proposals for colony administration. Pang Shangpeng oversaw salt colonies in Jiangbei, then moved on to the Nine Frontiers, where he and Governor-General Wang Chonggu worked out colony policy in painstaking detail. By then inertia had set in for years, and in the end little was actually achieved. Supervising Secretary Guan Huaili said: "Colony farming cannot thrive. There are four reasons: first, the frontier is under strict military alert;" second, oxen and seed are not provided; third, able-bodied men flee and disperse; fourth, the fields lie beyond enemy lines; Even so, colony administrators still try to raise assessments from the registers—either by docking monthly rations or by charging per capita make-up payments."

Colony yields hit their lowest point under Hongzhi and Zhengde; they rose again in Jiajing, and by Longqing the levy had returned to one dou per mu. Yet more and more colonists deserted their posts. Grain officials paid no attention to whether colony fields were actually farmed and issued only half the monthly rations. Frontier colony plots sometimes turned saline or sandy, yet the grain quota could not be lowered. The colony-field censor added extra capital conversion charges on top of the quota, and colonists found the burden increasingly unbearable. By the Wanli era, registered colony land stood at more than 644,000 qing—249,000 qing less than under Hongwu. Fields shrank year by year while grain quotas grew; such was the decay. At the time, Shandong governor Zheng Rubi proposed opening farmland on the Changshan islands off the north coast of Dengzhou. Fujian governor Xu Fuyuan successfully reclaimed fields on Tanshan along the Fujian coast and then requested opening land on Nanri Mountain and Penghu as well; he also argued that coastal hills in Zhejiang—Chenqian, Jintang, Putuo, Yuhuan, and Nanji—could all be brought under cultivation. Tianjin governor Wang Yingjiao, for his part, proposed establishing colonies at Tianjin. Some plans were shelved at court and never enacted; others were abandoned soon after they began. Under the Tianqi emperor, touring censor Zhang Shenyan revived the proposal for Tianjin colony farming. Censor Zuo Guangdou ordered river commissioner Lu Guanxiang to expand paddy cultivation on a large scale, and Vice Minister of Imperial Rites Dong Yingju took up the work in turn. Zuo also set up colony schools in Hejian and Tianjin, tested candidates in horsemanship and archery, and granted one hundred mu to each student who passed. Li Jizhen, governor of Tianjin, also threw himself into colony work, but drought and locusts came year after year, and nothing lasting could be achieved. In the Ming there were many pasture grounds, and they often encroached on private holdings. But nothing harmed the people more than imperial estates and the manor lands of princes, meritorious kin, and eunuchs. The Hongwu emperor granted manor lands to meritorious officials from dukes and marquises down to grand councillors—up to one hundred qing in the largest cases—and princes received one thousand qing. He also granted public fields to dukes, marquises, and military officers, and public fields to civil officials as well, using the rent to pay their salaries. Every commander who fell in battle was granted public fields. Tenants on the manors of meritorious officials often used their patrons' power to defy the law; the emperor summoned the ministers and warned them against it. Later dukes and marquises were restored to annual stipends and returned their granted fields to the state.
20
仁、宣之世，乞請漸廣，大臣亦得請沒官莊舍。 然甯王權請灌城為庶子耕牧地，帝賜書，援祖制拒之。 至英宗時，諸王、外戚、中官所在佔官私田，或反誣民佔，請案治。 比案問得實，帝命還之民者非一。 乃下詔禁奪民田及奏請畿內地。 然權貴宗室莊田墳塋，或賜或請，不可勝計。 御馬太監劉順家人進薊州草場，進獻由此始。 宦官之田，則自尹奉、喜寧始。 初，洪熙時，有仁壽宮莊，其後又有清寧、未央宮莊。 天順三年，以諸王未出閣，供用浩繁，立東宮、德王、秀王莊田。 二王之籓，地仍歸官。 憲宗即位，以沒入曹吉祥地為宮中莊田，皇莊之名由此始。 其後莊田遍郡縣。 給事中齊莊言：「天子以四海為家，何必置立莊田，與貧民較利。 」弗聽。 弘治二年，戶部尚書李敏等以災異上言：「畿內皇莊有五，共地萬二千八百餘頃； 勳戚、中官莊田三百三十有二，共地三萬三千餘頃。 管莊官校招集羣小，稱莊頭、伴當，佔地土，斂財物，汙婦女。 稍與分辯，輒被誣奏。 官校執縛，舉家驚惶。 民心傷痛入骨，災異所由生。 乞革去管莊之人，付小民耕種，畝徵銀三分，充各宮用度。 」帝命戒飭莊戶。 又因御史言，罷仁壽宮莊，還之草場，且命凡侵牧地者，悉還其舊。
Under the Hongxi and Xuande emperors, land petitions grew more common, and even grand ministers could request confiscated manor houses and estates. When Prince Ning Quan asked for Guancheng as farmland and pasture for his younger sons, the emperor replied in writing, citing ancestral precedent to refuse. By the reign of Emperor Yingzong, princes, imperial in-laws, and eunuchs were seizing official and private land everywhere, sometimes falsely accusing commoners of encroachment and demanding prosecution. When investigations proved the facts, the emperor ordered land returned to the people in more than one case. He then issued an edict forbidding the seizure of commoners' land and petitions for land within the capital region. Yet manor lands and burial grounds for the powerful and the imperial clan, whether granted or petitioned for, were beyond counting. The household of Imperial Horse Supervisor Liu Shun presented the Jizhou pasture grounds to the throne—and with that began the practice of land presentations. Eunuch landholdings began with Yin Feng and Xi Ning. At first, under Hongxi, there was the Renshou Palace estate; later came the Qingning and Weiyang Palace estates as well. In the third year of Tianshun, because the princes had not yet left the palace and expenses were heavy, estates were set up for the crown prince, Prince De, and Prince Xiu. When the two princes went to their fiefs, the land reverted to the state. When Emperor Xianzong took the throne, he confiscated Cao Jixiang's lands as palace estates—the term "imperial estate" dates from this. After that, manor lands spread through counties and prefectures everywhere. Supervising Secretary Qi Zhuang said: "The Son of Heaven takes the four seas as his home—why establish manor lands and compete for profit with the poor?" The emperor would not heed him. In the second year of Hongzhi, Minister of Revenue Li Min and others memorialized on calamities: "Within the capital region there are five imperial estates, totaling more than 12,800 qing;" there are 332 estates belonging to meritorious kin and eunuchs, totaling more than 33,000 qing. Estate managers recruit thugs called estate heads and attendants—they seize land, extort goods, and assault women. Anyone who so much as disputes them is falsely accused to the throne. Official agents arrest and bind them, and whole families are thrown into terror. Popular grief runs bone-deep; this is where calamities come from. We beg that estate managers be removed, the land turned over to commoners to farm, and three fen of silver levied per mu to meet the needs of the palaces. The emperor ordered the estate households admonished. On the censors' advice, the Renshou Palace estate was abolished and returned to pasture, and anyone who had encroached on grazing land was ordered to restore it.
21
又定制，獻地王府者戍邊。 奉禦趙瑄獻雄縣地為皇莊，戶部尚書周經劾其違制，下瑄詔獄。 敕諸王輔導官，導王奏請者罪之。 然當日奏獻不絕，氣請亦愈繁。 徽、興、岐、衡四王，田多至七千餘頃。 會昌、建昌、慶雲三侯爭田，帝輒賜之。 武宗即位，逾月即建皇莊七，其後增至三百餘處。 諸王、外戚求請及奪民田者無算。
A regulation was also issued: anyone who presented land to a princely residence would be sent to frontier garrison duty. Palace attendant Zhao Xuan presented Xiong County land as an imperial estate; Minister of Revenue Zhou Jing impeached him for violating regulations, and Zhao was imprisoned by imperial order. An edict directed the tutors of the princes: any who guided a prince in such petitions would be punished. Yet land presentations never stopped, and shameless petitions grew ever bolder. The four princes Hui, Xing, Qi, and Heng each held as much as seven thousand qing or more. When the three marquises Huichang, Jianchang, and Qingyun disputed land, the emperor promptly granted it to them. When Emperor Wuzong took the throne, he established seven imperial estates within a month; they later grew to more than three hundred. Princes and imperial in-laws petitioned for land and seized commoners' fields beyond counting.
22
世宗初，命給事中夏言等清核皇莊田。 言極言皇莊為厲於民。 自是正德以來投獻侵牟之地，頗有給還民者，而宦戚輩復中撓之。 戶部尚書孫交造皇莊新冊，額減於舊。 帝命核先年頃畝數以聞，改稱官地，不復名皇莊，詔所司徵銀解部。 然多為宦寺中飽，積逋至數十萬以為常。 是時，禁勳戚奏討、奸民投獻者，又革王府所請山場湖陂。 德王請齊、漢二庶人所遺東昌、兗州閒田，又請白雲等湖，山東巡撫邵錫按新令卻之，語甚切。 德王爭之數四，帝仍從部議，但存籓封初請莊田。 其後有奏請者不聽。
Early in the Jiajing reign, the emperor ordered Supervising Secretaries Xia Yan and others to audit imperial estate lands. Xia Yan spoke forcefully of the harm imperial estates did to the people. From then on, much of the land presented and seized since the Zhengde era was returned to the people, but eunuchs and imperial in-laws again sabotaged the effort from within. Minister of Revenue Sun Jiao compiled a new register of imperial estates, with totals lower than before. The emperor ordered a verification of acreage from previous years, renamed the lands official property rather than imperial estates, and decreed that the responsible offices collect silver and remit it to the Ministry of Revenue. Yet eunuchs embezzled much of it, and arrears in the hundreds of thousands became routine. At that time, petitions from meritorious kin and illegal land presentations by schemers were banned, and princely requests for hills, lakes, and ponds were abolished. Prince De requested the abandoned lands in Dongchang and Yanzhou left by the two junior princes Qi and Han, and also asked for lakes such as Baiyun; Shandong governor Shao Xi rejected the requests under the new rules in sharply worded terms. Prince De disputed the matter four times, but the emperor still sided with the ministry, allowing only the manor lands originally granted at enfeoffment. After that, further petitions were not approved.
23
又定，凡公主、國公莊田，世遠者存什三。 嘉靖三十九年遣御史瀋陽清奪隱冒莊田萬六千餘頃。 穆宗從御史王廷瞻言，復定世次遞減之限：勳臣五世限田二百頃，戚畹七百頃至七十頃有差。 初，世宗時，承天六莊二湖地八千三百餘頃，領以中官，又聽校舍兼併，增八百八十頃，分為十二莊。 至是始領之有司，兼併者還民。 又著令宗室買田不輸役者沒官，皇親田俱令有司徵之，如勳臣例。 雖請乞不乏，而賜額有定，徵收有制，民害少衰止。
It was also decreed that for princess and state-duke manor lands, after many generations only three-tenths would be retained. In the thirty-ninth year of Jiajing, Censor Shen Yang was dispatched to recover more than 16,000 qing of concealed and falsely registered manor lands. Emperor Muzong followed Censor Wang Tingzhan's advice and re-established generational reduction limits: meritorious officials in the fifth generation were capped at 200 qing; imperial in-laws from 700 qing down to 70 qing depending on rank. Under the Jiajing emperor, Chengtian had six estates and two lakes totaling more than 8,300 qing, overseen by eunuchs; schools were also allowed to annex land, adding 880 qing and splitting the holdings into twelve estates. Now oversight passed to regular officials, and annexed lands were returned to the people. It was also decreed that any imperial clansman who bought land but evaded corvée labor would have it confiscated, and all imperial kin lands would be collected by regular officials on the same terms as meritorious officials. Although petitions never ceased, grants had fixed limits and collection followed rules, and the harm to the people eased somewhat.
24
神宗賚予過侈，求無不獲。 潞王、壽陽公主恩最渥。 而福王分封，括河南、山東、湖廣田為王莊，至四萬頃。 羣臣力爭，乃減其半。 王府官及諸閹丈地徵稅，旁午於道，扈養廝役廩食以萬計，漁斂慘毒不忍聞。 駕帖捕民，格殺莊佃，所在騷然。 給事中官應震、姚宗文等屢疏諫，皆不報。 時復更定勳戚莊田世次遞減法，視舊制稍寬。 其後應議減者，輒奉詔姑留，不能革也。 熹宗時，桂、惠、瑞三王及遂平、甯德二公主莊田，動以萬計，而魏忠賢一門，橫賜尤甚。 蓋中葉以後，莊田侵奪民業，與國相終云。
Emperor Shenzong's grants were extravagantly generous; no request went unfulfilled. Prince Lu and Princess Shouyang received the greatest favor. When Prince Fu was enfeoffed, land across Henan, Shandong, and Huguang was seized for princely estates, reaching forty thousand qing. Ministers protested vigorously, and the grant was cut in half. Princely officials and eunuchs measured land and collected taxes in an unending stream along the roads; retainers and servants numbered in the tens of thousands, and their extortion was so brutally cruel it was unbearable to hear. Imperial warrants were used to arrest commoners and kill estate tenants, and unrest broke out everywhere. Supervising Secretaries Guan Yingzhen, Yao Zongwen, and others memorialized repeatedly in protest, but received no response. At the time the generational reduction rules for meritorious kin manor lands were revised again, somewhat more lenient than before. Afterward, whenever reduction was debated, an edict would usually defer abolition—reform could never be carried through. Under the Tianqi emperor, the three princes Gui, Hui, and Rui and the two princesses Suiping and Ningde held manor lands reckoned in the tens of thousands of qing, while the clan of Wei Zhongxian received especially lavish grants. From the mid-dynasty onward, manor lands seized commoners' holdings—a scourge that lasted until the dynasty's end.

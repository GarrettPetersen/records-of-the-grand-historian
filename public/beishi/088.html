眭誇馮亮鄭修崔廓子賾徐則張文詡
Sui Kua, Feng Liang, Zheng Xiu, Cui Kuo, Cui Zhen, Xu Ze, and Zhang Wenshu
2
蓋兼濟獨善，顯晦之殊，其事不同，由來久矣。 昔夷、齊獲全于周武，華矞不容于太公，何哉？ 求其心者，許以激貪之用； 督其跡者，矯以教義之風。 而肥遁不歸，代有其人矣。 故《易》稱「遁世無悶」，「不事王侯」。 《詩》云「皎皎白駒，在彼空谷」。 《禮》云「儒有上不臣天子，下不事諸侯」。 《語》曰「舉逸民，天下之人歸心焉」。 雖出處殊途，語默異用，各言其志，皆君子之道也。
The paths of aiding the world at large and of perfecting oneself in solitude, and the contrast between public service and withdrawal, have never been the same; the distinction is ancient. In olden days Boyi and Shuqi came through unscathed under King Wu of Zhou, while Huayu found no welcome with the Grand Duke—why was that? For those who probed their inner motives, they were allowed a role in awakening the grasping. Those who examined their conduct shaped them with the breeze of righteous instruction. Yet men who withdrew completely and never came back—every age has had them. Hence the Book of Changes speaks of "withdrawing from the world without discontent" and of "not serving kings and lords." The Book of Songs says, "Bright, bright is the white colt, there in the empty valley." The Book of Rites says that some scholars "do not serve the Son of Heaven above, nor attend on feudal lords below." The Analects says, "Promote reclusive worthies, and the people of the realm will give you their hearts." Though whether they enter service or withdraw, speak out or keep silent, differs from man to man, each declares his own purpose; all alike belong to the way of the gentleman.
3
洪崖兆其始，箕山扇其風，七人作乎周年，四皓光乎漢日。 魏、晉以降，其流逾廣。 其大者則輕天下，細萬物； 其小者則安苦節，甘賤貧。 或與世同塵，隨波瀾以俱逝； 或違時矯俗，望江湖而獨往。 狎玩魚鳥，左右琴書，拾遺粒而織落毛，飲石泉而庇松柏。 放情宇宙之外，自足懷抱之中。 然皆欣欣於獨善，鮮汲汲於兼濟。 夷情得喪，忘懷累有。 比夫邁德弘道，匡俗庇人，可得而小，不可得而忽也。 而受命哲王，守文令主，莫不束帛交馳，蒲輪結轍，奔走岩穀，唯恐不逮者，何哉？ 以其道雖未弘，志不可奪，縱無舟楫之功，終有堅貞之操，足以立懦夫之志，息貪競之風。 與苟得之徒，不可同年共日，所謂無用以為用，無為而無不為也。
Hongya marked the beginning, Jishan spread the example, the Seven Worthies appeared in Zhou times, and the Four White-Heads shone in the Han. From the Wei and Jin dynasties onward, the stream of reclusion grew ever wider. The greater among them treated the realm lightly and regarded the myriad things as small; the lesser were content with austere principle and took humble poverty as their portion. Some mingled with the world and were carried off with its currents; others defied their times and set themselves apart, looking toward rivers and lakes as they went their own way. They delighted in fish and birds, kept lute and books close at hand, gathered stray grain and wove shed feathers, drank from stony springs, and took shelter under pine and cypress. They let their hearts roam beyond the universe and found contentment within themselves. Yet all rejoiced in perfecting themselves alone, and few were urgent to aid the world at large. They treated gain and loss alike and put aside the weight of worldly cares. Beside those who advance virtue, spread the Way, reform custom, and shelter the people, recluses may seem small in scale, yet they cannot be dismissed. Yet sage kings who received the Mandate and cultured rulers who upheld tradition all sent silken gifts in haste and rush-wheeled carriages in train, hurrying to cliffs and valleys lest they fail to win such men—why? Because though their Way had not been widely realized, their resolve could not be broken; even without the achievement of steering the state, their steadfast integrity was enough to stiffen cowards' hearts and quiet the wind of greedy rivalry. They cannot be mentioned in the same breath as those who seize whatever they can; this is what is meant by "using the useless as use" and "through non-action, nothing is left undone."
4
自叔世澆浮，淳風殆盡，錐刀之末，競入成群。 而能冥心物表，介然離俗，望古獨適，求友千齡，亦異人矣！ 何必御霞乘雲而追日月，窮極天地，始為超遠哉！
Since the late age, when manners grew thin and shallow and pure custom all but vanished, men scrambled in crowds over the smallest profit. Yet those who could fix their minds beyond the things of the world, stand wholly apart from custom, take antiquity alone as their measure, and seek companions a thousand years away—such men were extraordinary indeed! Why must one ride the clouds and chase the sun and moon, or exhaust heaven and earth, before being called truly remote from the world?

The Book of Wei lists Sui Kua, Feng Liang, Li Mi, and Zheng Xiu in its "Biographies of Retired Scholars." The Book of Sui lists Li Shiqian, Cui Kuo, Cui Zhen, Xu Ze, and Zhang Wenshu in its "Biographies of Recluses." Here Li Mi and Shiqian are placed with their family biographies; the rest are gathered in this chapter to complete the "Biographies of Recluses."

眭誇，一名旭，趙郡高邑人也。 祖邁，晉東海王越軍謀掾，後沒石勒，為徐州刺史。 父邃，字懷道，慕容寶中書令。 誇少有大度，不拘小節，耽好書傳，未曾以世務經心。 好飲酒，浩然物表。 年三十，遭父喪，須鬢致白，每一悲哭，聞者為之流涕。 高尚不仕，寄情丘壑。 同郡李順願與之交，誇拒而不許。 邦國少長莫不憚之。 少與崔浩為莫逆之交。 浩為司徒，奏徵為中郎，辭疾不赴。 州郡逼遣，不得已，入京都，與浩相見。 經留數日，唯飲酒談敘平生，不及世利。 浩每欲論屈之，竟不能發言，其見敬憚如此。 浩後遂投詔書於誇懷，亦不開口。 誇曰：「桃簡，卿已為司徒，何足以此勞國士也？ 吾便將別。 」桃簡，浩小名。 浩慮誇即還，時乘一騾，更無兼騎，乃以誇騾內之廄中，冀相維縶。 誇遂托鄉人輸租者，謬為御車，乃得出關。 浩知而歎曰：「眭誇獨行士，本不應以小職辱之，又使其人杖策復路，吾當何辭以謝也！ 」時朝法甚峻，誇既私還，將有私歸之咎。 浩仍相左右，始得無坐。 經年，送誇本騾，兼遺以所乘馬，為書謝之。 誇更不受其騾馬，亦不復書。 及浩沒，為之素服，受鄉人弔唁，經一時乃止。 歎曰：「崔公既死，誰能更容眭誇！ 」婦父巨鹿魏攀，當時名達之士，未嘗備婿之禮，情同朋好。 或人謂誇曰：「吾聞有大才者必居貴仕，子何獨在桑榆乎？ 」遂著《知命論》以釋之。 及卒，葬日赴會者如市。 無子。
Sui Kua, also called Xu, was from Gaoyi in Zhao commandery. His grandfather Mai served as military planning aide to Jin's Prince of Donghai, Sima Yue; later he submitted to Shi Le and became inspector of Xuzhou. His father Sui, styled Huaidao, was director of the Secretariat under Murong Bao. From youth Kua had a generous spirit, cared little for petty rules, loved books and records, and never troubled his mind with worldly affairs. He loved wine and lived with an expansive ease beyond the dust of the world. At thirty he lost his father; his beard and temples turned white, and whenever he wept in mourning those who heard him wept as well. Noble in spirit, he refused office and gave his heart to hills and valleys. Li Shun of the same commandery wished to befriend him, but Kua refused. Young and old throughout the district stood in awe of him. In youth he and Cui Hao were the closest of friends. When Hao became minister of works, he memorialized to summon Kua as gentleman of the palace, but Kua pleaded illness and declined. Pressed by provincial and commandery authorities, he had no choice but to enter the capital and meet Hao. He stayed several days, drinking and talking over old times, never touching worldly gain. Hao often wished to argue him into submission, yet could never bring himself to speak; such was the respect and awe Kua inspired. Later Hao thrust the imperial edict into Kua's bosom, but Kua still would not speak. Kua said, "Taojian, you are already minister of works—why trouble a man of the realm with such a thing? I am leaving now." "Taojian" was Hao's childhood name. Fearing Kua would leave at once—Kua had only a single mule and no spare mount—Hao stabled Kua's mule, hoping to keep him there. Kua then slipped out through the pass by posing as the driver for a fellow townsman delivering rent grain. When Hao learned of it he sighed and said, "Sui Kua is a man who walks his own path; he should never have been slighted with a petty post, and to send such a man whipping his way home—what apology can I possibly offer!" Court law was very strict at the time, and because Kua had returned without permission he faced punishment for unauthorized departure. Hao continued to shield him on every side, and only then did Kua escape punishment. A year later he returned Kua's mule and also sent the horse he had ridden, with a letter of apology. Kua again refused the mule and horse and sent no reply. When Hao died, Kua wore unadorned mourning for him and accepted condolences from fellow townspeople; only after a season did he cease. He sighed and said, "Now that Lord Cui is dead, who will still tolerate Sui Kua!" His father-in-law Wei Pan of Julu was a celebrated man of the day; he never kept the full rites owed a son-in-law, but treated Kua as a friend. Someone said to Kua, "I have heard that men of great talent always take high office—why do you alone linger in life's twilight?" He then wrote the "Treatise on Knowing Fate" to explain his choice. When he died, mourners at his burial filled the streets like a market crowd. He left no sons.

馮亮，字靈通，南陽人，梁平北將軍蔡道恭之甥也。 少博覽諸書，又篤好佛理。 隨道恭至義陽，會中山王英平義陽，獲焉。 英素聞其名，以禮待接。 亮性清靜，後隱居嵩山，感英之德，以時展覲。 英亡，亮奔赴，盡其哀慟。 宣武嘗召以為羽林監，領中書舍人，將令侍講《十地》諸經，固辭不許。 又欲使衣幘入見，苦求以幅巾就朝，遂不強逼。 還山數年，與僧禮誦為業，蔬食飲水，有終焉之志。 會逆人王敞事發，連山中沙門法。 而亮被執赴尚書省，十餘日，詔特免雪。 亮不敢還山，遂寓居景明寺，敕給衣食及其從者數人。 後思其舊居，復還山室。 亮既雅愛山水，又兼工思，結架岩林，甚得棲遊之適。 頗以此聞，宣武給其工力，令與沙門統僧暹、河南尹甄深等同視嵩山形勝之處，遂造閒居佛寺。 林泉既奇，營制又美，曲盡山居之妙。 亮時出京師。 延昌二年冬，因遇篤疾，宣武敕以馬輿送令還山，居嵩高道場寺，數日卒。 詔贈帛二百匹，以供凶事。
Feng Liang, styled Lingtong, was from Nanyang and nephew of Liang's general who pacified the north, Cai Daogong. In youth he read widely and was deeply devoted to Buddhist teaching. He followed Daogong to Yiyang; when Prince of Zhongshan Ying captured the city, Liang was taken prisoner. Ying had long heard of him and treated him with courtesy. Liang was by nature quiet and pure; later he withdrew to Mount Song, grateful for Ying's kindness, and visited him from time to time. When Ying died, Liang hurried to mourn him and grieved to the utmost. Emperor Xuanwu once summoned him as supervisor of the forest of feathers and concurrent attendant of the Secretariat, intending to have him lecture on the Ten Stages and other sutras at court, but he firmly refused. The emperor also wished him to appear in official cap and dress; Liang begged earnestly to attend court in a plain headcloth, and was not pressed further. Back in the mountains for several years, he devoted himself to ritual and scripture with the monks, lived on vegetables and water, and meant to end his days there. When the rebel Wang Chang's plot was exposed, the monks of the mountains were implicated as well. Liang was seized and sent to the Secretariat; after more than ten days an edict specially pardoned and cleared him. Liang dared not return to the mountains and lodged at Jingming Monastery, where an edict provided food and clothing for him and several attendants. Later, longing for his old home, he returned to his mountain dwelling. Liang had long loved mountains and waters and was also skilled in design; he built among cliffs and forests and found there the perfect ease of dwelling and wandering. Word of this spread; Emperor Xuanwu supplied workmen and had him, together with the chief of monks Seng Xian, intendant of Henan Zhen Shen, and others, survey Mount Song's finest sites, where they built the Hermitage Buddhist Monastery. The woods and springs were marvelous, the construction exquisite, capturing to the full the charm of life in the mountains. Liang sometimes came to the capital. In the winter of the second year of Yanchang he fell gravely ill; Emperor Xuanwu ordered him carried back to the mountains in a horse litter; he stayed at Songgao Daochang Monastery and died within a few days. An edict granted two hundred bolts of silk for the funeral.

遺誡兄子綜，殮以衣蒨，左手持板，右手執《孝經》一卷，置屍磐石上，去人數里外，積十餘日，乃焚於山，灰燼處，起佛塔經藏。 初、亮以盛冬喪，連日驟雪，窮山荒澗，鳥獸饑窘，僵屍山野，無所防護。 時有壽春道人惠需，每旦往看其屍，拂去塵霰。 禽蟲之跡，交橫左右，而初無侵毀。 衣服如本，唯風蒨巾。 又以亮識舊南方法師信大栗十枚，言期之將來十地果報，開亮手，以置把中。 經宿，乃為蟲鳥盜食，皮殼在地，而亦不傷肌體。 焚燎之日，有素霧蓊鬱，回繞其傍，自地屬天，彌朝不絕。 山中道俗營助者百餘人，莫不異焉。
He left instructions to his nephew Zong: dress him in plain reddish garments, place a tablet in his left hand and one volume of the Classic of Filial Piety in his right, set the body on a boulder several li from any dwelling, leave it for more than ten days, then burn it on the mountain; where the ashes fell, raise a stupa and a scripture repository. At first, because Liang died in deep winter and snow fell day after day in the remote mountains and desolate ravines, birds and beasts were starving; his stiff corpse lay in the wild hills with nothing to guard it. At the time the Shouyang Daoist Huixu came each morning to look upon the body and brush away dust and snow. The tracks of birds and insects crossed to and fro on either side, yet the body was not harmed. The garments were unchanged; only the reddish headcloth had been blown away. Because Liang had known the southern monk Xin, Xin sent ten large chestnuts, saying he looked to Liang's future reward of the Ten Stages; he opened Liang's hand and placed them in his grasp. After a night the chestnuts were eaten by insects and birds; only the shells lay on the ground, yet the body itself was not harmed. On the day of cremation a pale mist rose thick and lush, circling the pyre from earth to heaven and lasting the whole morning without cease. More than a hundred monks and laymen of the mountains who assisted in the rites all marveled at it.

鄭修，北海人也。 少隱于岐南凡穀中，依岩結宇，不交世俗，雅好經史，專意玄門。 前後州將，每徵不至。 岐州刺史魏蘭根頻遣致命，修不得已，暫出見蘭根，尋還山舍。 蘭根申表薦修，明帝詔付雍州刺史蕭寶夤訪實以聞。 會寶夤作逆，事不行。
Zheng Xiu was from Beihai. In youth he withdrew to Fangu south of Qi, built a dwelling against the cliffs, kept apart from the world, loved the classics and histories, and devoted himself to the study of the profound. Provincial governors summoned him repeatedly, but he never came. Wei Langen, inspector of Qi, sent urgent summons again and again; Xiu had no choice but to come out briefly to see him, then soon returned to his mountain dwelling. Langen submitted a memorial recommending Xiu; Emperor Ming ordered the inspector of Yong, Xiao Baoyin, to investigate and report. Just then Baoyin rebelled, and the matter came to nothing.

崔廓，字士玄，博陵安平人也。 父子元，齊燕州司馬。 廓少孤貧，母賤，由是不為邦族所齒。 初為裏佐，屢逢屈辱，於是感激，逃入山中。 遂博覽書籍，多所通涉，山東學者皆宗之。 既還鄉，不應辟命。 與趙郡李士謙為忘言友，時稱崔、李。 士謙死，廓哭之慟，為之作傳，輸之秘府。 士謙妻盧氏寡居，每家事，輒令人諮廓取定。 廓嘗著論言刑名之理，其義甚精，文多不載。 隋大業中，終於家。
Cui Kuo, styled Shixuan, was from Anping in Boling commandery. His father Ziyuan was administrator of Yan province under Qi. Kuo was orphaned and poor from youth; his mother was of humble birth, and for this reason the clan would not acknowledge him. He first served as a village aide, suffered repeated humiliations, and in a surge of feeling fled into the mountains. He then read widely and mastered many subjects, and scholars throughout the eastern provinces looked to him as their leader. After he returned home, he refused every official summons. He and Li Shiqian of Zhao commandery were friends who needed no words between them; people of the day spoke of them as Cui and Li. When Shiqian died, Kuo mourned him with deep grief, wrote his biography, and submitted it to the imperial secret archive. Shiqian's widow, Lady Lu, would send someone to consult Kuo whenever a household matter needed deciding. Kuo once wrote a treatise on penal law whose argument was exceedingly fine; most of the text is not preserved here. During the Daye reign of Sui, he died at home.

Early in the Kaihuang era, Prince Xia of Qin recommended him, and he placed at the top of the archery-examination. By imperial edict he joined the scholars in fixing ritual music; he was appointed collator, then promoted to director of harmonies. Su Wei, director of sacrifices, held him in high esteem. 昔漢氏西京，梁王建國，平臺東苑，慕義如林，馬卿辭武騎之官，枚乘罷弘農之守。 每覽史傳，嘗竊怪之：何乃脫略官榮，棲遲籓邸？ 以今望古，方知雅志。 彼二子者，豈徒然哉！ 足下博聞強記，鉤深致遠，視漢臣之三篋，似陟蒙山； 對梁相之五車，若吞雲夢。 吾兄欽賢重士，敬愛忘疲，先築郭隗之宮，常置穆生之醴。 今者重開土宇，更誓山河。 地方七百，牢籠曲阜； 城兼七十，包舉臨淄。 大啟南陽，方開東閤。 想得奉飛蓋，曳長裾，藉玳筵，躡珠履，歌山桂之偃蹇，賦池竹之檀欒。 其崇貴也如彼，其風流也如此，幸甚幸甚，何樂如之！ 高視上京，有懷德祖； 才謝天人，多慚子建。 書不盡意，寧俟繁辭。
In antiquity the Han had its Western Capital and the Prince of Liang founded his state; at the Terrace Garden and Eastern Park, men drawn to righteousness gathered like a forest—Sima Xiangru quit his post as armed escort, and Mei Cheng relinquished the governorship of Hongnong. Whenever I read the histories, I have privately marveled: why did they shrug off official honor and tarry at a prince's residence? Viewing the past from the present, one finally understands their exalted purpose. Those two men—were they truly without purpose? You are broad in learning and strong in memory, plumbing depths to reach what is distant—set beside the Han minister's three chests of books, it is like ascending Mount Meng; set before the Liang chancellor's five cartloads, it is as though one could swallow the marshes of Yun and Meng. My elder brother reveres the worthy and esteems scholars, honoring them until he forgets weariness—he first built a palace for Guo Kui and constantly set out the wine reserved for Mu Sheng. Now we reopen our fief and renew our oath over rivers and mountains. A domain of seven hundred li, enclosing Qufu; and seventy cities together, embracing Linzi. He throws wide the gates of Nanyang and newly opens the Eastern Lodge. I imagine you riding beneath flying canopies, trailing long robes, resting on tortoise-shell mats, treading pearl-toed slippers, singing of mountain cassia bending in the wind, and composing on the luxuriant bamboo by the pool. Such is his lofty dignity, such his refined grace—how exceedingly fortunate; what greater joy could there be! Looking up toward the upper capital, I cherish the memory of Dezhi; my talent falls short of heaven's chosen; I am deeply ashamed before Zijian. Words on paper cannot exhaust my meaning; I shall wait to say more.

一昨伏奉教書，榮貺非恆，心靈自失。 若乃理高《象系》，管輅思而不解； 事富《山海》，郭璞注而未詳。 至於五色相宣，八音繁會，鳳鳴不足喻，龍章莫之比。 吳劄之論《周頌》，詎盡揄揚； 郢客之奏《陽春》，誰能赴節？ 伏惟令王殿下，稟潤天潢，承輝日觀，雅道邁于東平，文藝高於北海。 漢則馬遷、蕭望，晉則裴楷、張華。 雞樹騰聲，鵷池播美，望我清塵，悠然路絕。
Only yesterday I humbly received your instructive letter; the grace shown me is beyond the ordinary, and my spirit has lost its footing. Were the reasoning as lofty as the Appended Phrases of the Book of Changes, Guan Lu might ponder it and still not understand; were the subject as rich as the Classic of Mountains and Seas, Guo Pu might annotate it and still leave it unclear. As for the five colors sounding in concert and the eight tones joining in harmony—phoenix song is too slight a likeness, and dragon pattern cannot match it. Wu Zha's discourse on the Odes of Zhou—how could it exhaust all praise? The man of Ying performing the Spring in Yang—who could keep the measure? Humbly considering Your Highness, you inherit the moisture of heaven's ford and bear the radiance of the sun-pinnacle; in refined learning you surpass the Duke of Dongping, and in literary craft you excel the Prince of Beihai. Under Han there were Sima Qian and Xiao Wang; under Jin, Pei Kai and Zhang Hua. The cock tree rings with fame, the egret pool spreads its luster—you gaze down on my humble dust from a road already far and still.

祖浚燕南贅客，河朔惰游，本無意于希顏，豈有心于慕藺。 未嘗聚螢映雪，懸頭刺股。 讀《論》唯取一篇，披《莊》不過盈尺。 況復桑榆漸暮，藜藿屢空，舉燭無成，穿楊盡棄。 但以燕求馬首，薛養雞鳴，謬齒鴻儀，虛班驥ＩＺ。 挾太山而超海，比報德而非難； 堙昆侖以為池，匹酬恩而反易。
Zujun is a tag-along guest south of Yan, an idle wanderer north of the Yellow River; I never had it in mind to emulate Yan Hui, nor any wish to admire Lin Xiangru. I never gathered fireflies to read by snowlight, nor hung my hair from a beam and pierced my thigh with an awl. In reading the Analects I take but a single chapter; in opening the Zhuangzi I do not pass a foot's breadth. Moreover, mulberry and elm draw on toward evening, thistle and bean broth are again empty, the lamp is raised without success, and the willow shot is wholly abandoned. Yet like one who sought a horse's head in Yan, or raised a cock to crow in Xue, I am wrongly counted among great ceremonies and falsely ranked among steeds renowned for fleet hooves. To hold Mount Tai and leap the sea would not be hard beside repaying your kindness; to dam Kunlun and make a pool would be easy beside returning your favor.

Suddenly the Zhou paulownia received its auspicious omen and the Tang waters sustained the house—at your gate stand generals and ministers, and your trees are fit for peach and plum. The true dragon is about to descend—who would still crave a name? The careless flute-player slips away first—what need for another hearing? Yet your kindly intent restrains and uplifts, diminishing the high and benefiting the low—therefore rivers and seas are called kings, and hillocks cannot match them. If Cao Zhi should chance to hear your lofty discourse, he would not lose his fair name; if Yang Xiu stood secretly in the lower wind, he likewise would not impair his pure virtue. In the fourth year of Daye he accompanied the emperor to the Fenyang Palace and halted at Heyang garrison. Wang Yan, magistrate of Lantian, obtained on Mount Lantian a jade figure three or four inches tall, dressed in a large-collared robe and wearing a kerchief-cap. He memorialized the court about it. The emperor questioned his ministers, but none could identify it. Zhen answered, "According to my investigation, before Emperor Wen of the Han there was no kerchief-cap—this must therefore have been made since Emperor Wen." "I have seen in the Record of the Songshan Shrine by Grand Steward Lu Yuanming of Wei the statement: 'There is a spirit being fashioned in jade, an image several inches long, who sometimes appears and sometimes hides; when he appears, the age is prolonged.'" "'Humbly considering that Your Majesty responds to Heaven and follows the people, having fixed the tripod at Song and Luo, the mountain god has revealed himself—I dare proclaim felicitation.'" Thereupon he bowed twice, and all the officials congratulated in turn. The Son of Heaven was greatly pleased and bestowed two hundred bolts of silk. On the progress to Mount Tai, the emperor asked Zhen, "Where is the Sheep's Gut Pass?" Zhen answered, "According to the Geography Monograph in the Book of Han, Huguan county in Shangdang has a Sheep's Gut Pass." The emperor said, "That is not the one." He answered again, "According to the Terrestrial Book by Huangfu Mi, ninety li north of Taiyuan there is a Sheep's Gut Pass." The emperor said, "That is the one." Thereupon he said to Niu Hong, "Cui Zujun is what people call 'ask one and know two.'"

In the fifth year he received an edict to compile with the scholars the Illustrated Gazetteer of the Realm in two hundred fifty volumes and presented it to the throne. The emperor was displeased with it and ordered Yu Shiji and Xu Shanxin to expand it into six hundred volumes. He left office to mourn his father but was soon recalled and ordered to resume his post. During the Liaodong campaign he was appointed eagle-bearer chief clerk. The names of the commanderies and districts established in Liaodong were all proposed by Zhen. By edict he composed the Record of the Eastern Campaign. In the ninth year he was appointed chief of staff to the Prince of Yue. At that time bandits in Shandong swarmed like bees; the emperor sent him to pacify Gaoyang and Xiangguo, and more than eight hundred who surrendered returned home. In the twelfth year he accompanied the imperial progress to Jiangdu. When Yuwen Huaji murdered the emperor, he appointed Zhen composition director, but Zhen pleaded illness and refused the post. On the journey he fell ill and died at Pengcheng at the age of sixty-nine.

賾與河南元善、河東柳䛒、太原王劭、吳興姚察、琅琊諸葛潁、信都劉焯、河間劉炫相善，每因休假，清談竟日。 所著詞、賦、碑、志十餘萬言，撰《洽聞志》七卷，《八代四科志》三十卷。 未及施行，江都傾覆，咸為煨燼。
Zhen was close to Yuan Shan of Henan, Liu Bing of Hedong, Wang Shao of Taiyuan, Yao Cha of Wuxing, Zhuge Ying of Langye, Liu Zhuo of Xindu, and Liu Xuan of Hejian; on every holiday they would hold pure conversation until the day ended. His writings in verse, rhapsody, inscription, and record totaled more than a hundred thousand characters; he also compiled the Record of Comprehensive Inquiry in seven scrolls and the Record of Four Categories Through Eight Dynasties in thirty scrolls. Before they could be put into use, Jiangdu fell and everything was consumed to ashes in the flames.

徐則，東海郯人也。 幼沈靜，寡嗜欲，受業于周弘正，善三玄，精於論議，聲擅都邑。 則歎曰：「名者實之賓，吾其為賓乎！ 」遂懷棲隱之操，杖策入縉雲山。 後學者數百人苦請教授，則謝而遣之。 不娶妻，常服巾褐。 陳太建中，應召來憩於至真觀。 期月，又辭入天台山。 因絕粒養性，所資唯松水而已，雖隆冬冱寒，不服綿絮。 太傅徐陵為之刊山立頌。
Xu Ze was from Tan in Donghai commandery. From youth he was withdrawn and quiet, with few desires; he studied under Zhou Hongzheng, mastered the Three Mysteries, excelled in debate, and his reputation filled the capital. Ze sighed and said, "Fame is the guest of reality—am I then to be the guest?" Thereupon he embraced the resolve to withdraw, took up his staff, and entered Mount Jinyun. Later several hundred scholars earnestly begged him to instruct them, but Ze refused and sent them away. He never married and always wore a cloth cap and brown robes. During the Taojian era of Chen he answered a summons and stayed for a time at the Zhizhen Abbey. After a month he again withdrew and entered Mount Tiantai. Thereupon he abstained from grain to nourish his nature, sustaining himself only on pine-infused water; even in the depth of winter's cold he wore no cotton quilt. The grand tutor Xu Ling had a stele carved on the mountain and composed a eulogy for him.

初在縉雲山，太極真人徐君降之曰：「汝年出八十，當為王者師，然後得道也。 」晉王廣鎮揚州，聞其名，手書召之曰：「夫道得眾妙，法體自然，包涵二儀，混成萬物，人能弘道，道不虛行。 先生履德養空，宗玄齊物，深曉義理，頗味法門。 悅性沖玄，恬神虛白，餐松餌術，棲息煙霞。 望赤城而待風雲，遊玉堂而駕龍鳳。 雖復藏名台嶽，猶且騰實江、淮。 藉甚嘉猷，有勞寤寐。 欽承素道，久積虛襟，側席幽人，夢想岩穴。 霜風已冷，海氣將寒，偃息茂林，道體休悆。 昔商山四皓，輕舉漢庭； 淮南八公，來儀籓邸。 古今雖異，山谷不殊。 市朝之隱，前賢已說。 導凡述聖。 非先生而誰？ 故遣使人，往彼延請，想無勞東帛，賁然來思，不待蒲輪，去彼空穀。 希能屈己，佇望披云。 」則謂門人曰：「吾今年八十一，王來召我，徐君之旨，信而不征。 」於是遂詣揚州。 晉王將請受道法，則辭以時日不便。 其後夕中，命待者取香火，如平常朝禮之儀，至於五更而死。 支體柔弱如生，停留數旬，顏色不變。 晉王下書曰：「天台真隱東海徐先生，虛確居宗，沖玄成德，齊物處外，檢行安身。 草褐蒲衣，餐松餌朮，棲隱靈岳，五十餘年。 卓矣仙才，飄然騰氣，千尋萬頃，莫測其涯。 寡人欽承道風，久餐德素，頻遣使乎，遠此延屈，冀得虔受上法，式建良緣。 至止甫爾，未淹旬日，厭塵羽化，反真靈府。 身體柔軟，顏色不變，經方所謂屍解地仙者哉。 誠復師禮未申，而心許有在，雖忘怛化，猶愴於懷。 喪事所資，隨須供給。 霓裳羽蓋，既且騰雲； 空槨餘衣，詎藉墳壟？ 但杖舄在爾，可同俗法。 宜遣使人，送還天台定葬。」
When he was first on Mount Jinyun, the Transcendent Lord Xu descended to him and said, "When you pass eighty you will become a king's teacher; only then will you attain the Way." When Prince Jin held broad garrison at Yangzhou, hearing his fame he wrote in his own hand to summon him, saying, "The Way obtains the myriad subtleties; its law embodies nature; it embraces the Two Powers and blends to form the ten thousand things. When a man can enlarge the Way, the Way does not travel in vain." "Sir, you tread in virtue and nourish emptiness, revere mystery and equalize all things; you deeply understand the principles of meaning and have savored the gates of the dharma." "You delight in nature's pure mystery, keep your spirit tranquil, void, and white; you dine on pine and consume elixirs, dwelling among mist and rosy clouds." "You gaze toward Mount Chicheng awaiting wind and clouds, roam the Jade Hall borne by dragon and phoenix." "Though you hide your name on lofty mountain terraces, your worth still shines forth across the Jiang and Huai." "Your fine counsel is widely celebrated; it has long occupied my thoughts waking and sleeping." "I reverently honor the pure Way and have long kept an open heart; I sit with seat turned aside awaiting a recluse and dream of life in mountain caves." "The frost wind has turned cold and the sea air is growing chill; rest in the deep forest and let your body of the Way find its ease." "Long ago the Four Hoary Heads of Mount Shang readily entered the Han court;" "the Eight Lords of Huainan came to serve at a prince's residence." "Times may change, but mountains and valleys remain the same." "Sages of old already spoke of hiding in plain sight within the city." To guide the ordinary and expound the sacred. If not you, Master, then who? Therefore I send an envoy to invite you; I trust you need not trouble yourself with formal gifts—come forth splendidly at once; do not wait for a carriage of rush wheels to draw you from your empty valley. I hope you will condescend to come; I stand waiting for the clouds to part. " Xu Ze told his disciples, "I am eighty-one this year, and the Prince has summoned me; Lord Xu's prophecy has proved true." " With that he set out for Yangzhou. When Prince Jin was about to receive instruction in the Way, Ze declined, saying the time was not auspicious. That evening he had his attendants bring incense and performed the rites he usually observed at dawn; at the fifth watch he died. His body remained supple as in life; he lay for several weeks, and his complexion did not change. Prince Jin issued a proclamation: "The true recluse of Tiantai, Master Xu of Donghai, took emptiness and certainty as his foundation, subtle mystery as his completed virtue, treated all things as equal while dwelling apart from the world, and disciplined his conduct to preserve his person. Clad in grass and rush, feeding on pine and living on atractylodes, he dwelt hidden on sacred mountains for more than fifty years. His transcendent gifts were extraordinary, his spirit lofty and free; like depths of a thousand fathoms or breadth of ten thousand leagues, none could fathom his limits. I have long reverenced the wind of the Way and nourished myself on your pure virtue; again and again I sent envoys to invite you from afar, hoping devoutly to receive the supreme teaching and establish a worthy bond. He had only just arrived, less than ten days ago, when he cast off the dusty world, ascended in transformation, and returned to the true realm of spirit. His body remained soft and his complexion unchanged—is this not what the medical classics call corpse liberation and earthly immortality? Though the rites of discipleship were never completed, my heart had already pledged itself to him; though I should rejoice in his transformation, sorrow still fills my breast. Provide whatever his funeral requires. Rainbow robes and feathered canopies—he has already ascended on clouds; an empty coffin and leftover garments—what need has he of mound and tomb? Only his staff and shoes remain, to show conformity with ordinary custom. Send envoys to return him to Mount Tiantai for proper burial."

At that time, from Jiangdu to Mount Tiantai, travelers on the road often saw Ze walking on foot, saying he had been released to return. When he reached his old dwelling, he took his scriptures and Daoist texts and distributed them among his disciples; he had a room swept clean and said, "If guests come, receive them here. 25
張文詡，河東人也。 父琚，開皇中，為洹水令，以清正聞。 文詡博覽群書，特精《三禮》。 隋文帝方引天下名儒碩學之士，文詡時游太學，博士房暉遠等莫不推伏之。 書侍御史皇甫誕，一時朝彥，恆執弟子之禮，以所乘馬就學邀屈。 文詡遂每牽馬步進，意在不因人自致也。 右僕射蘇威聞而召之，與語大悅，勸令從官，文詡固辭。 仁壽末，學廢，文詡策杖而歸，灌園為業。 州郡頻舉，皆不應命。 事母以孝聞。 每以德化人，鄉黨頗移風俗。 嘗有人夜中竊刈其麥者，見而避之。 盜因感悟，棄麥而謝。 文詡慰諭之，自誓不言，固令持去。 經數年，盜者向鄉人說之，始為遠近所悉。 鄰家築牆，心有不直，文詡因毀舊堵以應之。 文詡常有腰疾，會醫者自言善禁，文詡令禁之，遂為刀所傷，至於頓伏床枕。 醫者叩頭請罪。 文詡遽遣之，因為隱，謂妻子曰：「吾昨風眩，落坑所致。 」其掩人短，皆此類也。 州縣以其貧素，將加賑恤，輒辭不受。 嘗閒居無事，從容歎曰：「老冉冉而將至，恐修名之不立！ 」以如意擊幾自樂，皆有處所，時人方之閔子騫、原憲焉。 終於家，鄉人為立碑頌，號曰張先生。
Zhang Wenshu was from Hedong. His father Ju served as Magistrate of Huanshui during the Kaihuang era and was known for purity and uprightness. Wenshu read widely among the classics and was especially expert in the Three Rites. When Emperor Wen was gathering renowned scholars and eminent learned men from across the empire, Wenshu was at the Imperial Academy, and the erudite Fang Xianyuan and others all deferred to him. Huangfu Dan, Attending Secretary in the Imperial Library, was a celebrated figure of the court and always observed the rites of a disciple toward him; he would take the horse he rode and go to the academy to invite Wenshu to accompany him. Wenshu would always lead the horse and walk alongside, his intent being not to advance himself by relying on others. Su Wei, Right Vice Director of the Department of State Affairs, heard of him and summoned him; after speaking with him he was greatly pleased and urged him to take office, but Wenshu firmly declined. At the end of the Renshou era the academy was abolished; Wenshu took up his staff and returned home, making irrigation and gardening his livelihood. The province and commandery repeatedly recommended him, but he never answered their summons. He became known for his filial devotion to his mother. He always transformed others through virtue, and his village and district gradually changed their ways. Once someone stole and reaped his wheat at night; Wenshu saw him and withdrew. The thief, moved by this, abandoned the wheat and apologized. Wenshu comforted him, swore himself not to speak of it, and firmly had him take the wheat away. After several years the thief told his fellow townspeople, and only then was the story known far and near. A neighbor built a wall that was not quite straight; Wenshu therefore tore down his old wall to match it. Wenshu once suffered from pain in his lower back; a physician who claimed skill in spell-binding treated him and wounded him with a blade, so that he collapsed on his bed. The physician kowtowed and begged forgiveness. Wenshu immediately sent him away and covered the matter for him, telling his wife and children, "Yesterday I had a dizzy spell and fell into a pit—that is what caused it. " His way of covering others' faults was all of this kind. The province and county, seeing that he was poor and plain, were about to offer relief and support, but he always declined and would not accept. Once at leisure with nothing to do, he sighed calmly and said, "Old age comes on gradually—I fear a cultivated name will not be established! " He would strike the armrest with his ruyi scepter for his own pleasure, each blow landing in its place; men of the time compared him to Min Ziqian and Yuan Xian. He died at home; his fellow townspeople erected a stele in praise of him and called him Master Zhang.

論曰：古之所謂隱逸者，非伏其身而不見也，非閉其言而不出也，非藏其智而不發也。 蓋以恬淡為心，不皦不昧，安時處順，與物無私者也。 眭誇忘懷纓冕，畢志丘園，或隱不違親，貞不絕俗； 或不教而勸，虛往實歸，非有自然純德，其孰能至此？ 然文詡見傷無慍，徐則志在沈冥，不可親疏，莫能貴賤，皆可謂抱樸之士矣。 崔廓感於屈辱，遂以肥遁見稱； 祖浚文籍之美，足以克隆堂構。 父子雖動靜殊方，其于成名一也，美哉！
Discussion: What the ancients called recluses was not those who hid their bodies from sight, sealed their words within, or buried their wisdom unspoken. They took tranquil detachment as their heart, neither dazzling nor obscure, settled peacefully in their age and circumstances, and harbored no selfish designs toward the world. Sui Kua set aside caps and robes of office and fulfilled his resolve among fields and gardens; some lived in reclusion without forsaking kin, remained steadfast without severing ties to the world; some taught without lecturing and drew others empty-handed yet sent them home enriched—without innate purity of virtue, who could attain such heights? Yet when Wenshu was harmed he showed no resentment, and Ze's mind dwelt in profound obscurity—neither intimacy nor distance could touch him, neither rank high nor low could define him; all may be called men who embrace unadorned simplicity. Cui Kuo, moved by insult, won fame through his resolute withdrawal from the world; Zu Jun's mastery of letters was sufficient to continue and magnify the family's inherited achievement.

眭誇馮亮鄭修崔廓子賾徐則張文詡
Sui Kua, Feng Liang, Zheng Xiu, Cui Kuo and his son Ze, Xu Ze, and Zhang Wensu
2
蓋兼濟獨善，顯晦之殊，其事不同，由來久矣。 昔夷、齊獲全于周武，華矞不容于太公，何哉？ 求其心者，許以激貪之用； 督其跡者，矯以教義之風。 而肥遁不歸，代有其人矣。 故《易》稱「遁世無悶」，「不事王侯」。 《詩》云「皎皎白駒，在彼空谷」。 《禮》云「儒有上不臣天子，下不事諸侯」。 《語》曰「舉逸民，天下之人歸心焉」。 雖出處殊途，語默異用，各言其志，皆君子之道也。
Serving the world at large and cultivating virtue in solitude, public engagement and withdrawal from the world—these are different paths, and the distinction is an old one. Long ago Boyi and Shuqi were spared by King Wu of Zhou, yet Hua Xu could not abide with the Grand Duke Jiang—what accounts for this? Those who probe their hearts regard them as useful for rousing the greedy; those who examine their conduct would reform them with the teaching of righteousness. Yet men who prosper in withdrawal and never return—every generation has known them. Hence the Classic of Changes speaks of "withdrawal from the world without discontent" and of "not serving kings and feudal lords." The Book of Odes has it: "Bright, bright the white colt, in that empty valley." The Book of Rites states that "the scholar does not serve the Son of Heaven above, nor the feudal lords below." The Analects teaches that when one "raises up reclusive subjects, all under Heaven will turn their hearts" to the ruler. Though their paths of engagement and withdrawal differ, and speech and silence serve different ends, each expresses his own intent—all alike belong to the way of the gentleman.
3
洪崖兆其始，箕山扇其風，七人作乎周年，四皓光乎漢日。 魏、晉以降，其流逾廣。 其大者則輕天下，細萬物； 其小者則安苦節，甘賤貧。 或與世同塵，隨波瀾以俱逝； 或違時矯俗，望江湖而獨往。 狎玩魚鳥，左右琴書，拾遺粒而織落毛，飲石泉而庇松柏。 放情宇宙之外，自足懷抱之中。 然皆欣欣於獨善，鮮汲汲於兼濟。 夷情得喪，忘懷累有。 比夫邁德弘道，匡俗庇人，可得而小，不可得而忽也。 而受命哲王，守文令主，莫不束帛交馳，蒲輪結轍，奔走岩穀，唯恐不逮者，何哉？ 以其道雖未弘，志不可奪，縱無舟楫之功，終有堅貞之操，足以立懦夫之志，息貪競之風。 與苟得之徒，不可同年共日，所謂無用以為用，無為而無不為也。
Hong Ya marked the beginning; Mount Ji spread the tradition; the Seven [recluses] appeared in the Zhou age; the Four Whiteheads of Mount Shang shone in Han times. From the Wei and Jin dynasties onward, the current grew ever broader. Those of grand cast disdained the realm and held all things lightly; those of lesser stature were content with austere integrity and gladly bore poverty. Some mixed with the world and drifted away with the current; others spurned their times, stood apart from convention, and turned toward rivers and lakes alone. They played with fish and birds, kept zither and books close at hand, gathered stray grains and wove shed feathers, drank from stone springs, and took shelter beneath pines and cypresses. They let their feelings roam beyond the cosmos and found sufficiency within their own hearts. Yet all rejoiced in solitary cultivation of virtue and were rarely eager to benefit the world at large. They were indifferent to gain and loss and free from burdens of care. Beside those who advance virtue, enlarge the Way, correct custom, and shelter the people, they may seem small, yet they cannot be dismissed. Yet sage kings who received the Mandate and cultured rulers who upheld the tradition—all sent silken gifts in swift succession and rushed wheel-rim carriages in crowded trains to mountain valleys, fearing they might not arrive in time. Why was this? Because although their Way had not yet been broadly realized, their resolve could not be broken; even without the achievement of ferrying others across, they possessed steadfast integrity enough to stiffen the spine of the timid and still the wind of greedy rivalry. They cannot be mentioned in the same breath as those who seize what they can—the very sense of "finding use in uselessness, accomplishing all through non-action."
4
自叔世澆浮，淳風殆盡，錐刀之末，競入成群。 而能冥心物表，介然離俗，望古獨適，求友千齡，亦異人矣！ 何必御霞乘雲而追日月，窮極天地，始為超遠哉！
Since the late age when manners grew shallow and pure custom nearly vanished, men scrambled in swarms over the merest trifle. Yet those who could still the heart beyond mere things, stand wholly apart from the vulgar world, look to antiquity in solitary accord, and seek companions a thousand years hence—they too were extraordinary men! Why must one ride the clouds and chase the sun and moon, exhausting heaven and earth, before one counts as surpassingly remote!

案《魏書》列眭誇、馮亮、李謐、鄭脩為《逸士傳》。 《隋書》列李士謙、崔廓、廓子賾、徐則、張文詡為《隱逸傳》。 今以李謐、士謙附其家傳，其餘並編附篇，以備《隱逸傳》云。
The Book of Wei records Sui Kua, Feng Liang, Li Mi, and Zheng Xiu in its "Biographies of Reclusive Gentlemen." The Book of Sui places Li Shiqian, Cui Kuo, Kuo's son Ze, Xu Ze, and Zhang Wensu in its "Biographies of Recluses." Here Li Mi and Shiqian are placed in their family biographies; the remainder are gathered in this chapter to complete the "Biographies of Recluses."

眭誇，一名旭，趙郡高邑人也。 祖邁，晉東海王越軍謀掾，後沒石勒，為徐州刺史。 父邃，字懷道，慕容寶中書令。 誇少有大度，不拘小節，耽好書傳，未曾以世務經心。 好飲酒，浩然物表。 年三十，遭父喪，須鬢致白，每一悲哭，聞者為之流涕。 高尚不仕，寄情丘壑。 同郡李順願與之交，誇拒而不許。 邦國少長莫不憚之。 少與崔浩為莫逆之交。 浩為司徒，奏徵為中郎，辭疾不赴。 州郡逼遣，不得已，入京都，與浩相見。 經留數日，唯飲酒談敘平生，不及世利。 浩每欲論屈之，竟不能發言，其見敬憚如此。 浩後遂投詔書於誇懷，亦不開口。 誇曰：「桃簡，卿已為司徒，何足以此勞國士也？ 吾便將別。 」桃簡，浩小名。 浩慮誇即還，時乘一騾，更無兼騎，乃以誇騾內之廄中，冀相維縶。 誇遂托鄉人輸租者，謬為御車，乃得出關。 浩知而歎曰：「眭誇獨行士，本不應以小職辱之，又使其人杖策復路，吾當何辭以謝也！ 」時朝法甚峻，誇既私還，將有私歸之咎。 浩仍相左右，始得無坐。 經年，送誇本騾，兼遺以所乘馬，為書謝之。 誇更不受其騾馬，亦不復書。 及浩沒，為之素服，受鄉人弔唁，經一時乃止。 歎曰：「崔公既死，誰能更容眭誇！ 」婦父巨鹿魏攀，當時名達之士，未嘗備婿之禮，情同朋好。 或人謂誇曰：「吾聞有大才者必居貴仕，子何獨在桑榆乎？ 」遂著《知命論》以釋之。 及卒，葬日赴會者如市。 無子。
Sui Kua, also called Xu, came from Gaoyi in Zhao commandery. His grandfather Mai had been military strategist to Prince Yue of Donghai under the Jin, then fell to Shi Le and became Inspector of Xuzhou. His father Sui, styled Huaidao, served Murong Bao as Director of the Secretariat. From youth Kua had a generous spirit and cared nothing for petty constraints; devoted to books and records, he never let worldly business weigh on his mind. He loved wine and lived with an expansive detachment beyond the world of affairs. At thirty he lost his father; his beard and temples turned white, and each time he wept in mourning those who heard him wept as well. Lofty in character, he would not take office and entrusted his heart to hills and valleys. Li Shun of the same commandery wished to befriend him, but Kua refused. Young and old throughout the region all stood in awe of him. In his youth he was sworn friends with Cui Hao. When Hao became Grand Tutor, he memorialized to summon Kua as Gentleman-in-Attendance, but Kua pleaded illness and declined. The province and commandery pressed him until he had no choice but to enter the capital and see Hao. He stayed several days, drinking wine and talking over their lives together, never touching worldly gain. Whenever Hao wished to argue and win him over, he could not bring himself to speak—such was the respect and awe Kua inspired. Later Hao tossed an imperial edict into Kua's bosom, but Kua still would not speak. Kua said, "Taojian, you are already Grand Tutor—why bother a man of the realm with such a thing? I shall take my leave now." " Taojian was Hao's childhood name. Hao, fearing Kua would leave at once—for he himself rode a single mule with no spare mount—stabled Kua's mule in his own stable, hoping to detain him. Kua then joined a fellow townsman delivering rent grain, passed himself off as the driver's assistant, and thus got through the pass. When Hao learned of it he sighed, "Sui Kua is a man who walks his own path; he should never have been insulted with a petty post, and to send him staff in hand back along the road—what words can I offer in apology? " At the time court law was severe; since Kua had returned without permission, he faced punishment for unauthorized departure. Hao still shielded him on both sides, and only then did Kua escape punishment. More than a year later he sent Kua his original mule along with the horse he himself had ridden, with a letter of apology. Kua would not accept the mule or horse and wrote no reply. When Hao died, Kua wore plain mourning for him and accepted condolences from fellow townsfolk; after a season he ceased. He sighed, "Now that Lord Cui is dead, who can still tolerate Sui Kua? " His father-in-law Wei Pan of Julu was a celebrated man of the day; he never observed the formal rites due a son-in-law, yet they were friends in spirit. Someone said to Kua, "I hear that men of great talent always hold high office—why do you alone linger in life's twilight? " Thereupon he wrote "On Knowing Fate" to explain himself. At his death, mourners at the funeral filled the streets like a market fair. He left no sons.

馮亮，字靈通，南陽人，梁平北將軍蔡道恭之甥也。 少博覽諸書，又篤好佛理。 隨道恭至義陽，會中山王英平義陽，獲焉。 英素聞其名，以禮待接。 亮性清靜，後隱居嵩山，感英之德，以時展覲。 英亡，亮奔赴，盡其哀慟。 宣武嘗召以為羽林監，領中書舍人，將令侍講《十地》諸經，固辭不許。 又欲使衣幘入見，苦求以幅巾就朝，遂不強逼。 還山數年，與僧禮誦為業，蔬食飲水，有終焉之志。 會逆人王敞事發，連山中沙門法。 而亮被執赴尚書省，十餘日，詔特免雪。 亮不敢還山，遂寓居景明寺，敕給衣食及其從者數人。 後思其舊居，復還山室。 亮既雅愛山水，又兼工思，結架岩林，甚得棲遊之適。 頗以此聞，宣武給其工力，令與沙門統僧暹、河南尹甄深等同視嵩山形勝之處，遂造閒居佛寺。 林泉既奇，營制又美，曲盡山居之妙。 亮時出京師。 延昌二年冬，因遇篤疾，宣武敕以馬輿送令還山，居嵩高道場寺，數日卒。 詔贈帛二百匹，以供凶事。
Feng Liang, styled Lingtong, came from Nanyang and was nephew to Cai Daogong, Liang's General Who Pacifies the North. In youth he read widely and was deeply devoted to Buddhist teaching. He followed Daogong to Yiyang; when Prince Ying of Zhongshan pacified Yiyang, Liang was taken captive. Ying had long known his reputation and treated him with courtesy. Liang was by nature serene; later he lived in seclusion on Mount Song, grateful for Ying's kindness, and visited him from time to time. When Ying died, Liang hastened to mourn him and grieved to the utmost. Emperor Xuanwu once summoned him as Supervisor of the Feathered Forest and concurrent Palace Gentleman of the Secretariat, intending to have him lecture on the Dasabhumi and other sutras, but Liang firmly refused. They also wished him to wear formal court dress for audience, but he earnestly begged to attend in a plain headcloth, and they did not press him. He returned to the mountain for several years, devoting himself to chanting with monks, eating only vegetables and drinking water, resolved to end his days there. When the rebel Wang Chang's plot was exposed, the mountain monks were implicated as well. Liang was seized and taken to the Ministry of the Interior; after more than ten days an edict specially cleared him. Liang dared not return to the mountain and lodged at Jingming Temple; an edict provided food and clothing for him and several attendants. Later, longing for his old home, he returned to his mountain dwelling. Liang loved landscape by nature and was skilled in design; he built among cliffs and forests and found there the perfect place to live and wander. Word of this spread; Xuanwu supplied laborers and ordered him, together with the Supervisor of Monks Sengxian, Intendant of Henan Zhen Shen, and others, to survey Mount Song's finest sites, where they built the Secluded Dwelling Buddhist Temple. The woods and springs were sublime, the construction exquisite—together they captured every charm of life in the mountains. Liang would sometimes visit the capital. In winter of the second year of Yanchang he fell gravely ill; Xuanwu ordered him carried back to the mountain in a horse litter; he stayed at Songgao Daochang Temple and died within days. An edict granted two hundred bolts of silk for the funeral.

遺誡兄子綜，殮以衣蒨，左手持板，右手執《孝經》一卷，置屍磐石上，去人數里外，積十餘日，乃焚於山，灰燼處，起佛塔經藏。 初、亮以盛冬喪，連日驟雪，窮山荒澗，鳥獸饑窘，僵屍山野，無所防護。 時有壽春道人惠需，每旦往看其屍，拂去塵霰。 禽蟲之跡，交橫左右，而初無侵毀。 衣服如本，唯風蒨巾。 又以亮識舊南方法師信大栗十枚，言期之將來十地果報，開亮手，以置把中。 經宿，乃為蟲鳥盜食，皮殼在地，而亦不傷肌體。 焚燎之日，有素霧蓊鬱，回繞其傍，自地屬天，彌朝不絕。 山中道俗營助者百餘人，莫不異焉。
He instructed his nephew Zong to enshroud him in plain hemp, place a tablet in his left hand and a scroll of the Classic of Filial Piety in his right, lay his body on a boulder several li from any dwelling, wait more than ten days, then burn him on the mountain; at the site of the ashes a pagoda and sutra repository were raised. At first, since Liang died in deep winter and snow fell day after day, in remote mountains and desolate ravines birds and beasts were starving; his stiff corpse lay in the wild hills, wholly unprotected. At the time the monk Huixu of Shouyang went each morning to view the corpse and brush away dust and snow. The tracks of birds and insects crisscrossed all around, yet nothing had harmed the body. His clothes were unchanged; only the hemp headcloth had been stirred by the wind. Moreover, because Liang had known the southern monk Xin, he placed ten large chestnuts in his hand, saying he awaited the future reward of the ten bodhisattva stages; he opened Liang's hand and laid them in his palm. After a night insects and birds had eaten them; the shells lay on the ground, yet his flesh was unharmed. On the day of the cremation, a white mist rose thick about him, circling from earth to heaven and lasting the whole morning. More than a hundred lay and monastic helpers on the mountain who attended the rites—all were astonished.

鄭修，北海人也。 少隱于岐南凡穀中，依岩結宇，不交世俗，雅好經史，專意玄門。 前後州將，每徵不至。 岐州刺史魏蘭根頻遣致命，修不得已，暫出見蘭根，尋還山舍。 蘭根申表薦修，明帝詔付雍州刺史蕭寶夤訪實以聞。 會寶夤作逆，事不行。
Zheng Xiu came from Beihai. In youth he lived in seclusion in Fangu Valley south of Qi, building his dwelling against the cliffs, shunning worldly contact; he loved the classics and histories and devoted himself to the arcane teachings. Successive provincial governors summoned him, but he never came. Wei Langen, Inspector of Qi, sent repeated urgent summons; Xiu had no choice but to emerge briefly to see him, then soon returned to his mountain dwelling. Langen memorialized recommending Xiu; Emperor Ming ordered Xiao Baoyin, Inspector of Yong, to investigate and report. But when Baoyin rebelled, the matter came to nothing.

崔廓，字士玄，博陵安平人也。 父子元，齊燕州司馬。 廓少孤貧，母賤，由是不為邦族所齒。 初為裏佐，屢逢屈辱，於是感激，逃入山中。 遂博覽書籍，多所通涉，山東學者皆宗之。 既還鄉，不應辟命。 與趙郡李士謙為忘言友，時稱崔、李。 士謙死，廓哭之慟，為之作傳，輸之秘府。 士謙妻盧氏寡居，每家事，輒令人諮廓取定。 廓嘗著論言刑名之理，其義甚精，文多不載。 隋大業中，終於家。
Cui Kuo, styled Shixuan, came from Anping in Boling commandery. His father Ziyuan had been Administrator of Yan province under the Qi. Kuo lost his father early and grew up poor; his mother was of humble birth, and for this the clan looked down on him. At first he served as a village aide and was repeatedly humiliated; stirred by this, he fled into the mountains. He then read widely across many books and gained broad mastery; scholars throughout the eastern provinces all looked to him as their leader. After he returned home, he declined all summons to office. He and Li Shiqian of Zhao commandery were friends who needed no words between them; their day knew them simply as Cui and Li. When Shiqian died, Kuo wept for him with deep grief, wrote his biography, and deposited it in the imperial archives. Shiqian's widow, Lady Lu, would send someone to consult Kuo on every household matter before deciding. Kuo once wrote a treatise on legal principles that was remarkably subtle; most of it is not reproduced here. During the Sui Daye era, he died at home.

His son Ze, styled Zujun, could compose prose by the age of seven. He was short in stature but gifted with eloquence. Early in the Kaihuang reign, Prince Xiao of Qin recommended him; he took top honors in the palace examination. The emperor ordered him to work with the Confucian scholars on fixing the court music; he was appointed Collator and later promoted to Music Harmonizer. Su Wei, Director of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices, held him in deep regard. He left office to mourn his mother; utterly filial by nature, he took neither food nor water for five days. 昔漢氏西京，梁王建國，平臺東苑，慕義如林，馬卿辭武騎之官，枚乘罷弘農之守。 每覽史傳，嘗竊怪之：何乃脫略官榮，棲遲籓邸？ 以今望古，方知雅志。 彼二子者，豈徒然哉！ 足下博聞強記，鉤深致遠，視漢臣之三篋，似陟蒙山； 對梁相之五車，若吞雲夢。 吾兄欽賢重士，敬愛忘疲，先築郭隗之宮，常置穆生之醴。 今者重開土宇，更誓山河。 地方七百，牢籠曲阜； 城兼七十，包舉臨淄。 大啟南陽，方開東閤。 想得奉飛蓋，曳長裾，藉玳筵，躡珠履，歌山桂之偃蹇，賦池竹之檀欒。 其崇貴也如彼，其風流也如此，幸甚幸甚，何樂如之！ 高視上京，有懷德祖； 才謝天人，多慚子建。 書不盡意，寧俟繁辭。
In the Western Han capital and in the realm of Prince Xiao of Liang, terraces and eastern parks drew men seeking patronage in throngs; Sima Xiangru gave up his Martial Cavalry post, and Mei Sheng resigned his Hongnong governorship. Reading the histories, I have often wondered privately: why did they spurn official honors and linger at a prince's mansion? Only by comparing past and present do I grasp their lofty purpose. Those two were no mere idlers! You are widely learned and deeply retentive, probing the profound and reaching far; beside the Han minister's three satchels of books, you seem to climb Mount Meng; and facing the Liang chancellor's five cartloads of books, you seem to swallow Lake Yunmeng whole. My elder brother reveres worthies and esteems scholars, honoring them without growing weary; he has built a hall in the manner of Guo Kui and keeps wine ready as for Mu Sheng. Our realm has been enlarged afresh and our bond with the land renewed. Our domain spans seven hundred li, embracing all that Qufu represents; our cities number seventy and more, taking in all that Linzi once held. We have opened wide the southern residence and are preparing the eastern pavilion for guests. I imagine you riding beneath the carriage canopy, trailing your long robe, seated on inlaid mats, shod in pearl-studded slippers, singing of the cassias on the hill and composing verses on the bamboos by the pool. To rise so high in honor and grace—to live with such elegance—what greater fortune could there be! Looking toward the imperial capital, I think of Yang Xiu; My talent falls far short of his, and I feel much ashamed before Cao Zhi. Words cannot exhaust what I feel; I will not weary you with more.

14
一昨伏奉教書，榮貺非恆，心靈自失。 若乃理高《象系》，管輅思而不解； 事富《山海》，郭璞注而未詳。 至於五色相宣，八音繁會，鳳鳴不足喻，龍章莫之比。 吳劄之論《周頌》，詎盡揄揚； 郢客之奏《陽春》，誰能赴節？ 伏惟令王殿下，稟潤天潢，承輝日觀，雅道邁于東平，文藝高於北海。 漢則馬遷、蕭望，晉則裴楷、張華。 雞樹騰聲，鵷池播美，望我清塵，悠然路絕。
Only yesterday I received your gracious letter; the honor you showed me was beyond ordinary measure, and I was quite overwhelmed. Reasoning higher than the Image and Attached texts of the Changes—Guan Lu would ponder it and fail to understand; Learning richer than the Classic of Mountains and Seas—Guo Pu would annotate it and still not exhaust it. As for harmonies of color and sound woven together—phoenix song is too slight a metaphor, dragon markings too poor a comparison. Wu Zha's praise of the Zhou hymns could scarcely do justice; and when a visitor from Ying played "Spring in Yang," who could keep the measure? Your Highness draws your grace from the imperial line and your radiance from the eastern peak; in refined conduct you surpass the Prince of Dongping, in literary art you exceed the Prince of Beihai. The Han had Sima Qian and Xiao Wangzhi; the Jin had Pei Kai and Zhang Hua. Your fame resounds in the court grove and spreads beside the imperial pool; gazing up at your brilliance, I find the path between us impossibly far.
15
祖浚燕南贅客，河朔惰游，本無意于希顏，豈有心于慕藺。 未嘗聚螢映雪，懸頭刺股。 讀《論》唯取一篇，披《莊》不過盈尺。 況復桑榆漸暮，藜藿屢空，舉燭無成，穿楊盡棄。 但以燕求馬首，薛養雞鳴，謬齒鴻儀，虛班驥ＩＺ。 挾太山而超海，比報德而非難； 堙昆侖以為池，匹酬恩而反易。
I am but a stray guest from south of Yan, an idler drifting through the north—never minded to emulate Yan Hui, never dreamed of rivaling Lin Xiangru. I never burned fireflies to read by winter light or stuck needles in my thigh to stay awake. Of the Analects I know but one chapter; of Zhuangzi I have read barely a scroll's length. My years advance like the sun toward the mulberry trees; my pantry stands empty; I cannot light a candle to any purpose, nor hit the willow leaf with my bow. Yet as Yan sought the steed's head and Xue kept the cock to greet the dawn, I have wrongly been counted among your grand retainers and given a place on the roster of fine steeds. To shoulder Mount Tai and cross the sea would be easier than repaying your kindness; yet damming Kunlun to make a pond would prove harder than rewarding your grace—mine is the easy part.
16
忽屬周桐錫瑞，唐水承家，門有將相，樹宜桃李。 真龍將下，誰好有名； 濫吹先逃，何須別聽。 但慈旨抑揚，損上益下，江海所以稱王，丘陵為之不逮。 曹植儻豫聞高論，則不殞令名； 楊脩若竊在下風，亦詎虧淳德。 無任荷戴之至，謹奉啟以聞。
Suddenly Heaven has blessed the Zhou parasol-tree and the Tang waters flow to your house; generals and ministers grace your gate, and peach and plum should flourish on your line. The true dragon is about to descend—who would cling to empty fame? The impostor in the orchestra would flee first—why should you seek another musician? Only your gracious words, raising the low and tempering the high, make rivers and seas king over hills that cannot rival them. Had Cao Zhi heard your lofty discourse, his fair name would not have perished; and had Yang Xiu stood in your lee, his pure virtue would not have suffered. I cannot express how deeply I am obliged; I respectfully submit this in reply.
17
大業四年，從駕汾陽宮，次河陽鎮。 藍田令王曇于藍田山得一玉人，長三四寸，著大領衣，冠幘。 奏之。 詔問群臣，莫有識者。 賾答曰：「謹案：漢文帝已前，未有冠幘，即是文帝以來所制也。 臣見魏大司農盧元明撰《嵩高山廟記》云：『有神人，以玉為形，像長數寸，或出或隱，出則令世延長。 』伏惟陛下，應天順人，定鼎嵩、雒，嶽神自見，臣敢稱慶。 」因再拜，百官畢賀。 天子大悅，賜縑二百匹。 從駕往太山，詔問賾曰：「何處有羊腸阪？ 」賾答曰：「臣案《漢書·地理志》，上党壺關縣有羊腸阪。 」帝曰：「不是。 」又答曰：「臣案皇甫士安撰《地書》，云太原北九十里，有羊腸阪。 」帝曰：「是也。 」因謂牛弘曰：「崔祖浚所謂問一知二。」
In the fourth year of Daye, he accompanied the emperor to Fenyang Palace and halted at Heyang Station. Wang Tan, Magistrate of Lantian, found in Mount Lantian a jade figure three or four inches tall, dressed in broad-collared robes and wearing a cap with turban. He memorialized the court about it. The emperor questioned his ministers, but none could identify it. Ze replied: "Upon investigation, no caps with turbans appear before Emperor Wen of Han; this style must date from his reign onward." I have seen in the Mount Song Temple Record by Lu Yuanming, Grand Minister of Agriculture under Wei: 'A divine being shaped in jade, several inches tall, appears and vanishes; when it appears, the age is prolonged.' Your Majesty has answered Heaven and won the people's hearts, established the throne at Song and Luo—and now the mountain spirit has revealed itself. I venture to offer my congratulations. He bowed twice, and all the officials joined in congratulation. The emperor was greatly pleased and granted him two hundred bolts of silk. On the journey to Mount Tai, the emperor asked Ze: "Where is Sheep's Intestines Slope?" Ze replied: "According to the Treatise on Geography in the Book of Han, Huguan county in Shangdang has such a slope." The emperor said: "That is not the one." Ze answered again: "In Huangfu Mi's Book of the Earth it says that ninety li north of Taiyuan there is a Sheep's Intestines Slope." The emperor said: "That is the one." He then said to Niu Hong: "Cui Zujun is the man of whom they say, 'ask one thing and he knows two.'"
18
五年，受詔與諸儒撰《區宇圖志》二百五十卷，奏之。 帝不善之，更令虞世基、許善心演為六百卷。 以父憂去職，尋起令視事。 遼東之役，授鷹揚長史。 置遼東郡縣名，皆賾之議也。 奉詔作《東征記》。 九年，除越王長史。 于時山東盜賊蜂起，帝令撫慰高陽、襄國，歸首者八百餘人。 十二年，從駕江都。 宇文化及之弑帝也，引為著作郎，稱疾不起。 在路發疾，卒于彭城，年六十九。
In the fifth year he was ordered to compile with the scholars the Territorial Gazetteer in two hundred fifty volumes and presented it to the throne. The emperor was displeased with it and had Yu Shiji and Xu Shansin expand it to six hundred volumes. He left office to mourn his father but was soon recalled to resume his duties. During the Liaodong campaign he was made Chief Clerk of the Hawk-Flying Army. The names given to the commanderies and counties in Liaodong were all Ze's proposals. By imperial order he wrote the Record of the Eastern Expedition. In the ninth year he was appointed Chief Clerk to the Prince of Yue. When bandits rose throughout the eastern provinces, the emperor sent him to pacify Gaoyang and Xiangguo; more than eight hundred surrendered. In the twelfth year he accompanied the emperor to Jiangdu. After Yuwen Huaji murdered the emperor, Ze was offered the post of Composition Draftsman but pleaded illness and refused to serve. He fell ill on the road and died at Pengcheng at the age of sixty-nine.
19
賾與河南元善、河東柳䛒、太原王劭、吳興姚察、琅琊諸葛潁、信都劉焯、河間劉炫相善，每因休假，清談竟日。 所著詞、賦、碑、志十餘萬言，撰《洽聞志》七卷，《八代四科志》三十卷。 未及施行，江都傾覆，咸為煨燼。
Ze was close friends with Yuan Shan of Henan, Liu Bian of Hedong, Wang Shao of Taiyuan, Yao Cha of Wuxing, Zhuge Ying of Langye, Liu Zhuo of Xindu, and Liu Xuan of Hejian; on their days off they would talk together from morning till night. He wrote more than one hundred thousand words in lyrics, rhapsodies, steles, and memorials, and compiled Comprehensive Accounts in seven volumes and Records of Eight Dynasties and Four Categories in thirty volumes. Before any could be published, Jiangdu fell; all were burned to ash.
20
徐則，東海郯人也。 幼沈靜，寡嗜欲，受業于周弘正，善三玄，精於論議，聲擅都邑。 則歎曰：「名者實之賓，吾其為賓乎！ 」遂懷棲隱之操，杖策入縉雲山。 後學者數百人苦請教授，則謝而遣之。 不娶妻，常服巾褐。 陳太建中，應召來憩於至真觀。 期月，又辭入天台山。 因絕粒養性，所資唯松水而已，雖隆冬冱寒，不服綿絮。 太傅徐陵為之刊山立頌。
Xu Ze came from Tan in Donghai commandery. From youth he was calm and reserved, with few desires; he studied under Zhou Hongzheng, mastered the Three Profundities, and excelled in debate; his renown filled the capital. Xu Ze sighed and said, "Fame is the guest of substance—am I to be the guest? With that he resolved to withdraw from the world and, staff in hand, made his way into Mount Jinyun." Later hundreds of students begged him to teach, but he refused and sent them away. He never married and always wore a scholar's cap and coarse hemp robes. During the Chen Taijian era he answered a summons and stayed at the Abbey of Perfect Truth. After a month he withdrew again into Mount Tiantai. He lived on pine nuts and spring water alone; even in the depths of winter he wore no quilted clothes. Grand Tutor Xu Ling carved an inscription on the mountain and erected a eulogy in his honor.
21
初在縉雲山，太極真人徐君降之曰：「汝年出八十，當為王者師，然後得道也。 」晉王廣鎮揚州，聞其名，手書召之曰：「夫道得眾妙，法體自然，包涵二儀，混成萬物，人能弘道，道不虛行。 先生履德養空，宗玄齊物，深曉義理，頗味法門。 悅性沖玄，恬神虛白，餐松餌術，棲息煙霞。 望赤城而待風雲，遊玉堂而駕龍鳳。 雖復藏名台嶽，猶且騰實江、淮。 藉甚嘉猷，有勞寤寐。 欽承素道，久積虛襟，側席幽人，夢想岩穴。 霜風已冷，海氣將寒，偃息茂林，道體休悆。 昔商山四皓，輕舉漢庭； 淮南八公，來儀籓邸。 古今雖異，山谷不殊。 市朝之隱，前賢已說。 導凡述聖。 非先生而誰？ 故遣使人，往彼延請，想無勞東帛，賁然來思，不待蒲輪，去彼空穀。 希能屈己，佇望披云。 」則謂門人曰：「吾今年八十一，王來召我，徐君之旨，信而不征。 」於是遂詣揚州。 晉王將請受道法，則辭以時日不便。 其後夕中，命待者取香火，如平常朝禮之儀，至於五更而死。 支體柔弱如生，停留數旬，顏色不變。 晉王下書曰：「天台真隱東海徐先生，虛確居宗，沖玄成德，齊物處外，檢行安身。 草褐蒲衣，餐松餌朮，棲隱靈岳，五十餘年。 卓矣仙才，飄然騰氣，千尋萬頃，莫測其涯。 寡人欽承道風，久餐德素，頻遣使乎，遠此延屈，冀得虔受上法，式建良緣。 至止甫爾，未淹旬日，厭塵羽化，反真靈府。 身體柔軟，顏色不變，經方所謂屍解地仙者哉。 誠復師禮未申，而心許有在，雖忘怛化，猶愴於懷。 喪事所資，隨須供給。 霓裳羽蓋，既且騰雲； 空槨餘衣，詎藉墳壟？ 但杖舄在爾，可同俗法。 宜遣使人，送還天台定葬。」
While he was on Mount Jinyun, the Perfected Lord Xu of the Grand Ultimate appeared to him and said, "When you pass eighty, you shall become a king's teacher—only then will you attain the Way." When Yang Guang, Prince of Jin, was stationed at Yangzhou and heard of him, he wrote a personal summons: "The Way gathers all mysteries; its law embodies nature itself, embracing heaven and earth and shaping the ten thousand things. A man can enlarge the Way—the Way does not walk in emptiness." You tread the path of virtue and cultivate inner stillness; you hold to the profound and see all things as one; you understand principle deeply and have savored the teachings of the Law. Your nature delights in purity; your spirit rests in tranquil clarity; you nourish yourself on pines and live by immortal arts, dwelling at ease among mountain mists and rosy clouds. You gaze toward Mount Chicheng awaiting the wind and clouds of destiny; you wander the Jade Hall riding dragon and phoenix. Though you hide your name among sacred peaks, your fame still ripens across the Jiang and Huai. Your wise counsel is greatly to be relied upon—it haunts our waking and our dreams. I have long revered the pure Way and kept an open heart; with mat turned sidewise I await a recluse, dreaming of those who dwell in mountain caves. The frost wind has turned cold and the sea air will soon bite—rest at ease in the deep forest; let your Way-honed body find its peace. Long ago the Four Hoary Heads of Mount Shangshan lightly took their place at the Han court; The Eight Gentlemen of Huainan came in ceremony to a prince's domain. Past and present may differ, but valley and peak call alike. The sage who hides within the bustle of court and market—former sages have already described him. To guide the common and expound the sage. Who but you could do this? Therefore I send messengers to invite you in person. I trust you need no silken gifts to come forth with shining grace, nor wait for a carriage with rush wheels to leave your empty vale. I hope you will humble yourself—I stand waiting for you to part the clouds and appear. Xu Ze said to his disciples, "I am eighty-one this year. The prince has summoned me. Lord Xu's prophecy is true, though the summons is not yet fulfilled." With that he went to Yangzhou. When the Prince of Jin was about to request instruction in the Way, Ze declined on the grounds that the day was not auspicious. That night he ordered his attendants to bring incense and perform his usual morning rites; by the fifth watch he was dead. His body remained soft as if alive; for several weeks his complexion did not change. The Prince of Jin issued a proclamation: "The true recluse of Tiantai, Lord Xu of Donghai—serene and steadfast as his faith, profound in virtue, treating all things as one while standing apart, governing his conduct to settle his soul. In hemp and rush-cloth robes he fed on pine nuts and atractylodes, dwelling on sacred peaks for more than fifty years. A transcendent spirit, loftier than measure, rising on powers none can fathom. I have long revered your Way and savored your virtue, sending envoys again and again to bring you here, hoping to receive your highest teachings and forge a noble bond. He had barely arrived, less than ten days, when weary of the dusty world he ascended, returning to the realm of true spirits. His body soft, his face unchanged—is this not what the scriptures call corpse liberation, an earthly immortal? Though I never completed the rites of discipleship, my heart had already pledged itself; though I know he has passed beyond sorrow, grief yet fills my breast. Provide whatever his funeral requires. Clad in rainbow robes beneath a canopy of feathers, he has already risen on clouds; What need has an empty coffin or leftover garments for an earthly mound? Only his staff and shoes remain—bury these according to common custom. Send envoys to escort his remains to Tiantai for burial there."
22
是時，自江都至天台，在道多見則徒步，云得放還。 至其舊居，取經書道法，分遣弟子，仍令淨掃一房，曰：「若有客至，宜延之於此。 」然後跨石梁而去，不知所之。 須臾屍柩至，知其靈化，時年八十二。 晉王聞而益異之，賵物千段，遣畫工圖其狀，令柳{巧言}為之贊。
At that time, along the road from Jiangdu to Tiantai, many reported seeing Xu Ze walking on foot, saying he had been freed to go home. He reached his old dwelling, gathered his scriptures and Daoist texts, distributed them among his disciples, and had a room swept clean, saying, "If guests should come, receive them here. Then he crossed the Stone Bridge and went away—none knew where. Presently his coffin arrived, and they knew his spirit had transformed; he was eighty-two. The Prince of Jin, hearing this, was all the more amazed; he sent a thousand lengths of funeral gifts, had painters depict Xu Ze's likeness, and commissioned Liu Jian to write an encomium.
23
時有建安宋玉泉、會稽孔道茂、丹陽王遠知等，亦行辟穀道，以松水自給，皆為煬帝所重。
At that time Song Yuquan of Jian'an, Kong Daomao of Kuaiji, Wang Yuanzhi of Danyang, and others also practiced fasting from grain, living on pine nuts and spring water, and all were held in high regard by Emperor Yang.
24
張文詡
Zhang Wensu
25
張文詡，河東人也。 父琚，開皇中，為洹水令，以清正聞。 文詡博覽群書，特精《三禮》。 隋文帝方引天下名儒碩學之士，文詡時游太學，博士房暉遠等莫不推伏之。 書侍御史皇甫誕，一時朝彥，恆執弟子之禮，以所乘馬就學邀屈。 文詡遂每牽馬步進，意在不因人自致也。 右僕射蘇威聞而召之，與語大悅，勸令從官，文詡固辭。 仁壽末，學廢，文詡策杖而歸，灌園為業。 州郡頻舉，皆不應命。 事母以孝聞。 每以德化人，鄉黨頗移風俗。 嘗有人夜中竊刈其麥者，見而避之。 盜因感悟，棄麥而謝。 文詡慰諭之，自誓不言，固令持去。 經數年，盜者向鄉人說之，始為遠近所悉。 鄰家築牆，心有不直，文詡因毀舊堵以應之。 文詡常有腰疾，會醫者自言善禁，文詡令禁之，遂為刀所傷，至於頓伏床枕。 醫者叩頭請罪。 文詡遽遣之，因為隱，謂妻子曰：「吾昨風眩，落坑所致。 」其掩人短，皆此類也。 州縣以其貧素，將加賑恤，輒辭不受。 嘗閒居無事，從容歎曰：「老冉冉而將至，恐修名之不立！ 」以如意擊幾自樂，皆有處所，時人方之閔子騫、原憲焉。 終於家，鄉人為立碑頌，號曰張先生。
Zhang Wensu was from Hedong. His father Ju served as Magistrate of Huanshui during the Kaihuang era and was known for integrity. Wensu read widely and was especially masterful in the Three Rites. As Emperor Wen was gathering renowned scholars from across the realm, Wensu studied at the Imperial Academy, and erudites such as Fang Xianyuan all deferred to him. Huangfu Dan, Censor-in-Chief, one of the leading men at court, always treated him as his teacher and came on horseback to invite him to lectures. Wensu would walk alongside, leading the horse himself, unwilling to gain distinction through another man's patronage. When Su Wei, Vice Director of the Right, heard of him and summoned him, he found their conversation delightful and urged Wensu to enter government service, but Wensu firmly refused. When the academy closed at the end of the Renshou era, Wensu took his staff and returned home to tend his garden. The provinces repeatedly recommended him, but he never answered their summons. He was famed for filial devotion to his mother. He continually won people over through virtue, and his village gradually changed its ways. Once, when someone stole into his fields at night to cut his wheat, Wensu saw him and stepped aside. The thief was moved to repentance, abandoned the wheat, and apologized. Wensu comforted him, vowed never to speak of it, and insisted he take the wheat away. Only years later did the thief tell his neighbors, and then the story became known far and wide. When a neighbor built a wall that encroached on his line, Wensu tore down his own old wall to align with it. Wensu suffered from a chronic back ailment. A physician who claimed skill in spirit-healing was summoned; during the treatment Wensu was cut by a knife and collapsed upon his bed. The physician kowtowed in apology. Wensu hurried him away and concealed the matter, telling his wife, "I had a dizzy spell yesterday and fell into a ditch. Such was his way of shielding others' faults. When the local authorities wished to grant him relief because of his poverty, he always refused. Once in quiet leisure he sighed and said, "Old age creeps upon me—I fear my good name may never be made! He would tap his armrest with a ruyi in quiet contentment, each gesture measured and sure; men of the day compared him to Min Ziqian and Yuan Xian. He died at home; his neighbors erected a memorial stele to praise him, addressing him as Master Zhang.
26
論曰：古之所謂隱逸者，非伏其身而不見也，非閉其言而不出也，非藏其智而不發也。 蓋以恬淡為心，不皦不昧，安時處順，與物無私者也。 眭誇忘懷纓冕，畢志丘園，或隱不違親，貞不絕俗； 或不教而勸，虛往實歸，非有自然純德，其孰能至此？ 然文詡見傷無慍，徐則志在沈冥，不可親疏，莫能貴賤，皆可謂抱樸之士矣。 崔廓感於屈辱，遂以肥遁見稱； 祖浚文籍之美，足以克隆堂構。 父子雖動靜殊方，其于成名一也，美哉！
The historians remark: what the ancients called a recluse was not one who hid his body from sight, sealed his lips, or buried his wisdom. Rather, he keeps a tranquil heart, neither ostentatious nor obscure, rests in his time and accords with circumstance, and harbors no selfishness toward the world. Sui Kua forgot rank and office and found his fulfillment in gardens and hills; some hid without abandoning family duty, others kept faith without cutting themselves off from the world; Or won men without preaching and sent them home enriched—who but a man of pure inborn virtue could achieve such ends? Zhang Wensu, injured yet without resentment, and Xu Ze, whose purpose lay in deepest obscurity, alike beyond favor or neglect, beyond high or low esteem—both may be called men who embrace simplicity. Cui Kuo, stung by humiliation, won fame as one who made a stately withdrawal; While his son Zujun's mastery of letters was enough to sustain the family's standing. Father and son took different paths, yet each won renown in his own way—how admirable!

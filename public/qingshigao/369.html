林則徐，字少穆，福建侯官人。 少警敏，有異才。 年二十，舉鄉試。 巡撫張師誠闢佐幕。 嘉慶十六年進士，選庶吉士，授編修。 歷典江西、雲南鄉試，分校會試。 遷御史，疏論福建閩安副將張寶以海盜投誠，宜示裁抑，以防驕蹇，被嘉納。 未幾，出為杭嘉湖道，修海塘，興水利。 道光元年，聞父病，引疾歸。 二年，起授淮海道，未之任，署浙江鹽運使。 遷江蘇按察使，治獄嚴明。 四年，大水，署布政使，治賑。 尋丁母憂，命赴南河修高家堰堤工，事竣回籍。 六年，命署兩淮鹽政，以未終制辭，服闋，補陝西按察使。 遷江寧布政使，父憂歸。 十年，補湖北布政使，調河南，又調江寧。 十一年，擢河東河道總督。 疏陳稭料為河工第一弊藪，親赴各廳察驗； 又言碎石實足為埽工之輔，應隨宜施用。 十二年，調江蘇巡撫。 吳中洊飢，奏免逋賦，籌撫卹。 前在籓司任，議定賑務章程，行之有效，至是仍其法，宿弊一清。 賑竣，乃籌積穀備荒。 清釐交代，盡結京控諸獄。 考覈屬吏，疏言：「察吏莫先於自察，必將各屬大小政務，逐一求盡於心，然後能以驗群吏之盡心與否。 如大吏之心先未貫徹，何從察其情偽？ 臣惟持此不敢不盡之心，事事與僚屬求實際。 」詔嘉之，勉以力行。 先是總督陶澍奏濬三江，則徐方為臬司，綜理其事，旋以憂去。 至是黃浦、吳淞工已竣，則徐力任未竟者，劉河工最要，撥帑十六萬五千有奇，白茆次要，官紳集捐十一萬兩，同時開濬，以工代賑。 兩河舊皆通海，易淤，且鑿河工鉅，改為清水長河，與黃埔、吳淞交匯通流。 各於近海修閘建壩，潮汐泥沙不能壅入，內河漲，則由壩洩出歸海。 复就原河逢灣取直​​，節省工費三萬餘兩，用濬附近劉河之七浦河，及附近白茆之徐六涇、東西護塘諸河。 又濬丹徒、丹陽運河，寶帶橋泖淀諸工，以次興舉，為吳中數十年之利。 兩署兩江總督。 十七年，擢湖廣總督。 荊、襄歲罹水災，大修堤工，其患遂弭。 整頓鹽課，以減價敵私無成效，專嚴緝私之禁，銷數大增。 湖南鎮筸兵悍，數肇釁，巡閱撫馭，密薦總兵楊芳，擢為提督，移駐辰州，慎固苗疆屯防。 十八年，鴻臚寺卿黃爵滋請禁鴉片煙，下中外大臣議。 則徐請用重典，言：「此禍不除，十年之後，不惟無可籌之餉，且無可用之兵。 」宣宗深韙之，命入覲，召對十九次。 授欽差大臣，赴廣東查辦，十九年春，至。 總督鄧廷楨已嚴申禁令，捕拏煙犯，洋商查頓先避回國。 則徐知水師提督關天培忠勇可用，令整兵嚴備。 檄諭英國領事義律查繳煙土，驅逐躉船，呈出煙土二萬餘箱，親蒞虎門驗收，焚於海濱，四十餘日始盡。 請定洋商夾帶鴉片罪名，依化外有犯之例，人即正法，貨物入官，責具甘結。 他國皆聽命，獨義律枝梧未從。 於是閱視沿海砲台，以虎門為第一門戶，橫檔山、武山為第二門戶，大小虎山為第三門戶。 海道至橫檔分為二支，右多暗沙，左經武山前，水深，洋船由之出入。 關天培創議於此設木排鐵練二重，又增築虎門之河角砲台，英國商船後至者不敢入。 義律請令赴澳門載貨，冀囤煙私販，嚴斥拒之，潛泊尖沙嘴外洋。 會有英人毆斃華民，抗不交犯，遂斷其食物，撤買辦、工人以困之。 七月，義律藉索食為名，以貨船載兵犯九龍山砲台，參將賴恩爵擊走之。 疏聞，帝喜悅，報曰：「既有此舉，不可再示柔弱。 不患卿等孟浪，但戒卿等畏葸。 」御史步際桐言出結徒虛文，則徐以彼國重然諾，不肯出結，愈不能不向索取，持之益堅。 尋義律浼澳門洋酋轉圜，原令載煙之船回國，貨船聽官查驗。 九月，商船已具結進口，義律遣兵船阻之，開砲來攻，關天培率游擊麥廷章奮擊敗之。 十月，又犯虎門官湧，官軍分五路進攻，六戰皆捷。 詔停止貿易，宣示罪狀，飭福建、浙江、江蘇嚴防海口。 先已授則徐兩江總督，至是調補兩廣。 府尹曾望顏請罷各國通商，禁漁船出洋。 則徐疏言：「自斷英國貿易，他國喜，此盈彼絀，正可以夷制夷。 如概與之絕，轉恐聯爲一氣。 粵民以海為生，概禁出洋，其勢不可終日。 」時英船寄椗外洋，以利誘奸民接濟銷菸。 二十年春，令關天培密裝砲械，僱漁船疍戶出洋設伏，候夜順風縱火，焚毀附夷匪船，接濟始斷。 五月，再焚夷船於磨刀洋。 諜知新來敵船揚帆北鄉，疏請沿海各省戒嚴。 又言夷情詭譎，若迳赴天津求通貿易，請優示懷柔，依嘉慶年間成例，將遞詞人由內地送粵。 六月，英船至廈門，為閩浙總督鄧廷楨所拒。 其犯浙者陷定海，掠寧波。 則徐上疏自請治罪，密陳兵事不可中止，略曰：「英夷所憾在粵而滋擾於浙，雖變動出於意外，其窮蹙實在意中。 惟其虛憍性成，愈窮蹙時，愈欲顯其桀驁，試其恫喝，甚且別生秘計，冀售其奸； 一切不得行，仍必帖耳俯伏。 第恐議者以為內地船砲非外夷之敵，與其曠日持久，不如設法羈縻。 抑知夷情無厭，得步進步，威不能克，患無已時。 他國紛紛效尤，不可不慮。 」因請戴罪赴浙，隨營自效。 七月，義律至天津，投書總督琦善，言廣東燒煙之釁，起自則徐及鄧廷楨二人，索價不與，又遭詬逐，故越境呈訴。 琦善據以上聞，上意始動。 時英船在粵窺伺，复連敗之蓮花峰下及龍穴洲。 捷書未上，九月，詔曰：「鴉片流毒內地，特遣林則徐會同鄧廷楨查辦，原期肅清內地，斷絕來源，隨地隨時，妥為辦理。 乃自查辦以來，內而奸民犯法不能淨盡，外而興販來源並未斷絕，沿海各省紛紛徵調，糜餉勞師，皆林則徐等辦理不善之所致。 」下則徐等嚴議，飭即來京，以琦善代之。 尋議革職，命仍回廣東備查問差委。 琦善至，義律要求賠償煙價，廈門、福州開埠通商，上怒，復命備戰。 二十一年春，予則徐四品卿銜，赴浙江鎮海協防。 時琦善雖以擅與香港逮治，和戰仍無定局。 五月，詔斥則徐在粵不能德威並用，褫卿銜，遣戍伊犁。 會河決開封，中途奉命襄辦塞決，二十二年，工竣，仍赴戍，而浙江、江南師屢敗。 是年秋，和議遂成。 二十四年，新疆興治屯田，將軍布彥泰請以則徐綜其事。 週曆南八城，濬水源，闢溝渠，墾田三萬七千餘頃，請給回民耕種，改屯兵為操防，如議行。 二十五年，召還，以四五品京堂候補。 尋署陝甘總督。 二十六年，授陝西巡撫，留甘肅，偕布彥泰治叛番，擒其酋。 二十七年，授雲貴總督。 雲南漢、回互鬥焚殺，歷十數年。 會保山回民控於京，漢民奪犯，毀官署，拆瀾滄江橋以拒，鎮道不能制。 則徐主止分良莠，不分漢、回。 二十八年，親督師往剿，途中聞彌渡客回滋亂，移兵破其巢，殲匪數百。 保山民聞風股栗，縛犯迎師，誅其首要，散其脅從，召漢、回父老諭以恩信。 遂搜捕永昌、順寧、雲州、姚州歷年戕官諸重犯，威德震洽，邊境乃安。 加太子太保，賜花翎。 二十九年，騰越邊外野夷滋擾，遣兵平之。 以病乞歸。 逾年，文宗嗣位，疊詔宣召，未至，以廣西逆首洪秀全稔亂，授欽差大臣，督師進剿，並署廣西巡撫。 行次潮州，病卒。 則徐威惠久著南服，賊聞其出，皆震悚，中道遽歿，天下惜之。 遺疏上，優詔賜卹，贈太子太傅，諡文忠。 雲南、江蘇並祀名宦，陝西請建專祠。 則徐才識過人，而待下虛衷，人樂為用，所蒞治績皆卓越。 道光之季，東南困於漕運，宣宗密詢利弊，疏陳補救本原諸策，上畿輔水利議，文宗欲命籌辦而未果。 海疆事起，時以英吉利最強為憂，則徐獨曰：「為中國患者，其俄羅斯乎！ 」後其言果驗。 鄧廷楨鄧廷楨，字嶰筠，江蘇江寧人。 嘉慶六年進士，選庶吉士，授編修。 屢分校鄉、會試，稱得士。 十五年，授台灣遺缺知府，浙江巡撫蔣攸銛請留浙，補寧波。 母憂歸，服闋，補陝西延安府，歷榆林、西安，以善折獄稱。 平反韓城、南鄭冤獄，又全同州嫠婦母子，陝民歌頌，傳播京師。 二十五年，超擢湖北按察使，權布政使。 沿江民田歷年沈沒，而賦額仍在，為民累，悉請免之。 道光元年，遷江西布政使。 以前在西安失察渭南令故出縣民柳全璧殺人罪，罣誤，奪職。 議戍軍台，宣宗知其無私，特免遣戍，予七品銜，發直隸委用。 尋授通永道。 四年，擢陝西按察使，遷布政使。 六年，擢安徽巡撫。 自嘉慶時，安徽多大獄，鳳、潁兩郡俗尤悍，常以兵定，責繳兵械，私藏尚多。 廷楨乃立限，責成保長，逾限及私造者置之法。 任吏皆得人，刁悍之風稍戢。 舊例，潁州屬三人以上凶器傷人者，極邊煙瘴充軍，僉妻發配。 廷楨疏言：「悍俗誠宜重懲，婦女顧名節，多自殘求免，或自盡傷生，情在可矜，請停其例。 」遇水災，親乘舟勘賑。 修復安豐塘、芍陂水門，濬鳳陽沫河，加築堤閘。 嚴緝捕，屢獲劇盜。 以獲南河掘堤首犯陳端，詔嘉獎。 治皖十載，政尚安靜，境內大和。 十五年，擢兩廣總督。 鴉片煙方盛行，漏銀出洋為大患。 十六年，英吉利商人以躉船載煙，廷楨禁止不許進口，猶泊外洋，嚴旨驅逐。 沿海奸民勾結，禁令猝難斷絕。 廷楨與提督關天培整備海防，迭於大嶼山口、急水洋獲蟹艇，載銀鉅萬，盡數充賞，破獲囤煙私販。 十八年，英船載屬番男婦五百餘人赴澳門居住，驅令回國。 詔下禁煙議，疏言：「法行於豪貴，則小民易從； 令嚴於中土，則外貨自絀。 」十九年，林則徐奉命至廣東，廷楨與之同心協力，盡獲躉船積煙，焚之，嚴私販之罪； 臨以兵威，屢戰皆捷，事詳則徐傳。 奸民因失業，遍騰蜚語。 廷楨疏陳，略曰：「臣緝懲鴉片，三載於茲。 豪猾之徒，刑僇逋逃，身家既失，怨讟遂興。 查檢為希旨，掩捕為貪功，偵伺為詭謀，推​​鞫為酷罰。 誣以納賄，目以營私。 譏建議為急於理財，訾新例為輕於改律，狂悖紛熒，無非為煙匪洩憤。 」詔慰勉之。 調兩江、雲貴，皆未赴，閩防方急，遂調閩浙總督。 購洋砲十四運閩，以閩洋無內港，砲台建於海灘，沙浮不固，奏改為砲墩，囊沙堆築，外護以船。 募水勇飾商船出洋巡緝。 二十年三月，英船窺廈門，遣提督程恩高等迎敵於梅林澳，擊走之。 奸民勾通出洋運煙，分責水陸師嚴緝，遇即攻擊，迭有殲擒。 六月，敵船駛入廈門，求通貿易，阻之，遂開砲，來撲砲台，參將陳勝元、守備陳光福奮擊，斃其前隊數人，發砲傷敵甚眾，乃遁。 其分犯浙洋者，陷定海，廷楨率師赴剿，行次清風嶺，詔以閩防緊要，止其赴浙，遂駐兵泉州，招募練勇。 疏言：「英船二十餘艘聚泊定海，內地師船恐難驟近，必改造堅大之船，多配砲火，間道而進，方能制勝。 」九月，詔以廷楨等在粵辦理不善，轉滋事端，與林則徐同奪職。 二十一年，琦善撤沿海兵備，虎門失守，复追論廷楨久任兩廣，廢弛營務，與則徐同戍伊犁。 二十三年，釋還。 尋予三品頂戴，授甘肅布政使。 議清查荒地，親往歷勘，由銀州東盡洮、隴，西極酒泉，得田一萬九千四百餘頃，又番貢地一千五百餘頃，寧夏馬廠地歸公一百餘頃，熟地昇科，荒者招墾，詔嘉其勤，复二品頂戴。 二十五年，擢陝西巡撫，署陝甘總督。 番匪擾蒙部，遣兵邀擊於硫磺溝，平之。 尋回任。 二十六年，卒於官。 廷楨治行早為時稱，屢躓屢起，宣宗知之深，故卒用之。 績學好士，幕府多名流，論學不輟。 尤精於音韻之學，所著筆記、詩、詞並行世。 子爾恆，亦官至陝西巡撫，自有傳。 當廷楨之去福建也，逾年，英兵復至，陷廈門，遂窺台灣。 總兵達洪阿偕台灣道姚瑩屢卻之。 及和議成，同獲譴。 達洪阿達洪阿，字厚庵，富察氏，滿洲鑲黃旗人。 由護軍洊擢總兵。 道光十五年，調台灣鎮。 十八年，剿嘉義縣匪沈和等，賜花翎，加提督銜。 二十一年八月，英兵船至雞籠海口，達洪阿與姚瑩督兵御之。 副將邱鎮功燃巨砲折其桅，敵船衝礁破碎，擒斬甚眾，賜雙眼花翎。 九月，敵船再至雞籠三沙灣，复卻之。 剿平嘉義、鳳山土匪，予騎都尉世職。 二十二年，敵船犯淡水、彰化間之大安港，欲入口。 達洪阿謀於姚瑩，瑩曰：「此未可與海上爭鋒，必以計殲之。 」乃募漁舟投敵任鄉導，誘令從土地公港入，擱淺中流，伏發，大破之，落水死者無算，其竄入漁舟者，擊斬殆盡。 詔嘉台灣三次破敵，達洪阿等智勇兼施，大揚國威，賜號阿克達春巴圖魯，加太子太保銜。 敵船遊奕外洋，乘間掩擊，迭有俘獲，遂不復至。 既而英師再陷定海，浙江、江蘇軍屢挫，乃議和。 英將濮鼎查訴稱台灣所戮皆遭風難民，達洪阿等冒功捏奏，命總督怡良赴台灣查辦。 至即傳旨革職逮問，兵民不服，勢洶洶，達洪阿等撫慰乃散。 至京，下刑部獄，尋釋之，予三等侍衛，充哈密辦事大臣。 歷伊犁參贊大臣，西寧辦事大臣。 二十六年，偕陝甘總督布彥泰剿平黑錯寺番匪。 三十年，授副都統。 咸豐元年，從大學士賽尚阿勦賊廣西，破紫金山西南砲台。 以病回京。 三年，粵匪犯畿輔，率八旗兵赴臨洺關進剿。 從欽差大臣勝保擊賊靜海，四戰皆捷，追至下西河，副都統佟鑑、天津知縣謝子澄陣亡。 詔斥達洪阿先退，革職，留營效力。 四年，敗賊獻縣，復原官。 尋追賊阜城，受傷，卒於軍。 贈都統銜，予騎都尉兼一云騎尉世職，諡壯武。 姚瑩自有傳。 論曰：林則徐才略冠時，禁煙一役，承宣宗嚴切之旨，操之過急； 及敵氛蹈瑕他犯，遂遭讒屏斥。 論者謂粵事始終倚之，加之操縱，潰裂當不致此。 則徐瀕謫，疏陳：「自道光元年以來，粵關徵銀三千餘萬兩，收其利必防其害。 使以關稅十分之一制砲造船，制夷已可裕如。 」誠為讜論。 惟當時內治廢弛，外情隔膜，言和言戰，皆昧機宜，其禍豈能倖免哉？ 鄧廷楨與則徐同心禦侮，克保岩疆。 若達洪阿、姚瑩卻敵台灣，固由守禦有方，亦因敵非專注，朝廷皆不得已而罪之，諸人卒皆復起，而名節播宇內、煥史冊矣。
Lin Zexu, whose style name was Shaomu, came from Houguan in Fujian. As a youth he was quick-witted and showed exceptional ability. At twenty he passed the provincial civil service examination. Provincial Governor Zhang Shicheng recruited him as a staff adviser. In 1811 he passed the metropolitan examination, entered the Hanlin Academy as a bachelor, and was appointed a compiler. He later served as chief examiner for the provincial examinations in Jiangxi and Yunnan and as a grader for the metropolitan examination. Promoted to censor, he memorialized that Vice Commander Zhang Bao of Min'an in Fujian, a former pirate who had surrendered, ought to be kept in check lest he grow arrogant and unruly; the court approved his advice. Soon afterward he was posted as intendant of the Hangzhou-Jiaxing-Huzhou circuit, where he repaired sea dikes and advanced hydraulic works. In 1821, on hearing that his father was ill, he took leave on grounds of illness and went home. In 1822 he was recalled and named intendant of the Huai-Hai circuit but never assumed the post, serving instead as acting Zhejiang salt transport commissioner. He was promoted to Jiangsu provincial judicial commissioner and gained a reputation for handling cases with rigorous fairness. In 1824, after severe flooding, he acted as provincial treasurer and oversaw disaster relief. Soon after, while mourning his mother, he was ordered to the Southern Rivers to repair the Gaojia Embankment; when the project was complete he returned home. In 1826 he was ordered to act as Liang-Huai salt commissioner but declined while still in mourning; after his mourning period he was appointed Shaanxi provincial judicial commissioner. He was transferred to Jiangning provincial treasurer, then went home to mourn his father. In 1830 he was appointed Hubei provincial treasurer, transferred to Henan, then transferred again to Jiangning. In 1831 he was promoted to director-general of the Eastern Rivers. He memorialized that straw materials were the greatest abuse in river works and personally inspected each office; He also argued that crushed stone could effectively supplement revetment works and should be used wherever appropriate. In 1832 he was transferred to governor of Jiangsu. Famine struck the Suzhou region repeatedly; he memorialized for tax remissions and organized relief. While provincial treasurer he had drafted relief regulations that worked well; he now applied the same methods again and cleared away entrenched abuses. After relief was complete he began stockpiling grain against future famine. He cleared up official handovers and disposed of all cases pending appeal in the capital. In reviewing subordinates he memorialized: "The first step in inspecting officials is to inspect oneself; only when every affair, large or small, in each jurisdiction is fully understood can one judge whether officials are doing their duty. If a senior official has not first mastered the facts himself, how can he discern who is sincere and who is not? I hold myself to this standard of thoroughness and demand practical results from my staff in every matter. " The emperor praised him and urged him to persevere. Earlier, Governor-General Tao Zhu had proposed dredging the Three Rivers; Lin was then judicial commissioner and oversaw the project, but soon left office to mourn. The Huangpu and Wusong projects were by then complete; Lin took up what remained. The Liu River was the priority, funded with more than 165,000 taels from the treasury; the Baomao channel was secondary, with 110,000 taels raised by officials and gentry; both were dredged at once, labor substituting for relief. Both channels had originally opened to the sea, silted easily, and cost a fortune to dredge; they were converted into long freshwater canals linking the Huangpu and Wusong. Sluices and dams were built near the coast so tidal silt could not choke the canals; when inland waters rose, the excess was discharged to the sea through the dams. Where the old channel curved, he cut a straighter course, saving over 30,000 taels, which he used to dredge the Qipu River near the Liu River and the Xu Liujing and east and west Hutang channels near Baomao. He also dredged the Dantu and Danyang canals and works at Baodai Bridge and the lake district, undertaking them in turn to the lasting benefit of the Suzhou region. He twice acted as governor-general of the Two Jiangs. In 1837 he was promoted to governor-general of Huguang. The Jing and Xiang basins had suffered annual flooding; after major dike repairs the threat subsided. He reformed the salt monopoly: cutting prices to undercut smugglers failed, but strict enforcement against smuggling greatly increased legal sales. Hunan's Zhenqian garrison troops were unruly and often caused incidents; Lin toured the region, recommended Brigadier Yang Fang for promotion to provincial commander, posted him at Chenzhou, and tightened defenses along the Miao frontier. In 1838 Huang Juezi of the Honglu Temple petitioned to ban opium, and the court ordered discussion among all high officials. Lin urged harsh measures, writing: "Unless this evil is eradicated, within ten years the state will lack both revenue and usable troops." " Emperor Daoguang strongly agreed, summoned him to court, and conferred with him nineteen times. He was appointed Imperial Commissioner for Guangdong and arrived there in the spring of 1839. Governor-General Deng Tingzhen had already enforced the ban and arrested offenders; the merchant Dent had fled home ahead of the crackdown. Knowing Naval Commander Guan Tianpei to be loyal and capable, Lin ordered him to drill the troops and prepare defenses. He ordered British Consul Charles Elliott to surrender opium and expel receiving ships; when over 20,000 chests were produced, Lin personally supervised verification at Humen and burned them on the shore over more than forty days. He proposed that merchants smuggling opium be punished under the law for foreigners who commit capital crimes on Chinese soil—execution, confiscation, and a sworn bond of good conduct. Other nations complied; only Elliott refused. He inspected coastal defenses, ranking Humen as the first line, Hengdang and Wushan as the second, and the Great and Little Tiger Islands as the third. At Hengdang the channel split: the right branch was shoal-ridden, the left, deeper, passed Wushan and was the route foreign vessels used. Guan Tianpei proposed double lines of wooden stakes and iron chains there and new batteries at Humen's river bend; later British merchantmen dared not enter. Elliott asked to load cargo at Macao, intending to hoard opium for illicit trade; Lin refused and forced him to anchor secretly off Chuanbi. When a Briton beat a Chinese man to death and refused to surrender the killer, Lin cut off supplies and withdrew compradors and workers to pressure them. In July Elliott, claiming to need provisions, sent armed cargo ships against the Kowloon batteries; Assistant Commander Lai Enjue repulsed them. The emperor read Lin's report with pleasure and replied: "Now that you have acted, you must not show weakness again." I am not concerned that you may be rash, only that you may be timid." Censor Bu Jitong called the bond requirement mere formality; Lin replied that Britain prized honor and, having refused the bond, must be pressed all the harder. Elliott soon asked Macao's foreign community to mediate, agreeing that opium ships would leave and cargo vessels submit to inspection. In September merchant ships that had signed bonds tried to enter port; Elliott sent warships to block them and opened fire; Guan Tianpei and Acting Commander Mai Tingzhang drove them off. In October the British attacked Guanyong near Humen; Qing forces advanced in five columns and won six successive engagements. The court halted trade with Britain, published their offenses, and ordered Fujian, Zhejiang, and Jiangsu to guard their coasts. Lin had already been named governor-general of the Two Jiangs; he was now transferred to the Two Guang. Prefect Zeng Wangyan proposed ending trade with all nations and banning fishing boats from leaving port. Lin objected in a memorial: "Cutting off British trade pleases other nations; their gain is Britain's loss—precisely the strategy of setting foreigners against one another." If all trade were cut off, they might unite against us. Cantonese depend on the sea for their livelihood; a total ban on going to sea could not be sustained for even a day." Meanwhile British ships lay offshore, bribing locals to supply them with opium. In the spring of 1840 Lin had Guan Tianpei arm fishing boats and Tanka crews for night raids under favorable wind, burning supply vessels and breaking the smuggling chain. In May more British ships were burned at Modaoyang. Intelligence showed new enemy ships sailing north; Lin urged all coastal provinces to go on alert. He warned that if the British sailed to Tianjin to petition for trade, they should be treated leniently per Jiaqing precedent and escorted overland to Guangdong. In June British ships reached Xiamen and were repulsed by Governor-General Deng Tingzhen. Those that struck Zhejiang captured Dinghai and raided Ningbo. Lin submitted a confession and argued privately that the war must continue: "The British resent Guangdong but now harass Zhejiang; though unexpected, their desperation was foreseeable." Arrogant by nature, the more cornered they grow, the more they bluster, threaten, and plot; but when none of this works they will submit in the end. I fear others will say our ships and guns cannot match theirs and prefer appeasement to a long war. But foreigners are never satisfied; concede once and they demand more; if force fails, trouble never ends. Other powers will follow suit—this must be considered." He asked to go to Zhejiang under punishment and serve with the troops. In July Elliott reached Tianjin and wrote Governor Qishan blaming Lin and Deng for the opium burning, unpaid compensation, and insults that drove him to petition at the capital. Qishan reported this to the throne, and the emperor's resolve began to falter. Meanwhile British ships off Guangdong were again defeated below Lianhua Peak and at Longxuezhou. Before victory dispatches arrived, a September edict declared that opium poisoned the empire and Lin Zexu and Deng Tingzhen had been sent to eradicate it and cut off the source. Yet since their mission began, smuggling within had not ended, supplies from abroad had not ceased, and coastal provinces were mobilized at great cost—all due to Lin Zexu's mismanagement. Lin and Deng were censured, ordered to Beijing, and replaced by Qishan. Soon both were dismissed; Lin was ordered back to Guangdong pending further inquiry. Qishan arrived; Elliott demanded opium compensation and opening Xiamen and Fuzhou; the emperor, enraged, ordered war preparations renewed. In the spring of 1841 Lin was given fourth-rank courtier status and sent to defend Zhenhai in Zhejiang. Though Qishan had been arrested for ceding Hong Kong without authorization, peace and war remained undecided. In May an edict blamed Lin for failing to balance firmness and conciliation in Guangdong, stripped his courtier rank, and exiled him to Yili. The Yellow River breached at Kaifeng; Lin was ordered en route to help stem it; in 1842, work done, he continued to exile while armies in Zhejiang and Jiangnan suffered repeated defeats. Peace was concluded that autumn. In 1844 Xinjiang colonization was expanded; General Buyantai asked that Lin oversee it. He toured the eight southern cities, opened water sources, dug canals, reclaimed over 37,000 qing, assigned land to Hui farmers, and converted garrison troops to defense duties as planned. In 1845 he was recalled and placed on the waiting list for capital posts of the fourth and fifth ranks. He soon acted as governor-general of Shaanxi-Gansu. In 1846 he was appointed Shaanxi governor but stayed in Gansu, joining Buyantai to suppress rebellious tribes and capture their leader. In 1847 he was appointed governor-general of Yunnan-Guizhou. In Yunnan, Han and Hui communities had fought and burned one another for more than a decade. Meanwhile Baoshan Hui had appealed to Beijing; Han residents seized prisoners, wrecked government offices, and destroyed the Lancang River bridge to block the troops, and local commanders could not restore order. Lin insisted on judging people by conduct, not by whether they were Han or Hui. In 1848 he led the campaign in person; en route he learned of unrest among guest Hui at Midu, diverted his forces to destroy their stronghold, and killed several hundred rebels. Baoshan residents, terrified, bound the guilty and welcomed his army; Lin executed the leaders, released the coerced, and summoned Han and Hui elders to pledge goodwill. He then hunted down notorious murderers of officials in Yongchang, Shunning, Yunzhou, and Yaozhou; his authority restored order along the frontier. He was promoted to Grand Guardian of the Heir Apparent and awarded the peacock feather. In 1849 tribes beyond the Tengyue frontier raided; he sent troops and pacified them. He resigned citing illness. The next year Emperor Xianfeng succeeded and repeatedly summoned him; before he could arrive, with Hong Xiuquan ravaging Guangxi, Lin was named Imperial Commissioner to suppress the rebellion and acting Guangxi governor. He died of illness at Chaozhou on the march. Lin's renown in the south had long intimidated rebels; his sudden death en route was mourned throughout the empire. His death memorial reached the throne; the emperor granted condolences, posthumously named him Grand Tutor, and gave him the posthumous title Wenzhong. Yunnan and Jiangsu enshrined him among notable officials; Shaanxi petitioned for a dedicated temple. Lin's ability exceeded most men's, yet he treated subordinates with humility and won their loyalty; everywhere he served he left distinguished records. Late in Daoguang's reign the southeast struggled with grain transport; Emperor Daoguang privately consulted him; Lin proposed fundamental reforms and a capital-region waterworks plan that Emperor Xianfeng wished to implement but never did. When the coastal crisis began, Britain was feared as the strongest power; Lin alone said, "China's true danger may be Russia!" His prediction later proved true. Deng Tingzhen. Deng Tingzhen, style name Xiejun, was from Jiangning in Jiangsu. In 1801 he passed the metropolitan examination, entered the Hanlin Academy, and became a compiler. He repeatedly graded provincial and metropolitan examinations and was praised for recognizing talent. In 1810 he was named acting Taiwan prefect, but Zhejiang Governor Jiang Youqian kept him in the province and he was posted to Ningbo. After mourning his mother he was appointed Yan'an prefect in Shaanxi, served at Yulin and Xi'an, and won fame for adjudication. He reversed wrongful convictions in Hancheng and Nanzheng and saved a widow and son in Tongzhou; Shaanxi sang his praises and word reached Beijing. In 1820 he was promoted out of turn to Hubei judicial commissioner and acted as provincial treasurer. Riverbank farmland had long been flooded while taxes remained due; he secured full remissions. In 1821 he became Jiangxi provincial treasurer. He was dismissed for failing while in Xi'an to detect that a Weinan magistrate had wrongly released a murderer, Liu Quanbi. Sentenced to frontier service, he was spared because the emperor knew he was blameless, given seventh-rank status, and assigned to Zhili. He soon became intendant of Tongzhou-Yongping. In 1824 he became Shaanxi judicial commissioner, then provincial treasurer. In 1826 he was promoted to Anhui governor. Since Jiaqing, Anhui had seen many major cases; Feng and Ying were especially unruly and often required troops; weapon surrenders left many still hidden. Deng set deadlines, held baojia leaders accountable, and punished latecomers and illegal arms makers. His appointees were capable and local lawlessness subsided. By old rule, three or more armed assailants in Yingzhou were exiled to the frontier and their wives banished with them. Deng memorialized that while harsh punishment was warranted, women often killed themselves to avoid dishonor and should not be banished with their husbands; he asked that the rule be abolished. During floods he personally toured by boat to oversee relief. He restored Anfeng Pond and Quepi sluices, dredged Fengyang's Mo River, and strengthened dikes. Strict pursuit of bandits brought repeated captures of major gangs. Capturing Chen Duan, who had breached the Southern River dikes, won imperial praise. Ten years in Anhui brought quiet government and widespread peace. In 1835 he became governor-general of the Two Guang. Opium was rampant and silver draining abroad had become a national crisis. In 1836 British merchants stored opium on receiving ships; Deng barred import but they anchored offshore until the court ordered expulsion. Coastal smugglers colluded with foreigners and the trade could not be stopped overnight. Deng and Naval Commander Guan Tianpei strengthened defenses, seized smuggling boats with vast silver cargoes at Dayu Shan and Jishui Yang, and broke up opium hoards. In 1838 British ships brought over five hundred dependents to Macao; Deng expelled them. When the opium ban was debated, he wrote: "Enforce the law on the powerful and commoners will follow; if prohibition is strict inland, foreign opium will dry up." In 1839 Lin Zexu arrived in Guangdong; Deng cooperated fully, seized and burned opium from receiving ships, and imposed harsh penalties on smugglers; backed by force, they won repeated victories—detailed in Lin's biography. Displaced smugglers spread rumors everywhere. Deng memorialized: "I have fought opium for three years. Wealthy offenders, punished and ruined, now slander us. They call inspections flattery, arrests ambition, surveillance conspiracy, and trials cruelty. They accuse us of bribery and self-dealing. They mock reform as greed and new laws as rash—all to avenge opium smugglers." The emperor replied with encouragement. Transferred to the Two Jiangs and Yunnan-Guizhou, he never took those posts; with Fujian under threat he became Fujian-Zhejiang governor-general. He bought fourteen foreign guns for Fujian; sandy beaches made batteries unstable, so he built sand-filled gun platforms shielded by boats. He recruited coastal fighters and armed merchant vessels for patrols. In March 1840 British ships probed Xiamen; Provincial Commander Cheng Engao repulsed them at Meilin Bay. Smugglers still shipped opium offshore; land and naval forces attacked on sight with repeated successes. In June enemy ships entered Xiamen seeking trade; when blocked they bombarded the forts; Chen Shengyuan and Chen Guangfu killed the vanguard, wounded many, and drove them off. While British forces took Dinghai in Zhejiang, Deng marched to relieve it but was ordered to remain in Fujian; he stationed troops at Quanzhou and raised militia. He wrote: "Over twenty British ships lie at Dinghai; our junks cannot approach; we must build larger warships with heavy guns and take indirect routes to prevail." In September an edict blamed Deng and Lin for mishandling Guangdong and causing trouble; both were dismissed. In 1841 Qishan weakened coastal defenses and Humen fell; Deng's long Guangdong tenure was blamed; he and Lin were exiled to Yili. In 1843 he was released. He soon received third-rank insignia and became Gansu provincial treasurer. Surveying wasteland from Yinchuan east to Jiuquan west, he registered nearly 19,400 qing of fields, 1,500 qing of tribute land, and 100 qing of Ningxia pasture, taxing arable land and recruiting settlers; the emperor restored his second-rank insignia. In 1845 he became Shaanxi governor and acting Shaanxi-Gansu governor-general. Tribal raiders harassed Mongolia; he defeated them at Liuhuang Gou. He soon resumed his regular duties. In 1846 he died in office. Deng's reputation for governance was long established; though he fell and rose repeatedly, Emperor Daoguang trusted him to the end. Learned and fond of scholars, he kept distinguished advisers and debated scholarship constantly. He excelled in phonology; his notes, poetry, and lyrics all circulated widely. His son Erheng also became Shaanxi governor and has a separate biography. A year after Deng left Fujian, British forces returned, took Xiamen, and threatened Taiwan. Regional Commander Da Hong'a and Taiwan Intendant Yao Ying repeatedly drove them back. When peace was made both were censured. Da Hong'a. Da Hong'a, style name Hou'an, of the Fuca clan, was a Manchu of the Bordered Yellow Banner. He rose from the guards to regional commander. In 1835 he was transferred to command Taiwan. In 1838 he suppressed bandits led by Shen He in Jiayi, received the peacock feather, and acting provincial commander rank. In August 1841 British warships reached Keelung; Da Hong'a and Yao Ying led the defense. Vice Commander Qiu Zhenggong shattered a mast with heavy guns; the enemy ship wrecked on reefs; many were killed or captured; Da Hong'a received double peacock feathers. In September the British returned to Sansha Bay near Keelung and were repulsed again. He pacified bandits in Jiayi and Fengshan and received the hereditary rank of Cavalry Captain. In 1842 enemy ships approached Da'an harbor between Tamsui and Zhanghua intending to land. Da Hong'a consulted Yao Ying, who said they could not fight at sea and must use stratagem. They hired fishing boats to mislead the British into Tugonggang, where the ships grounded; ambush troops annihilated them, few survivors escaping ashore. The court praised three Taiwanese victories; Da Hong'a was named Akedachun Batulu and made Grand Guardian of the Heir Apparent. Patrols struck British ships offshore whenever opportunity offered, and the enemy stopped returning. Soon the British retook Dinghai; defeats in Zhejiang and Jiangsu led to peace talks. General Pottinger claimed the Taiwanese victims were shipwrecked civilians and accused Da Hong'a of fabricating victories; Governor Yiliang was sent to investigate. The investigators arrived to arrest them; troops and civilians rioted until Da Hong'a calmed them. In Beijing he was jailed, then released, made a third-rank guardsman, and posted to Hami. He later served as assistant at Ili and commissioner at Xining. In 1846 he joined Governor-General Buyantai to suppress the Heicuosi tribal rebels. In 1850 he was appointed vice commander-in-chief. In 1851 he followed Grand Secretary Sai Shang'a against rebels in Guangxi and took the southwest batteries on Zijin Mountain. Illness forced his return to Beijing. In 1853 he led Banner troops to Linming Pass when Guangdong rebels threatened the capital region. Serving Imperial Commissioner Sheng Bao at Jinghai, he won four battles and pursued the enemy to Xiaxihe, where Vice Commander Tong Jian and Tianjin Magistrate Xie Zicheng fell. An edict blamed Da Hong'a for retreating first, stripped his rank, but kept him at camp. In 1854 he defeated rebels at Xian County and was restored to office. Soon afterward, wounded while pursuing rebels at Fucheng, he died in camp. Posthumously named commander-in-chief, granted hereditary Cavalry Captain and Cloud Cavalry Captain ranks, and given the posthumous title Zhuangwu. Yao Ying has a separate biography. The historians comment: Lin Zexu's ability was unmatched in his day; in suppressing opium he carried out Emperor Daoguang's stern orders too hastily; when the enemy exploited openings and struck elsewhere, slander drove him from office. Some argue that had Guangdong policy rested in his hands throughout, the disaster would not have been so severe. Facing dismissal, Lin wrote: "Since 1821 Guangdong customs have collected over thirty million taels; profits must be weighed against dangers. Spending a tenth of customs revenue on guns and ships would have sufficed to control the foreigners." That was sound counsel. But domestic government was decaying, foreign realities misunderstood, and advocates of peace and war alike misjudged the moment—how could disaster have been avoided? Deng Tingzhen stood with Lin against foreign aggression and helped secure the coast. Da Hong'a and Yao Ying defended Taiwan effectively, though partly because Britain was not fully committed there; the court punished them reluctantly, yet all were eventually rehabilitated, and their reputations endure in history.

Li Duanfen, Xu Zhijing (with his son Ren Zhu), Chen Baozhen, Huang Zunxian, Ceng He, Yang Shenxiu, Yang Rui, Liu Guangdi, Tan Sitong, Tang Caichang, Lin Xu, and Kang Guangren. Li Duanfen, whose style name was Biyuan, came from Guizhu in Guizhou. In 1863 he became a jinshi, entered the Hanlin Academy as a bachelor, and received an appointment as compiler; Grand Secretary Woren and Minister Luo Dunyan both prized his talent. Ten years later he was sent out to serve as educational commissioner for Yunnan. This came after the Muslim rebellions, when frontier routes were cut and earlier commissioners had not been able to hold examinations throughout the province; Duanfen now toured each district in person, and learning gradually revived. In 1879 he was made a censor, but had to step aside when his uncle Chaoyi became metropolitan magistrate; he then resumed his former post. He rose step by step to Grand Secretariat bachelor. In 1892 he was transferred to vice minister of Justice. Six years on he was put in charge of the capital grain depots. He held literary office again and again, served as provincial examiner four times and as associate metropolitan examiner once, and took pleasure in advancing promising scholars. While examining candidates in Guangdong he recognized Liang Qichao's ability, married him to his cousin's daughter, and thereafter often took his advice, speaking at length on Western and Eastern political systems. About this time Kang Youwei submitted memorials calling for reform and for the expansion of education. In 1896 he memorialized to found a university in the capital and to establish schools in every province, prefecture, and county, each with separate halls for study; together with libraries, instrument halls, and translation bureaus, a wider press, and students sent abroad for study. In 1898 he privately recommended Kang Youwei and Tan Sitong as men of outstanding capacity. He also urged revision of the contradictory procedural regulations of the ministries; the emperor warmly approved and ordered each department head to set a deadline for clearing away old abuses. He was promoted to minister of Rites. Soon the reform party fell; Duanfen denounced himself in a memorial, was stripped of office, and was banished to Xinjiang. He fell ill on the journey and was allowed to remain at Ganzhou. In 1901 he was amnestied and returned home to lecture at the Jingshi Academy in Guizhou. He died in 1907. In 1909, at the petition of Yunnan and Guizhou officials in the capital, his former rank was restored. Xu Zhijing (with his son Ren Zhu), whose style name was Zijing, was a native of Yixing in Jiangsu but was registered at Wanping. In 1876 he became a jinshi, entered the Hanlin Academy, and was appointed compiler. He rose step by step to reader-in-waiting of the Hanlin Academy. After his father's mourning period he was recalled in 1897 to his former post. He had long worried over the mounting foreign threat and looked for ways to advise the throne. His son Ren Zhu, then a compiler serving as Hunan educational commissioner and promoting new learning, wrote urging him to recommend Kang Youwei. Zhijing then memorialized: "The nation's course is still unsettled; I beg Your Majesty to declare your firm decision, showing whether reform is to be embraced or rejected." " — thus to sound out the emperor's mind. Soon an edict did call for men of talent; he then recommended Kang Youwei as eminently employable, together with Liang Qichao, Huang Zunxian, and others. He followed with further memorials to abolish the eight-legged essay in favor of policy essays, cut redundant offices, and create honorary grand secretary posts. Citing acute border danger, he urged training a strong army and strongly recommended Yuan Shikai to take charge of military affairs. The emperor approved every point and ordered them implemented. Xu Yingkui, minister of Rites, and others who had blocked memorials were removed, and Zhijing was appointed acting right vice minister. In August 1898 the empress dowager resumed rule; every official involved in the reform was punished. Zhijing lost his post and was imprisoned, soon sentenced to lifelong detention; Ren Zhu was dismissed as well. In 1900, when the allied armies occupied Beijing, he was released to await judgment and then amnestied. He died at seventy-five. Chen Baozhen, whose style name was Youming, was a native of Yining in Jiangxi. From youth he showed strong character; his literary work was disciplined, and Zeng Guofan prized him. As a provincial graduate he joined his father Wei Lin in organizing local defense against the Taiping rebels. He then went to Hunan, entered Yi Peishen's staff, and campaigned between Laifeng and Longshan. When Shi Dakai attacked, the troops were starving and exhausted; Baozhen went to Yongshun to raise grain, supplies kept coming, the defense held firm, and the rebels gradually withdrew. In Jiangxi he helped Xi Baotian plan the destruction of Hong Fuzhen; when peace returned he was ranked as prefect and was exceptionally promoted to Hebei circuit intendant. He founded the Academy of Practical Learning, chose students from three prefectures, and brought in distinguished teachers. He was made surveillance commissioner of Zhejiang but was later dismissed for an offense. Hunan governor Wang Wenshao recommended him; in 1890 he was called to the capital, made surveillance commissioner of Hubei, and acted as provincial treasurer. In 1894 he was promoted to Zhili provincial treasurer; at audience the war with Japan was raging; seeing the emperor's haggard face, he urged daily study of the Kangxi emperor's commentary on the Changes so that amid upheaval the ruler might keep his bearings. His many other memorials likewise won the emperor's approval. The emperor judged him loyal and put him in charge of the grain depots with sole responsibility for memorials. When the Treaty of Shimonoseki was signed, he wept and said, "The state is all but lost!" " The following year, on Rong Lu's recommendation, he was made governor of Hunan. A staff member named Ren Lin had built a faction for private profit; Baozhen punished him severely as soon as he took office. Zhili treasurer Wang Lian pleaded for Ren; Baozhen reported this to the throne and Wang was punished. Reinvestigating the case, he fully exposed Shi Nianzu's appointment of unworthy men, and Nianzu was dismissed. He thereby gained a reputation for uncompromising integrity. Hunan was backward and insular; he aimed to make the province a model of wealth and strength for the southeast, installing telegraph lines and steam launches, building an arms factory, and founding the Public Security Bureau, the Southern Study Society, and the Current Affairs Academy. He brought in Liang Qichao to direct education in Hunan, and local attitudes changed dramatically. He memorialized on reforming scholarship, training troops, and raising revenue; the emperor approved all points, told him to stand firm against loose talk, and issued a special edict of praise. Zhang Zhidong then enjoyed enormous prestige, and every provincial official watched him in silence. When Baozhen first governed Hubei as treasurer, they often clashed; he alone stood up to Zhang without favoritism; Zhang was displeased but could not prevail. In time the two became close allies and co-signed every memorial on the new policies, while Hubei governor Tan Jixun held aloof. Kang Youwei's proposals were meanwhile winning repeated approval. Long admiring Zeng Guofan and Hu Linyi as talent-spotters, he recommended Yang Rui, Liu Guangdi, Tan Sitong, and Lin Xu to help carry out the reforms. The throne was calling for adaptable talent; all four were suddenly promoted to metropolitan ministers to join the reform, and they memorialized on current affairs without restraint. He added that though talented, the four were junior in standing and might treat office too lightly; he asked that a senior figure such as Zhang Zhidong supervise them. The memorial arrived after the empress dowager had resumed rule; the four ministers were executed; the recommender was punished; Baozhen lost office, his son Sanli was dismissed, and Hunan school texts such as Study Compact, Definitions, Notes, and Answers were destroyed. Earlier, dismissed Ningxiang circuit intendant Zhou Han had been punished by the governor-general for posting anti-Christian broadsheets. When Baozhen arrived, Zhou printed new posters; Baozhen ordered them destroyed; Zhou beat those who tore them down; Baozhen had him jailed in anger. Conservatives hated him bitterly, yet reform-minded men praised him all the more for it. After his removal, every institution he had built for the public good, however successful, was dismantled until nothing remained. He died at seventy. Huang Zunxian, whose style name was Gongdu, was a native of Jiaying prefecture. As a provincial graduate he bought his way to circuit intendant. He served as counselor on the mission to Japan and submitted his Record of Japan to the court. He was soon made consul-general at San Francisco. American officials once used public health as a pretext to fill the jails with Chinese immigrants. Zunxian went straight to the jail and had his attendants measure its capacity, saying, "Is this place really more sanitary than where our people live?" " The officials apologized and released them immediately. He served as Chang-Bao salt commissioner and acted as surveillance commissioner. Chen Baozhen was then governor and pushing reforms; Zunxian first preached popular self-government, saying, "Begin by governing yourself and your own township." Extend that from one township to a county, a prefecture, a province, and the whole country, and you can build true republican government and reach the ideal of Great Unity. " He then modeled a Public Security Bureau on Western policing: every local matter of public welfare that the people could manage was entrusted to respected local leaders, with officials assisting only where needed. He soon left office, was appointed envoy to Japan, but before he could depart the reform purge struck and he was sent home. He wrote works including Human Realm Studio Poems and others. Ceng He, whose style name was Huaiqing, of the Xitala clan, was a Manchu of the Plain White Banner. His father Qingyun was general at Ningxia. By hereditary privilege he entered the Ministry of Works, rose to bureau director, served on the Grand Council staff, and became a censor. In 1883 he was sent out as Shaanxi grain transport commissioner. Peasants in Xi'an and Tongzhou districts usually paid grain tax at the provincial granary over long, difficult roads with many abuses; he reformed the system, and the people welcomed the change. Scholarly culture in the capital region was plain and craft skills poor; he brought in Bo Jingwei and Liu Guangzan to run the Guanzhong Academy, stressed practical learning, and won broad approval among scholars. He also set up a sericulture bureau, hired weaving masters to teach full silk processing, and annual output rivaled Shandong and Henan. In 1887 he was made surveillance commissioner. The following year he left office to mourn his mother. After mourning he resumed office and was soon made Gansu provincial treasurer. In 1898 he was transferred to Zhili but stayed in Gansu on kinship grounds. Promoted to governor of Hubei, he exclaimed, "With the times this desperate, how can we still cling to old rules and refuse change?" " He then used the Shaan-Gan governor-general's seal to memorialize on appointments, lot-drawing, finance, and litigation, urging flexible reform of precedent to strengthen the state. Reader-in-waiting Yigu and Zhang Zhongxin of the Court of Imperial Entertainments impeached him for subverting government, and he was dismissed by edict. Early in his capital career his family was desperately poor and he rented a shabby room. In provincial office he devoted himself tirelessly to public business, heedless of his own means. After his dismissal he went everywhere on foot; Shaanxi people regularly pooled money to support him. He grew still poorer and eventually sold divination in the capital markets in threadbare clothes. He died soon after. After the Xuantong reign began, Governor-General Duanfang had his former rank restored. Yang Shenxiu, whose style name was Yicun and whose original name was Yuxiu, was a native of Wenxi in Shanxi. As a youth he was quick-witted and versed in both Chinese and Western mathematics. Early on, as a provincial graduate, he bought his way to assistant director in the Ministry of Justice. On leave during a great famine he was ordered to organize relief under Yan Jingming; he proposed corvée and tax reforms that brought some relief. In 1889 he became a jinshi, kept his post while rising to bureau director, then became a censor. He once declared, "The times are desperate: without overturning the old order there can be no renewal, and without reform there can be no survival." " In 1898 Russia forced the cession of Port Arthur and Dalian Bay. Shenxiu urgently urged alliance with Britain and Japan to resist, in blunt, forceful language. The court was then eager to pursue reform, but senior ministers constantly dissented. He and Xu Zhijing then memorialized to fix the national course; he also urged reform of the examination essay by drawing on Song, Yuan, and Ming models; the matter went to the Ministry of Rites, but Minister Xu Yingkui withheld the memorial. When the special economic examination was discussed with plans to cut quotas, he joined Song Bolu in impeaching Xu for obstruction. The emperor ordered Xu to explain; Xu counter-attacked Kang Youwei for currying favor at court and implicated Shenxiu, but the emperor did not pursue Shenxiu. Censor Wen Ti impeached him for spreading Kang's Society to Protect the State and exposed Kang's court connections; the emperor rebuked Wen for personal vendetta, and Wen was blamed instead. More fired up than ever, he memorialized for a translation bureau, princely missions abroad, and regulations for study overseas; all were approved. He also proposed daily testing of ordinary officials, twenty at a time, to promote the capable and weed out the mediocre and out of touch; courtiers watched him with growing hostility. When slander reached the court against the urgently reforming Hunan governor Chen Baozhen, Shenxiu defended him; the emperor issued a special commendation, and Baozhen could proceed. In August the coup struck; the court trembled expecting mass executions; Shenxiu alone memorialized demanding the empress dowager restore power to the emperor. Before the memorial went up, his son Futian begged him to stop; he shouted him down and sent him away. He was soon arrested and sentenced to execution. Blunt and upright by nature, he often confronted others with their faults face to face, earning many enemies. In ten months as censor he submitted more than twenty sealed memorials; most drafts are lost, but three prison poems survive. He wrote Drafts from the Hall of Empty Voices and the New Gazetteer of Wenxi County. Yang Rui, whose style name was Shujiao, was a native of Mianzhu in Sichuan. Clever as a youth, he impressed educational commissioner Zhang Zhidong, who brought him onto his staff. At the Zunjing Academy he was the youngest student yet often topped his class. As an outstanding tribute student he passed the metropolitan examination and was appointed magistrate. When Zhang governed Guangdong and Guangxi, Rui followed him south. In 1885 he passed the Shuntian provincial examination and became a Grand Secretariat secretary. In 1898 Zhang recommended him for the special economic examination. Chen Baozhen also recommended him; with Liu Guangdi, Tan Sitong, and Lin Xu he was made a fourth-rank minister and Grand Council clerk to join the reforms. At audience he urged education and military training as the path to national salvation; the emperor approved. In the seventh month Wang Zhao of the Ministry of Rites submitted a sealed memorial; Xu Yingkui and other ministers blocked it from reaching the throne. The emperor was furious and dismissed all six ministers and vice ministers; the court was shaken. The emperor wrote Rui a secret instruction: "Lately I see the empress dowager does not want these senile, incompetent ministers removed in favor of bold, capable men, nor does she want the laws wholly changed." Do I not know that only vigorous reform can save a state so long weakened? But now it is beyond my power; if I forced reform through, I could not even keep my throne. You, Liu Guangdi, Tan Sitong, Lin Xu, and the others must work out how to advance able men, carry out reform in time, and yet not offend the empress dowager; memorialize at once for my decision — I am desperate with anxiety!" " Rui replied: "The empress dowager personally gave you the throne; you should put filial duty first and yield to her in affairs." Reform should proceed in stages; ministerial appointments and dismissals should not be rushed. " The emperor approved. Soon the empress dowager resumed rule; every advocate of reform was harshly punished. In prison he believed himself innocent and expected to clear his name at trial; the next day he was executed in the market with the others. After the Xuantong reign began, his son Qingchang submitted the secret edict to the Censorate for publication, and only then did it become known. Liu Guangdi, whose style name was Peicun, was a native of Fushun in Sichuan. In 1883 he became a jinshi and was appointed a director in the Ministry of Justice. He handled cases with rigorous precision; after offending a superior over a capital case he withdrew, shut himself in to study, and never returned to office. His family was poor and he was incorruptible; he accepted no gifts, even courtesy offerings, except from old friends. He was especially close to Yang Rui. He mastered the Offices of Zhou, the Rites, and the Elder and Younger Dai ritual commentaries. He was summoned on Chen Baozhen's recommendation, though against his inclination; he was about to decline by memorial when fellow Sichuan officials at court pressed him to accept. At one audience he urged that with the times perilous, the people suffering, and foreign threat mounting, the throne must open its mind to reform; the emperor praised him. With the memorial channel wide open, hundreds of submissions arrived daily; he spent whole days drafting replies and marking them for the emperor's decision. He told confidants afterward, "I cannot bear this work; I shall soon take leave and go home!" " Within a month disaster struck, and he surrendered himself to prison. At the execution Associate Grand Secretary Gangyi supervised; Guangdi exclaimed, "Executed without trial — how can this be?" " Ordered to kneel for the decree, he refused: "By ancestral law, even thieves who cry innocence at execution must be heard again." Even if we are worthless, what of the dignity of the state!" " Gangyi was silent; pressed again, he said, "I am only ordered to supervise the execution — what else do I know?" " Jailers forced him to kneel, but he stood erect. Yang Rui called, "Peicun, kneel!" Kneel! Just obey the decree. " Only then did he kneel and die. He wrote the Jiebai Studio Collected Poems and Prose. Tan Sitong (with Tang Caichang appended), whose style name was Fusheng, was a native of Liuyang in Hunan. His father Jixun was governor of Hubei. As a youth he was bold and ambitious; his writing was unconventional and forceful. He took continual renewal as his principle and paid little heed to conventional morality. His cautious father Jixun greatly disliked him for this. He joined Liu Jintang's staff in Xinjiang, bought his way to prefect, and was assigned to Jiangsu. When Chen Baozhen became Hunan governor, he returned home to help the reforms. When Liang Qichao founded the Southern Study Society, Sitong became its head. Hundreds gathered at each meeting; his impassioned speeches on current affairs moved many. In 1898 he was summoned to the capital, pleased the emperor at audience, and was made a fourth-rank minister and Grand Council clerk. Though all four were appointed together, Sitong offered the most proposals at each audience. The emperor wished to open the Maojin Hall and appoint advisers; Sitong was to draft edicts citing precedents before the emperor went to the Summer Palace for the empress dowager's approval. He said afterward, "Now I know the emperor has no real power at all!" " Rong Lu then governed the capital region; Yuan Shikai was training troops at Tianjin as surveillance commissioner. Yuan was promoted to vice minister and summoned to audience. Sitong once visited Yuan Shikai by night for secret talks. The next day Yuan returned to Tianjin. The next morning the empress dowager returned from the Summer Palace and seized power back. Liang Qichao hid in the Japanese legation; Sitong visited him and urged him to flee abroad. Sitong said, "Without those who flee, there is no future to plan for; without those who die, there is no way to repay the emperor." " He would not leave. Soon he was beheaded in public. He wrote Benevolence Studies, the Mangcang Studio Poetry Collection, and other works. Tang Caichang, whose style name was Fochen. As youths he and Sitong were famed together as "the two students of Liuyang," outstanding pupils of the Two Lakes Academy. Learning of Sitong's death, he grieved and plotted repeatedly; whenever he spoke of Emperor Guangxu he wept. In 1900, when the court fled Beijing, he secretly joined the Society of the Rich to raise troops in the name of loyal restoration, planning to attack Wuchang and Hankou. Captured, he spoke boldly without reserve, asked to die, and was executed. Lin Xu, whose style name was Dungu, was a native of Houguan in Fujian. At nineteen he ranked first in the Fujian provincial examination. He later took the metropolitan examination; when war with Japan broke out, he joined fellow candidates in a memorial on current affairs that went unanswered. He bought his way to a Grand Secretariat secretaryship. When Kang Youwei began advocating reform, he founded the Cantonese Study Society in the capital to stir scholarly morale; Sichuan, Zhejiang, Shaanxi, and Fujian societies followed. Lin led the Fujian Study Society and also joined the Society to Protect the State. Rong Lu, formerly Fuzhou general and a patron of Fujian scholars, took Lin onto his staff when he went to Tianjin. Soon recommended as talent, he was summoned to audience; speaking in his Fujian dialect, he was not fully understood. He drafted a memorial afterward that the emperor praised; he was ordered to join Tan Sitong and the others in confidential duties, and many edicts were drafted by him. When the coup came he was executed with the others at twenty-four. He wrote the Late Emerald Studio Poetry Collection. His wife, a granddaughter of Shen Baozhen, took poison on hearing the news but survived, then died of grief. Kang Guangren, personal name Youpu, known by his style name, was Kang Youwei's younger brother. He studied under his elder brother from youth. When Kang Youwei memorialized for reform, Guangren urged changing the examinations first so talent could emerge. After the provincial and metropolitan exams and the eight-legged essay were abolished, annual tests remained unchanged; he urged censors to protest until the throne approved. He then told Kang Youwei, "With examinations abolished, you should return south to build schools and train talent; only after several years can real reform begin." " Kang Youwei could not bring himself to leave. When the coup first broke, he again urged Kang to flee. Kang Youwei escaped; Guangren was arrested. In prison he was calm and cheerful; at execution he said, "China's chance to strengthen itself lies in this!" " Commentary: In the 1898 reform movement Emperor Guangxu, determined to strengthen the state, took the advice of Li Duanfen and others and called in a new generation of officials. The Hundred Days of reform awed China and the world, but factional struggle erupted and triggered the coup. Yang Rui, Liu Guangdi, Tan Sitong, Lin Xu, Yang Shenxiu, and Kang Guangren died on the same day; posterity calls them the Six Gentlemen and mourns their cause. Internal strife continued and spilled into foreign affairs. This led to the anti-foreign Boxer uprising of 1900 and ultimately to national collapse. This was a decisive turning point in the dynasty's fate.

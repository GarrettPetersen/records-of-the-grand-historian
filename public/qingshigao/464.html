Li Duanfen, Xu Zhijing (whose son was Ren Zhu), Chen Baozhen, Huang Zunxian, Zeng He, Yang Shenxiu, Yang Rui, Liu Guangdi, Tan Sitong, Tang Caichang, Lin Xu, and Kang Guangren. Li Duanfen, whose courtesy name was Biyuan, was a native of Guizhu. In the second year of the Guangxu reign he became a jinshi, entered the Hanlin as a junior compiler, and was appointed reviser; Grand Secretary Wo Ren and Minister Luo Dunyan both valued him highly. In the tenth year he was sent out to serve as educational commissioner for Yunnan. This was just after the rebellion, when the frontier districts were still cut off and the previous commissioner had not been able to examine candidates throughout the province. Duanfen now toured each district in person, and learning and culture gradually recovered. In the fifth year he was made a censor, but because his uncle was serving as metropolitan magistrate he had to withdraw for avoidance and was restored to his former post. He rose step by step to become a bachelor of the Grand Secretariat. In the eighteenth year he was appointed vice minister of the Ministry of Punishments. Six years later he was transferred to oversee the grain depots. He held literary office again and again—four times as provincial examiner and once as associate chief examiner for the metropolitan examination—and took pleasure in discovering and promoting talented scholars. While examining candidates in Guangdong he recognized Liang Qichao's ability, gave him his younger cousin in marriage, and thereafter often took Liang's advice, discoursing at length on the political systems of East and West. About this time Kang Youwei submitted memorials calling for reform and for the expansion of schooling. In the twenty-second year Duanfen memorialized for a national university in the capital and schools in every prefecture, department, and county, each with separate halls for study; together with libraries, instrument halls, and translation bureaus, more newspapers, and students selected to travel abroad. In the twenty-fourth year he secretly recommended Kang Youwei and Tan Sitong as men capable of high responsibility. He also pointed out that the regulations of the various ministries were inconsistent and asked that they be revised; the emperor especially approved, and ordered each department head to set a deadline for abolishing old abuses. He was elevated to minister of the Ministry of Rites. Before long Kang Youwei's faction fell; Duanfen reported on himself in a memorial, was stripped of office by edict, and was banished to Xinjiang. He fell ill on the road and was detained at Ganzhou. In the twenty-seventh year he was pardoned and returned home to lecture at the Jingshi Academy. He died in the thirty-third year. In the first year of the Xuantong reign, at the petition of capital officials from Yunnan and Guizhou, his former rank was restored. Xu Zhijing (whose son was Ren Zhu), whose courtesy name was Zijing, was a native of Yixing in Jiangsu and was registered under another domicile. In the second year of the Guangxu reign he became a jinshi, entered the Hanlin as a junior compiler, and was appointed reviser. He rose step by step to reader in waiting of the Hanlin Academy. After his period of mourning for his father ended, he was recalled in the twenty-third year to his former post. He had long been troubled by the daily tightening of foreign pressure and pondered how he might advise the throne. His son Ren Zhu, then a reviser serving as Hunan's educational commissioner, was promoting the new learning and wrote urging his father to recommend Kang Youwei. Zhijing then memorialized: "The nation's course is still unsettled; I beg that Your Majesty's own decision be made clear, so all may know what to follow or reject." In this way he hoped to discern the emperor's true intentions. Before long an edict did call for men of talent; Zhijing then memorialized that Kang Youwei was fit for high office, and also named Liang Qichao, Huang Zunxian, and others. He also sent repeated memorials asking to abolish the eight-legged essay, test candidates on policy essays instead, cut redundant offices, and create appropriate honorary posts. He further argued that with border troubles acute, the empire should train substantial forces, and he strongly recommended Yuan Shikai to take charge of military affairs. The emperor approved all these proposals and ordered them carried out. Minister of Rites Xu Yingkui and others who had blocked memorials to the throne were removed, and Zhijing was ordered to serve as acting vice minister on the right. In the eighth month of the twenty-fourth year the Empress Dowager resumed regency, and all who had taken part in the new policies were punished. Zhijing was stripped of office and imprisoned, soon sentenced to perpetual confinement; Ren Zhu was also dismissed from office. In the gengzi year, when the allied armies occupied the capital, he was released from prison to await judgment and was pardoned by edict. He died at seventy-five. Chen Baozhen, whose courtesy name was Youming, was a native of Yining in Jiangxi. From youth he had strong principles; his poetry and prose were disciplined, and Zeng Guofan valued him. As a provincial graduate he followed his father Weilin in organizing local militia to resist the Taiping rebels. He then went to Hunan and joined Yi Peishen's staff, campaigning between Laifeng and Longshan. When Shi Dakai attacked, the army was hungry and exhausted; Baozhen went to Yongshun to raise grain, supplies kept arriving, the defense held firm, and the rebels gradually withdrew. In Jiangxi he helped Xi Baotian plan the destruction of Hong Fuzhen; when peace returned he was rewarded with the rank of prefect and was exceptionally appointed to the Hebei Circuit. He founded the Zhijing Academy, chose students from three prefectures, and invited distinguished teachers to instruct them. He was transferred to surveillance commissioner in Zhejiang and was dismissed because of a case against him. The Hunan governor recommended his ability; in the sixteenth year he was summoned to the capital, appointed surveillance commissioner in Hubei, and given acting charge of the provincial administration commission. In the twentieth year he was promoted to administration commissioner of Zhili; on audience, with the war in the east pressing hard, he saw the emperor worn with worry and urged him to read daily the Kangxi Emperor's annotated Book of Changes, so that in change he might not lose his footing. His other memorials were numerous, and all pleased the emperor. The emperor regarded him as loyal and ordered him to manage the grain depots and to submit memorials on folded reports reserved for the throne. When the Treaty of Shimonoseki was concluded he wept and said: "The state is nearly lost! The next year, on Rong Lu's recommendation, he was promoted to governor of Hunan. On the governor's staff was Ren Lin, who had formed a faction for private gain; as soon as Baozhen arrived he punished him severely. The Zhili administration commissioner Wang Lian interceded for him; Baozhen reported this to the throne, and Lian was punished. When he re-examined the case against Shi Nianzu, he fully exposed how Nianzu had appointed unfit men, and Nianzu was stripped of office. From this he gained a reputation for uncompromising uprightness. Hunan had long been conservative and inward; Baozhen wished to make his province a model of wealth and strength for the southeast. He set up telegraph lines, steam launches, and a firearms factory, and also founded a defense bureau, the Southern Study Society, and the Current Affairs Academy. He brought in Liang Qichao to direct Hunan learning, and local custom was transformed. He also memorialized on rectifying scholarship, drilling troops, and raising funds; the emperor approved all and told him to hold to his views and not be swayed by loose talk, and issued a special edict praising him. At that time Zhang Zhidong enjoyed great fame, and all circuit and prefectural officials watched in silence. When Baozhen first held the Hubei administration commission, whenever they disagreed he alone argued with Zhidong without private bias; though Zhidong was displeased, he could do nothing. In time the two became closely allied; memorials on the new policies were always signed jointly, while the Hubei governor Tan Jixun would not join them. About this time Kang Youwei's memorials repeatedly had effect. He had long admired Zeng Guofan and Hu Linyi for promoting talent, and now memorialized that Yang Rui, Liu Guangdi, Tan Sitong, and Lin Xu should assist the new policies. The emperor had just called for men of adaptable talent; the four were abruptly promoted to capital grand secretaries and took part in the new policies, and thenceforth spoke on current affairs without reserve. Baozhen also said that though the four had talent, their standing was light and they might take office too lightly; he wished that a weighty senior minister such as Zhang Zhidong might lead them. The memorial had scarcely gone up when the Empress Dowager resumed regency; the four capital grand secretaries were executed, guilt reached the man who had recommended them, Baozhen was removed, his son Sanli was also dismissed, and the books written for Hunan studies were all destroyed. Earlier, the dismissed circuit intendant Zhou Han of Ningxiang had been punished by the governor for posting broadsheets attacking Western religion. When Baozhen arrived, Han again printed and circulated posters; Baozhen ordered them destroyed, Han beat those who destroyed them, Baozhen was angry and had him imprisoned. The old faction hated him to the marrow, yet men who favored reform also praised him warmly for this. After he left, every institution he had built for the people's benefit, though their good effects were already clear, was abolished until not one remained. He died at seventy. Huang Zunxian, whose courtesy name was Gongdu, was a native of Jiaying Prefecture. As a provincial graduate he bought office and became a circuit intendant. He served as attaché on a mission to Japan and wrote the Record of Japan, which he presented to the court. He was soon transferred to serve as consul-general at San Francisco. American officials once used sanitation as a pretext and crowded Chinese sojourners into prison until it was full. Zunxian went straight to the prison and had his attendants measure its capacity, saying: "Is sanitation here really better for health than where these people live?" The officials apologized and released them immediately. He served on the Changbao salt circuit and held acting charge of the surveillance commission. At that time Chen Baozhen was governor and was carrying out the new policies; Zunxian was the first to advocate popular self-government to the people, saying: "It is simply to govern oneself and govern one's own township." From one township extend to one county, one prefecture, one province, and then to the whole country—thus the highest form of republican government may be reached and the grand path of Great Unity attained. Thereupon, roughly imitating Western police patrols, he established a defense bureau: whatever served the people's welfare and could be undertaken by local effort was placed under it, led by men of local standing while officials supplemented where they fell short. Soon he resigned and received orders to go abroad as envoy; before he could depart the factional disaster broke out, and he was dismissed and returned home. He wrote works including the Draft Poems from the Human Realm Studio and others. Zeng He, whose courtesy name was Huaiqing, of the Xitala clan, was a bondservant of the Plain White Banner. His father Qingyun was general at Ningxia. By hereditary privilege he became a director in the Ministry of Works, rose to bureau director, served as clerk in the Grand Council, and was transferred to censor. In the ninth year he was appointed grain transport commissioner in Shaanxi. In Xi'an and Tongzhou and their districts peasants paying grain tax were required to deliver to the provincial granary; the roads were long and abuses many; he devised methods to clear them up, and the people welcomed the change. Scholarly custom in the capital region was plain and narrow and the arts poorly cultivated; he invited Bo Jingwei and Liu Guangzan to direct the Guanzhong Academy, supervised practical learning, and scholarly opinion united behind him. He also established a sericulture bureau, hired weaving masters to teach boiling, reeling, weaving, and dyeing, and each year produced silk and cloth rivaling Shandong and Henan. In the thirteenth year he was made surveillance commissioner. The next year he resigned to observe mourning for his mother. When mourning ended he was recalled to his former post and soon promoted to administration commissioner in Gansu. In the twenty-fourth year he was transferred to Zhili but withdrew for avoidance and remained in his present post. He was promoted to governor of Hubei and said with feeling: "The times are hard to this point—can we still cling to established rules and refuse to change?" Thereupon, using the Shaan-Gan governor-general's seal, he memorialized on filling offices, drawing lots, revenue, and litigation, urging that established precedents be flexibly changed to strengthen the state. Reader in waiting Yi Gu and vice minister Zhang Zhongxin impeached him for disordering government, and an edict stripped him of office. At first, when he held office in the capital, his family was extremely poor and he rented a mean dwelling. When he served in the provinces he worked tirelessly at the people's affairs and never troubled himself over his own means. After he was dismissed he went everywhere on foot, and the people of Shaanxi regularly pooled money each year to support him. Later he grew still poorer, until he wore ragged clothes and sold divination in the capital markets. He died soon after. When the reign title was changed, Governor-General Duan Fang memorialized to restore his former rank. Yang Shenxiu, whose courtesy name was Yicun and whose original name was Yuxiu, was a native of Wenxi in Shanxi. In youth he was quick and clever and was versed in both Chinese and Western mathematics. At first, as a provincial graduate he purchased office and became an assistant department director in the Ministry of Punishments. On leave at home he encountered a great famine; Yan Jingming was charged with organizing relief, and Shenxiu memorialized on reforming the corvée system, so distress was somewhat eased. In the fifteenth year he passed the jinshi examination, was promoted from his present post to bureau director, and was transferred to censor. He once said: "The times are perilously pressing; without reforming the old there is no way to seek the new, and without changing the laws there is no way to seek survival." In the twenty-fourth year the Russians compelled the cession of Port Arthur and Dalian Bay. Shenxiu strongly urged allying with Britain and Japan to resist them, in language that was very earnest and direct. At that time the court was keen to carry out the new policies, but high ministers often raised objections. He and Xu Zhijing memorialized in succession to settle the national policy; he also argued that the examination system was flawed and asked that Song, Yuan, and Ming precedents be consulted to rectify literary forms; the matter was referred to the Ministry of Rites, whose minister Xu Yingkui disapproved but did not report upward. When the special examination in political economy was discussed with a view to reducing the quota, Shenxiu and Song Bolu jointly impeached Xu for obstruction. The emperor ordered Xu to explain himself; when his memorial went up he impeached Kang Youwei for currying favor at key posts and asked that he be dismissed, implicating Shenxiu as well, but the emperor did not pursue it. Censor Wen Ti impeached Shenxiu for spreading Kang Youwei's Society to Preserve the Nation and exposed Kang's communications inside and outside the court; the emperor rebuked Wen for retaliating on another's behalf, and Wen himself was blamed. Shenxiu was all the more stirred and sent repeated memorials requesting a translation bureau, the dispatch of princes and dukes to travel abroad, and regulations for study overseas; all were approved. He also asked to examine ordinary officials, twenty each day in rotation, to select the upright and weed out the mediocre, foolish, and aged who were ignorant of current affairs; from this court ministers watched him all the more askance. Governor Chen Baozhen of Hunan was governing with great urgency and was slandered; Shenxiu explained the matter for him, and the emperor issued a special edict praising Baozhen, who was then able to carry out his aims. In the eighth month the coup occurred; the whole court was fearful, dreading mass executions, but Shenxiu alone submitted a defiant memorial asking the Empress Dowager to return power. Before the memorial went up, his son Futian earnestly admonished him to stop; Shenxiu sternly shouted at him to withdraw. Soon he was arrested and sentenced to public execution. He was blunt and upright by nature and often rebuked others to their faces, which won him many enemies. He held censorial office for ten months and submitted more than twenty sealed memorials; the drafts do not all survive, but three poems written in prison have circulated. He wrote works including Drafts from the Hall of Empty Voices and the New Gazetteer of Wenxi County. Yang Rui, whose courtesy name was Shujiao, was a native of Mianzhu in Sichuan. In youth he was clever and gifted; Educational Commissioner Zhang Zhidong admired his talent and took him into his staff. He studied at the Zunjing Academy, was the youngest in his class, and often ranked first. As an outstanding tribute student he passed the capital examination and received appointment as district magistrate. When Zhidong was governor of the two Guang provinces, Rui followed him to Guangdong. In the eleventh year he passed the Shuntian provincial examination and was selected as a secretary in the Grand Secretariat. In the twenty-fourth year Zhidong recommended him to sit for the special examination in political economy. Chen Baozhen also recommended him, and together with Liu Guangdi, Tan Sitong, and Lin Xu he was given the rank of fourth-grade grand secretary, appointed clerk in the Grand Council, and took part in the new policies. On audience he stated in person that promoting education and drilling troops were strategies to save the state, and pleased the emperor. In the seventh month Director Wang Zhao of the Ministry of Rites submitted a sealed memorial, but Ministers Xu Yingkui and others blocked it and did not present it. When the emperor heard of it he was greatly angered and stripped all six ministers and vice ministers of office; court officials were all ill at ease. The emperor personally wrote a secret edict to Rui saying: "Recently I have observed the Empress Dowager's intentions: she does not wish to remove these aged, muddled ministers and advance brave and penetrating men, nor does she wish to change the laws entirely." Do I not know that long weakness cannot be roused without vigorously carrying out the new policies? Yet at this moment my power is not enough; if I force reform through, I cannot even keep my throne. You and Liu Guangdi, Tan Sitong, Lin Xu, and the rest must deliberate fully on how outstanding men can be advanced so that the new policies may be carried out in time without offending the Empress Dowager; memorialize at once and await my choice. I am overcome with anxious distress! Rui memorialized again: "The Empress Dowager personally bestowed the throne upon Your Majesty; Your Majesty should put filial piety before all under Heaven and yield to circumstances in affairs." Reform should proceed in stages, and the advancement or dismissal of high ministers should not be too sudden. The emperor approved. Before long the Empress Dowager resumed regency, and all who had spoken for the new policies were given heavy punishment. After he was imprisoned he reckoned himself truly innocent and thought that once questioned he would easily be cleared; the next day an edict suddenly ordered him executed in the marketplace together with Liu Guangdi and the rest. When the reign title was changed, Rui's son Qingchang submitted the handwritten edict to the Censorate and asked that it be presented on his behalf; only then did it circulate. Liu Guangdi, whose courtesy name was Peicun, was a native of Fushun in Sichuan. In the ninth year of the Guangxu reign he passed the jinshi examination and was appointed director in the Ministry of Punishments. He handled affairs with strict precision; because of a criminal case he offended his superior, withdrew, closed his door to study diligently, and no longer set foot in the yamen. His family had long been poor, yet his nature was incorruptible; except for old friends he would not accept gifts even of courtesy. He was on good terms only with Yang Rui. He was versed in the Offices of Zhou, the Rites, and the Greater and Lesser Records of the Rites. When he was summoned he too was recommended by Chen Baozhen, yet this was not his lifelong wish; he was about to prepare a memorial declining, but Sichuan men holding office in the capital strongly urged him on. One day on audience he strongly stated that the times were perilous, the people distressed, and foreign danger pressing daily; he urgently urged open-minded planning for good government, and the emperor praised him. At that time the channels of remonstrance were broadly opened and officials and people submitted affairs by the hundreds each day; Guangdi spent the whole day annotating approvals or rejections to await the emperor's decision. On retiring he said to those close to him: "I can never bear this burden; I shall soon ask leave to return home at once!" Before a month had passed disaster struck, and he gave himself up to prison. At the execution Associate Grand Secretary Gangyi supervised the beheading; Guangdi exclaimed in surprise: "To be put to death without interrogation—how can this be?" He was ordered to kneel and hear the decree but would not, saying: "By ancestral institution, even for thieves, if at execution they cry out their innocence, they must be re-examined." Even if we are not worth sparing, what of the dignity of the state!" Gangyi was silent and did not answer; when asked again he said: "I am ordered to supervise the execution—that is all I know." The jailers forced him to kneel, but he stood erect as before. Yang Rui called out: "Peicun, kneel! Kneel down! It is only to obey the decree." Only then did he kneel and submit to execution. He wrote the Collected Prose and Poetry of the Jiebai Studio. Tan Sitong (with Tang Caichang), whose courtesy name was Fusheng, was a native of Liuyang in Hunan. His father Jixun was governor of Hubei. In youth he was free-spirited and ambitious; his writing was bold and unrestrained. His learning took daily renewal as its principle and regarded old doctrines of ethics and constancy as scarcely worth a thought. Jixun had always been strict and cautious, and for this Sitong rather disliked him. Sitong then traveled on Liu Jintang's staff in Xinjiang, purchased office as an associate prefect, and was assigned to Jiangsu. When Chen Baozhen became governor of Hunan, Sitong returned home to assist the new policies. Liang Qichao proposed establishing the Southern Study Society, and Sitong became its head. When meetings were held several hundred people regularly gathered; hearing Sitong speak passionately on current affairs, many were moved. In the twenty-fourth year he was summoned to the capital; his audience pleased the emperor, and he was promoted to fourth-grade grand secretary and clerk in the Grand Council. Though the four received the same appointment, whenever they were summoned to audience Sitong's proposals were the most numerous. The emperor wished to open the Maojin Hall and establish advisory officials; he ordered Sitong to draft edicts, which had to cite clearly precedents from former reigns, and was about to go in person to the Summer Palace to request the Empress Dowager's approval. Sitong withdrew and said to others: "Now I know that the emperor has no power at all!" At that time Rong Lu was governor-general of the metropolitan region, and Yuan Shikai was drilling troops at Tianjin as a surveillance commissioner. An edict promoted Yuan Shikai to vice minister and summoned him to audience. Sitong once went to Yuan Shikai by night to discuss matters with him. The next day Yuan returned to Tianjin. The following morning the Empress Dowager returned from the Summer Palace to the Forbidden City and took back political power. Liang Qichao took refuge in the Japanese legation; Sitong went to see him and urged him to flee east. Sitong said: "Without those who go, there is no way to plan for the future; without those who die, there is no way to repay the sage ruler." In the end he did not leave. Before long he was beheaded in the marketplace. He wrote works including Benevolence and the Collected Poems of the Mangcang Studio. Tang Caichang, whose courtesy name was Fochen. In youth he was equally famous with Sitong and was called "the two students of Liuyang"; he was a gifted student of the Two Lakes Academy. When he heard of Sitong's death he was grief-stricken and angry, plotted repeatedly, and whenever he spoke of Emperor Dezong would often weep. In the twenty-sixth year, when the two palaces went on tour, he secretly joined the Society of the Wealthy to plot an uprising in the name of loyal service to the throne and was about to attack Wuchang and Hankou. He was captured, spoke boldly without concealment, asked to die, and was killed. Lin Xu, whose courtesy name was Dungu, was a native of Houguan in Fujian. At nineteen he took first place in his province's civil examination. Later he took the metropolitan examination; when war broke out between China and Japan he rallied fellow candidates to submit a memorial on current affairs, but received no response. He purchased office and became a secretary of the Grand Secretariat. At that time Kang Youwei advocated reform and first established the Guangdong Study Society in the capital to rouse scholarly spirit, and the Sichuan, Zhejiang, Shaanxi, and Fujian study societies followed. Lin became leader of the Fujian Study Society and also joined the Society to Preserve the Nation. Rong Lu had earlier been general at Fuzhou and always favored Fujian scholars; when he reached Tianjin he took Lin into his staff. Soon he was summoned to audience on a memorial recommending talent; he spoke in his local dialect and the emperor did not fully understand. On retiring he drafted a folded memorial; the emperor praised it and ordered him to take part in confidential affairs with Tan Sitong and the rest; many edicts were drafted by Lin. When the coup broke out he was executed in the marketplace with the others, at the age of twenty-four. He wrote the Collected Poems of the Late Emerald Studio. His wife was a granddaughter of Shen Baozhen; when she heard of the disaster she took poison but did not die, and died of grief. Kang Guangren, whose personal name was Youpu and who was known by his courtesy name, was the younger brother of Kang Youwei. As a youth he studied with his elder brother. When Youwei memorialized requesting reform, Guangren argued that the examinations should be reformed first if talent was to emerge. Thereafter the provincial and metropolitan examinations and the eight-legged essay were abolished, but the annual county examination had not yet changed; Guangren urged censors to submit defiant memorials, and the emperor approved. Thereupon Guangren told Youwei: "Now that the examinations are abolished, you should return south for the time being and devote yourself to education, wait until many useful men have been trained, and only after several years speak of reform." Youwei could not bring himself to leave. When he first heard of the coup, Guangren again urged him to flee. Youwei escaped; Guangren was arrested. In prison he spoke and laughed as usual; at the execution he still said: "China's opportunity for self-strengthening lies here!" The commentator says: In the wuxu reform Emperor Dezong, stirred by indignation, sought to strengthen the state, followed the counsel of Li Duanfen and others, and summoned new men to office. For a hundred days the new policies were maintained; China and foreign countries looked on in awe; factional struggle suddenly arose and provoked the coup. Rui, Guangdi, Sitong, Xu, Shenxiu, and Guangren were executed on the same day; the world called them the "Six Gentlemen," and all mourned their purpose. Domestic strife did not end and soon entangled foreign relations. Afterward this led to the anti-foreign turmoil of the gengzi year and in the end brought the dynasty to ruin. This too was a great turning point in the rise and fall of the Qing dynasty.

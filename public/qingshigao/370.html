琦善琦善，字靜庵，博爾濟吉特氏，滿洲正黃旗人。 父成德，熱河都統，以先世格得理爾率屬歸附，世襲一等侯爵。 琦善由廕生授刑部員外郎，累遷通政司副使。 嘉慶十九年，出為河南按察使，歷江寧、河南布政使。 二十四年，擢河南巡撫。 河決馬營壩，偕尚書吳璥督工，甫塞而儀封南岸又決，奪職，予主事銜留工。 尋授河南按察使，調山東。 道光元年，就擢巡撫。 父憂，奪情任事，襲侯爵。 捕治臨清教匪馬進忠，又籌濟高家堰工費八十萬。 五年，京察，詔嘉其明幹有為，能任勞怨，加總督銜。 尋擢兩江總督，兼署漕運總督。 時高堰屢決，淤運阻漕。 琦善請用盤運法，並暫行海運，如議行。 七年，議啟王家營舊減壩，大濬正河，尋以減壩堵合，黃水倒漾，复閉禦黃壩，漕船倒塘灌放，詔斥失機，議革職，寬之，降授內閣學士。 尋复授山東巡撫。 九年，擢四川總督。 十一年，調直隸。 十六年，協辦大學士。 十八年，拜文淵閣大學士，仍留總督任。 琦善久膺疆寄，為宣宗所倚任。 二十年，海疆事急，駐天津籌辦防務。 八月，英兵船至海口，投書乞通商，訴林則徐、鄧廷楨等燒煙啟釁。 琦善招宴英領事義律及兵官，許以代奏。 遂入覲面陳，授欽差大臣，赴廣東查辦。 諭沿海疆吏但防要隘，遇英船毋開砲，義律乃率船回粵。 尋罷則徐、廷楨，命琦善署兩廣總督兼粵海關監督。 密疏臚陳粵事，略曰：「林則徐示令繳煙，許以賞犒，洋人頗存奢望。 迨後每煙一箱，僅給茶葉五斤，所得不及本銀百分之一； 又勒具'再販船貨入官、人即正法'甘結，迄未遵依，此釁所由起也。 當義律具禀繳煙，距撤退買辦五日，非出情原。 時義律僅止孤身，設有黨援，未必降心俯首。 英吉利國王無給林則徐文書之事，惟呂宋國王曾有來文，或因此誤傳。 林則徐稱定海陰濕，洋人病死甚多。 諮查洋人米穀牲畜尚充，疫癘病斃者多水手舵工，頭目死者不過數人。 從前外洋來信，祗言貿易。 自林則徐欲悉外情，多方購求漁利之人，造作播傳，真偽互見，此時紛紛查探，適墮術中。 林則徐奏各國憤英人阻其貿易，美利堅、法蘭西將遣船來與理論。 訪聞各國曾有此說，然迄未見兵船來粵。 前有美國二船，乘英人不備，進口，至今未敢駛出。 畏葸如斯，縱力足頡頏，恐未肯傷其同類。 虎門燒煙時，洋人觀者撰文數千言紀事，事誠有之，語多含譏刺，非心服。 林則徐稱具結之後，查驗他國來船，絕無鴉片。 如指上年而言，事屬以往，船貨無憑； 若指本年而言，來船尚未進口，不能知其有，亦安能信其無？ 」並言將軍阿精阿請團練水勇，及林則徐請鼓勵員弁，俟事定再議。 疏入，報聞，則徐以是獲罪。 時廣東撤水師歸營，猝被敵轟擊，掠去米艇兵丁，巡撫怡良以聞。 琦善又陳：「英人回粵，詞氣傲慢，義律託疾將回國，且兵船日增。 」得旨，仍暫停貿易，一面與議，一面籌防。 義律堅持索還煙價，並增廈門、福州通商，嚴旨拒不許。 十二月，義律見防禦漸撤，數遣挑戰，琦善諭止之。 義律曰：「戰後再議，未為遲也。 」乃犯虎門外沙角、大角兩砲台，副將陳連升力戰死之，遂陷。 提督關天培守靖遠砲台，總兵李廷鈺守威遠砲台，並請援，琦善不敢明發兵，夜遣二百人往。 二十一年正月，事聞，上震怒，下琦善嚴議，命御前大臣貝子奕山為靖逆將軍，戶部尚書隆文、湖南提督楊芳副之，率師赴粵協剿。 義律數索香港，志在必得，琦善當事急，佯許之而不敢上聞。 至是，義律獻出所踞砲台，並原繳還定海以易香港全島，別議通商章程。 琦善親與相見蓮花城定議，往返傳語，由差遣之鮑鵬將事，同城將軍、巡撫皆不預知。 及英人佔踞香港，出示安民，巡撫怡良奏聞，琦善方疏陳：「地勢無可扼，軍械無可恃，兵力不固，民情不堅，如與交鋒，實無把握，不如暫事羈縻。 」上益怒，詔斥琦善擅予香港，擅許通商之罪，褫職逮治，籍沒家產。 英兵遂奪虎門靖遠砲台，提督關天培死之。 奕山等至，戰復不利，廣州危急，許以煙價六百萬兩，圍始解，而福建、浙江復被擾。 琦善逮京，讞論大辟，尋釋之，命赴浙江軍營效力，未至，改發軍台。 二十二年，浙師复敗，吳淞不守，英兵遂入江，江寧戒嚴，於是耆英、伊里布等定和議，海內莫不以罷戰言和歸咎於琦善為作俑之始矣。 是年秋，予四等侍衛，充葉爾羌幫辦大臣。 二十三年，以三品頂戴授熱河都統。 御史陳慶鏞疏論僨事諸臣罪狀，上重違清議，再褫琦善職，意仍鄉用，未幾，予三等侍衛，充駐藏大臣。 二十六年，授四川總督。 二十八年，詔嘉其治蜀於吏治營伍實心整頓，复頭品頂戴。 尋協辦大學士，留總督任。 以平瞻對野番功被議敘。 二十九年，調陝甘總督，兼署青海辦事大臣，剿雍沙番及黑城撒拉回匪。 既而言官劾其妄殺，命都統薩迎阿往按，革職逮問。 咸豐二年，定讞發吉林效力贖罪，尋釋回。 時粵匪已犯湖南，勢日熾，屢易帥皆不能制。 起琦善署河南巡撫，駐防楚、豫界上。 以捐餉加都統銜，授欽差大臣，專辦防務。 湖北省城失守，觀望不能救。 三年春，賊遂連陷安徽、江寧省城，分擾鎮江、揚州，命琦善偕直隸提督陳金​​綬防江北。 三月，連敗賊於浦口雷塘，進剿揚州，分屯寶塔山、司徒廟，五戰皆捷。 秋，破浦口援賊，合圍揚州。 十二月，賊突圍出竄瓜洲，以收復揚州入告，詔斥勇潰縱賊，責令進剿瓜洲、儀徵，儀徵克復。 四年夏，連戰金川、瓜洲、三汊河，屢奏斬獲。 自琦善與向榮分主大江南北軍事，攻戰年餘，鎮江、瓜洲迄未克復，無得力水師，不能扼賊，琦善雖議增水師，亦未果。 是年秋，卒於軍，贈太子太保、協辦大學士，依總督例賜卹，諡文勤。 恭鏜子恭鏜，黑龍江將軍。 孫瑞洵，烏里雅蘇台參贊大臣； 瑞澂，兩湖總督。 瑞澂自有傳。 伊里布伊里布，字莘農，鑲黃旗紅帶子。 嘉慶六年進士，授國子監學正，改補典簿。 出為雲南府南關通判，署澂江知府，遷騰越知州。 二十四年，總督伯麟薦其熟練邊務，能馭土司，治緬匪有功，以應升用。 道光元年，從總督慶保剿平永北大姚夷匪，賜花翎，署永昌知府。 擢安徽太平知府。 歷山西冀寧道，浙江按察使，湖北、浙江布政使。 五年，擢陝西巡撫，調山東。 丁父憂，署雲南巡撫。 服闋，乃實授。 時阮元為總督，伊里布和而廉，有政聲。 回疆兵事起，自請從軍，詔斥不諳回情，妄行陳奏，奪職留任，尋復之。 十三年，擢雲貴總督。 京察，以久任邊疆，鎮撫得宜，被議敘。 十八年，協辦大學士，留總督任。 四川綦江奸民穆繼賢仇殺貴州仁懷武生趙應彩，遂糾眾踞方家溝為亂，伊里布率提督余步雲、布政使慶祿等破其巢，斬獲千餘，誅賊首穆繼賢、謝法真等，餘匪悉平，賜雙眼花翎。 十九年，調兩江總督。 二十年秋，英兵陷定海，命為欽差大臣，赴浙江查辦。 時已有論致寇由斷絕貿易燒煙起釁者，密諭察訪確情毋回護。 尋以琦善代林則徐，命沿海遇敵勿擊。 伊里布初至浙，駐鎮海籌防，疏報擊沉敵船，有所擒獲，命慰諭英人攻擊出於誤會，促令退兵交地，俘虜俟敵退釋還。 伊里布遣家丁張喜偕員弁赴定海犒師，英人亦答餽，奏聞，諭卻勿受。 請增調安徽、兩湖兵，允之。 裕謙方代署兩江總督，疏言：「各省皆可議守，獨浙江必應速戰。 」且言：「定海西境岑港為第一險要，應以精兵先據之。 」下伊里布體察辦理。 既而琦善在粵議款不得要領，兵端又開，二十一年正月，詔促伊里布進兵規复定海。 二月，義律既踞香港，盡調英船赴粵，以交還定海告。 詔斥附和琦善，以兵砲未集，藉詞緩攻，致敵船遁去，褫協辦大學士、雙眼花翎，暫留兩江總督任，以裕謙代為欽差大臣督浙師。 裕謙論劾伊里布遣家丁赴敵船事，命解任，帶張喜來京，下刑部訊鞫，褫職，遣戍軍台。 未幾，定海、鎮海、寧波相繼陷，裕謙殉之。 二十二年春，揚威將軍奕經援浙，復挫敗。 巡撫劉韻珂疏陳浙事危急，薦伊里布無急功近名之心，為一時僅見，請發軍營效力贖罪。 於是予七品頂戴，隨杭州將軍耆英赴浙，密諭相機辦理。 及英兵犯乍浦，耆英遣往設計退兵。 五月，署乍浦副都統，復令張喜傳語，英兵遂去乍浦，犯吳淞，由海入江，鎮江失守。 伊里布奉命偕耆英赴江寧議和，事詳耆英傳。 和議既成，英兵退，約於廣東議稅則，命偕耆英詳慎酌商，授廣州將軍、欽差大臣，辦理善後事宜。 二十三年，至粵，見民心不服，夷情狡橫，憂悴。 踰月病卒，贈太子太保，諡文敏。 宗室耆英宗室耆英，字介春，隸正藍旗。 父祿康，嘉慶間官東閣大學士。 耆英以廕生授宗人府主事，遷理事官。 累擢內閣學士，兼副都統、護軍統​​領。 道光二年，遷理籓院侍郎，調兵部。 四年，送宗室閒散移駐雙城堡。 五年，授內務府大臣，歷工部、戶部。 七年，授步軍統領。 九年，擢禮部尚書，管理太常寺、鴻​​臚寺、太醫院，兼都統。 十二年，畿輔旱，疏請察吏省刑，嘉納之，授內大臣。 十四年，以管理步軍統領勤事，被議敘。 歷工部、戶部尚書。 十五年，以相度龍泉峪萬年吉地，加太子少保。 命赴廣東、江西按事。 十七年，內監張道忠犯賭博，耆英瞻徇釋放，事覺，降兵部侍郎。 尋出為熱河都統。 十八年，授盛京將軍。 詔嚴禁鴉片，無論宗室、覺羅，按律懲治。 疏請旗民十家聯保，以憑稽察。 二十年，海疆戒嚴，疏請旅順口為水路衝衢，當扼要籌備。 英船入奉天洋面，先後游弋山海關、秦皇島等處，錦州、山海關皆設防。 二十二年正月，粵事急，琦善既黜，調耆英廣州將軍，授欽差大臣，督辦浙江洋務。 因御史蘇廷魁奏英吉利為鄰國所破，詔促耆英赴廣州本任，乘機進剿，尋知其訛傳，仍留浙江。 五月，吳淞失守，命偕伊里布赴江蘇相機籌辦。 英兵已入江，越圌山關，陷鎮江，踞瓜洲，耆英與揚威將軍奕經先後奏請羈縻招撫。 七月，英兵薄江寧下關，伊里布先至，英人索煙價、商欠、戰費共二千一百萬兩，廣州、福州、廈門、寧波、上海五港通商，英官與中國官員用平行禮，及劃抵關稅、釋放漢奸等款。 越三日，耆英至，稍稍駁詰之。 英兵突張紅旗，置砲鍾山上臨城，急止之，遣侍衛咸齡、江寧布政使恩彤、寧紹台道鹿澤良，偕伊里布家丁張喜，詣英舟，許據情奏聞。 宣宗憤甚，大學士穆彰阿以糜餉勞師無效、剿與撫費亦相等為言，乃允之。 耆英等與英將濮鼎查、馬利遜會盟於儀鳳門外靜海寺，同籤條約，先予六百萬，餘分三年給，和議遂成。 九月，英兵盡數駛出吳淞，授兩江總督，命籌辦通商及浙江、福建因地制宜之策。 二十三年，授欽差大臣，赴廣東議通商章程，就粵海關稅則分別增減，各口按新例一體開關，臚列整頓稅務條款，下廷議施行。 又奏美利堅、法蘭西等國一體通商，允之。 美國請入京瞻覲，卻不許。 二十四年，調授兩廣總督，兼辦通商事宜。 二十五年，協辦大學士，留總督任。 比利時、丹麥等國請通商，命體察約束。 二十六年，京察，以殫心竭慮坐鎮海疆，被議敘。 疏上練兵事宜，繕呈唐臣陸贄守備事宜狀，請下各將軍督撫置諸座右。 英國請於西藏定界通商，諭耆英堅守成約，毋為搖惑。 故事，廣東洋商居住澳門，貿易有定界，赴洋行發貨，不得擅入省城。 自江寧和議有省城設立棧房及領事入城之約，粵民猶持舊例，愬於大吏，不省，乃舉團練，眾議洶洶，不受官吏約束。 二十三年，濮鼎查將入城，粵民不可，逡巡去。 二十五年，英船復至，耆英遣廣州知府餘保純詣商，粵民鼓譟，安撫乃罷。 英人以登岸每遭窘辱，貽書大吏誚讓，群情憤激，不可曉諭。 至二十七年，英船突入省河，要求益堅，耆英謾許兩年後踐約，始退，自請議處。 諭嚴為防備，務出萬全。 耆英知終必有釁。 二十八年，請入覲，留京供職，賜雙眼花翎，管理禮部、兵部，兼都統。 尋拜文淵閣大學士，命赴山東查辦鹽務，校閱浙江營伍。 三十年，文宗即位，應詔陳言，略曰：「求治莫先於用人、理財、行政諸大端。 用人之道，明試以功。 人有剛柔，才有長短。 用違其才，君子亦恐誤事； 用得其當，小人亦能濟事。 設官分職，非為眾人藏身之地。 實心任事者，雖小人當保全； 不肯任怨者，雖君子當委置。 行政在於得人，迂腐之說，無裨時務，泥古之論，難合機宜，財非人不理。 今賦額四千餘萬，支用有餘，不能如額，以致短絀。 致絀之由，非探本窮源，不能通盤清釐。 與其正賦外別費經營，不如於正賦中覈實籌畫。 」疏入，特諭曰：「身為端揆，一言一動，舉朝所矜式。 耆英率意敷陳，持論過偏，顯違古訓，流弊曷可勝言。 」傳旨申飭。 耆英不自安，屢稱病。 是年十月，上手詔揭示穆彰阿及耆英罪狀，斥「耆英在廣東抑民奉夷，謾許入城，幾致不測之變。 數面陳夷情可畏，應事周旋，但圖常保祿位。 穆彰阿暗而難明，耆英顯而易見，貽害國家，其罪則一」。 猶念其迫於時勢，從寬降為部屬。 尋補工部員外郎。 咸豐三年，粵匪北犯，耆英子馬蘭鎮總兵慶錫奏請父子兄弟同赴軍前，命耆英隨巡防王大臣效力，以捐餉予四品頂戴。 五年，慶錫向屬員借貸被劾，耆英坐私告，革職圈禁。 八年，英人糾合法、美、俄諸國兵船犯天津，爭改條約，命大學士桂良、尚書花沙納扆往查辦。 巡防王大臣薦耆英熟悉情形，召對，自陳原力任其難，予侍郎銜，赴天津協議。 初耆英之在廣東也，五口通商事多由裁決，一意遷就。 七年冬，廣州陷，檔案為英人所得，譯出耆英章奏，多掩飾不實，深惡之。 及至天津，英人拒不見，惶恐求去，不候旨，回通州，於是欺謾之跡益彰，為王大臣論劾，嚴詔逮治，賜自盡。 論曰：罷戰言和，始發於琦善，去備媚敵，致敗之由。 伊里布有忍辱負重之心，無安危定傾之略，且廟謨未定，廷議紛紜，至江寧城下之盟，乃與耆英結束和議，損威喪權，貽害莫挽。 耆英獨任善後，留廣州入城之隙，兵釁再開，浸致庚申之禍。 三人者同受惡名，而耆英不保其身命，宜哉。
Qi Shan. Qi Shan, whose courtesy name was Jing'an, belonged to the Borjigit clan and came from the Manchu Plain Yellow Banner. His father Chengde served as military governor of Rehe. An ancestor named Gedeli'er had led their followers in submission to the dynasty, and the family held an hereditary first-class marquisate. Qi Shan began his career through the yinsheng privilege as an assistant director in the Ministry of Justice and rose step by step to vice commissioner of the Office of Transmission. In Jiaqing 19 he was appointed Henan provincial judicial commissioner and later served as provincial treasurer in Jiangning and Henan. In the twenty-fourth year of the reign he was promoted to governor of Henan. When the Yellow River burst at Ma Ying Dam, he worked with Minister Wu Jing to supervise repairs. The breach had barely been closed when the south bank at Yifeng gave way again. He lost his post but was given a secretary's rank and kept on the works. He was soon appointed Henan provincial judicial commissioner and then transferred to Shandong. In Daoguang 1 he was promoted in place to provincial governor. During mourning for his father he was allowed to stay in office rather than retire, and he succeeded to the family marquisate. He captured and punished the Linqing religious rebel Ma Jinzhong and raised eight hundred thousand taels for repairs to the Gaojia Weir. In the fifth year of the reign, the capital inspection brought an edict praising his clear judgment, effective action, and willingness to bear hardship and blame, and he was given governor-general rank. He was soon promoted to governor-general of Liangjiang and concurrently served as grain transport commissioner. The Gaoji Weir was breaching repeatedly, silt was choking the channel, and grain transport was at a standstill. Qi Shan proposed cart transport and a temporary shift to sea transport, and both measures were adopted. In the seventh year he proposed reopening the old relief dam at Wangjiaying and dredging the main channel. The relief dam was soon closed again, Yellow River water backed up, the blocking dam was shut, and tribute barges had to be filled by reverse irrigation. The court rebuked him for missing his chance, considered dismissing him, but showed leniency and demoted him to Grand Secretariat academician. He was soon reappointed governor of Shandong. In the ninth year he was promoted to governor-general of Sichuan. In the eleventh year he was transferred to Zhili. In the sixteenth year he was made associate grand secretary. In the eighteenth year he was appointed grand secretary of the Hall of Literary Deepness while retaining his post as governor-general. Qi Shan had long held important frontier posts and enjoyed the deep trust of Emperor Daoguang. In the twentieth year, as the coastal crisis intensified, he was posted to Tianjin to organize the defenses. In the eighth month British warships reached Haikou with a letter requesting trade and accusing Lin Zexu, Deng Tingzhen, and others of provoking war by burning opium. Qi Shan entertained the British consul Charles Elliot and his officers and promised to submit their petition to the throne. He then had an audience with the emperor, was appointed imperial commissioner, and sent to Guangdong to investigate. Coastal officials were ordered to hold only the key passes and not to fire on British ships, whereupon Elliot led his fleet back to Guangdong. Lin Zexu and Deng Tingzhen were soon dismissed, and Qi Shan was ordered to serve as acting governor-general of the two Guang provinces and superintendent of Guangdong customs. In a secret memorial he laid out the Guangdong situation at length, writing in summary: 'Lin Zexu publicly ordered the surrender of opium and promised rewards and compensation, and the foreigners came to expect much. Later they received only five jin of tea for each chest of opium—less than one percent of its silver value; they were also forced to sign a pledge that anyone who smuggled again would forfeit ship and cargo to the state and be executed on the spot, and since this was never accepted, the conflict began here. When Elliot formally offered to surrender the opium, it was only five days after the compradors had been withdrawn—not a gesture of genuine goodwill. At the time Elliot stood entirely alone; had he had allies at his back, he might never have submitted so meekly. The king of England had sent no letter to Lin Zexu; only the king of Luzon had once written, and the story may have arisen from that confusion. Lin Zexu reported that Dinghai was damp and unhealthy and that many foreigners had died of disease there. Inquiries showed that the foreigners still had ample provisions and livestock; most epidemic deaths were among sailors and helmsmen, and only a few officers had died. Earlier letters from abroad had spoken only of trade. Once Lin Zexu set out to learn everything about foreign affairs, he paid all sorts of opportunists to manufacture and spread intelligence, mixing truth with falsehood. The frantic inquiries of the moment played straight into their hands. Lin Zexu reported that other nations resented Britain for blocking their trade and that the United States and France would send warships to protest. Inquiries confirmed that such talk had circulated, yet no foreign warships had appeared at Guangdong. Two American ships had slipped into port while the British were off guard and still dared not sail out. So timid were they that even if strong enough to stand up to the British, they would hardly strike at their own kind. When opium was burned at Humen, foreign onlookers wrote lengthy accounts of the event. The event was real enough, but their language was mostly sarcastic—they were far from convinced. Lin Zexu claimed that once the pledges were signed, inspections of other nations' ships found no opium at all. If he meant the previous year, that was already past and there was no way to verify ships or cargo; if he meant this year, the ships had not yet entered port—how could one know they carried opium, much less swear they did not? ' He also reported that General A Jing'a had asked to organize river militia, and that Lin Zexu's proposal to reward officers should wait until the crisis was over. When the memorial reached the throne, Lin Zexu was punished on its account. Guangdong had just withdrawn its naval forces to barracks when the enemy suddenly opened fire, seized grain boats, and captured soldiers. Governor Yi Liang reported the attack. Qi Shan reported further: 'The British returned to Guangdong speaking arrogantly. Elliot claimed illness and said he would go home, while warships were arriving daily. ' The court ordered trade to remain suspended for now while negotiations continued and defenses were strengthened. Elliot insisted on compensation for the destroyed opium and on opening Xiamen and Fuzhou to trade, but stern orders from the throne refused both demands. In the twelfth month Elliot saw defenses being drawn down and sent repeated challenges. Qi Shan ordered his men not to respond. Elliot said, 'We can talk again after we fight—it will not be too late. ' He then attacked the Shajiao and Dajiao forts outside Humen. Vice commander Chen Liansheng fought to the death, and both forts fell. Regional commander Guan Tianpei held Jingyuan Fort and brigade commander Li Tingyu held Weiyuan Fort. Both asked for help, but Qi Shan dared not send troops openly and sent only two hundred men by night. When news reached the court in the first month of the twenty-first year, the emperor was furious. Qi Shan was ordered severely punished. Prince Yishan was named pacification commander, with Minister of Revenue Long Wen and Hunan regional commander Yang Fang as his deputies, and they were sent to Guangdong with an army. Elliot repeatedly demanded Hong Kong and meant to have it. With the crisis pressing, Qi Shan pretended to agree but did not dare tell the throne. Elliot now offered to give up the forts he held and originally proposed returning Dinghai in exchange for the whole of Hong Kong, with trade terms to be negotiated separately. Qi Shan met Elliot personally at Lotus City to settle terms, messages passing back and forth through the envoy Bao Peng. The garrison general and provincial governor in the same city knew nothing of it. When the British occupied Hong Kong and issued proclamations to calm the populace, Governor Yi Liang reported it to the throne. Only then did Qi Shan explain: 'The terrain cannot be held, our arms are unreliable, our troops are weak, and the people are unsettled. We have no real chance in a fight—it is better to appease them for now. ' The emperor's anger only grew. An edict condemned Qi Shan for ceding Hong Kong and allowing trade without authorization, stripped him of office, ordered his arrest, and confiscated his property. British forces then took Jingyuan Fort at Humen, and regional commander Guan Tianpei was killed. When Yishan and his forces arrived, the fighting went badly again. Guangzhou was in grave danger. Six million taels in opium compensation secured a lifting of the siege, but Fujian and Zhejiang were raided anew. Qi Shan was brought to the capital and sentenced to death. He was soon released and ordered to serve in the Zhejiang army, but before he arrived he was sent instead to a military penal station. In the twenty-second year the Zhejiang forces were beaten again, Wusong fell, British troops entered the Yangzi, and Jiangning went on alert. Qiying, Yi Libu, and others then negotiated peace, and across the empire the decision to stop fighting and sue for peace was blamed on Qi Shan as the man who had set it all in motion. That autumn he was given fourth-class imperial bodyguard rank and appointed assistant commissioner at Yarkand. In the twenty-third year he was appointed military governor of Rehe with third-rank insignia. Censor Chen Qingyong memorialized listing the faults of the ministers who had bungled the crisis. The emperor, going sharply against public opinion, stripped Qi Shan of office again, though still intending to use him later. Before long Qi Shan was given third-class bodyguard rank and appointed resident commissioner in Tibet. In the twenty-sixth year he was appointed governor-general of Sichuan. In the twenty-eighth year an edict praised his earnest reform of civil administration and the army in Sichuan and restored his first-rank insignia. He soon became associate grand secretary while keeping his post as governor-general. He was recommended for honors for pacifying the tribal peoples of Zhandui. In the twenty-ninth year he was transferred to governor-general of Shaanxi-Gansu and concurrently served as Qinghai commissioner, campaigning against the Yusha tribes and the Heicheng Salar rebels. Censors then impeached him for indiscriminate killing. Banner general Saying'a was sent to investigate, and Qi Shan was dismissed and arrested. In Xianfeng 2 he was sentenced to serve in Jilin to atone for his crimes and was soon released. Southern rebels had already invaded Hunan, their strength growing daily. Commanders were changed again and again, yet none could check them. Qi Shan was recalled to serve as acting governor of Henan, posted on the border between Huguang and Henan. For contributing military funds he was given banner general rank and appointed imperial commissioner with sole charge of the defenses. When the Hubei provincial capital fell, he held back and could not relieve it. In the spring of the third year the rebels took the capitals of Anhui and Jiangning in succession and raided Zhenjiang and Yangzhou. Qi Shan was ordered to join Zhili regional commander Chen Jinshou in defending the north bank of the Yangzi. In the third month he beat the rebels repeatedly at Pukou and Leitang, advanced against Yangzhou, and encamped at Baota Hill and Situgong Temple, winning five engagements in a row. In autumn he routed rebel reinforcements at Pukou and closed the ring around Yangzhou. In the twelfth month the rebels broke out and fled to Guazhou. Qi Shan reported the recovery of Yangzhou, but an edict rebuked his troops for letting the enemy escape and ordered him to pursue them at Guazhou and Yizheng. Yizheng was retaken. In the summer of the fourth year he fought repeatedly at Jinchuan, Guazhou, and San Chahe and reported many kills and captures. Once Qi Shan and Xiang Rong divided command of the armies north and south of the Yangzi, fighting dragged on for more than a year. Zhenjiang and Guazhou remained untaken. Without a capable navy they could not block the rebels, and though Qi Shan proposed building one up, that too came to nothing. That autumn he died in camp. He was posthumously made Senior Guardian of the Heir Apparent and associate grand secretary, granted mourning honors befitting a governor-general, and given the posthumous name Wenqin. Gongtang. His son Gongtang served as general of Heilongjiang. His grandson Ruixun served as commissioner at Uliastai; Ruicheng served as governor-general of the two Hu provinces. Ruicheng has his own biography. Yi Libu. Yi Libu, whose courtesy name was Xinnong, was a 'red girdle' descendant of the Bordered Yellow Banner. He passed the jinshi examination in Jiaqing 6, was appointed director of studies at the Imperial Academy, and later transferred to a clerical post. He was posted as sub-prefect at Yunnan's southern gate, served as acting prefect of Chengjiang, and was promoted to magistrate of Tengyue. In the twenty-fourth year Governor-General Bo Lin recommended him as experienced in frontier affairs, skilled at managing native chiefs, and successful against Burmese rebels, and he was promoted accordingly. In Daoguang 1 he followed Governor-General Qingbao in suppressing the Yongbei Dayao tribal rebels, was awarded the peacock feather, and served as acting prefect of Yongchang. He was promoted to prefect of Taiping in Anhui. He served successively as Jining circuit intendant in Shanxi, Zhejiang provincial judicial commissioner, and provincial treasurer in Hubei and Zhejiang. In the fifth year he was promoted to governor of Shaanxi and then transferred to Shandong. While mourning his father he served as acting governor of Yunnan. When mourning ended he received the appointment in full. While Ruan Yuan served as governor-general, Yi Libu was known as conciliatory and upright, and enjoyed a reputation for sound administration. When fighting broke out on the Muslim frontier, he volunteered for the campaign. An edict rebuked him for not understanding frontier conditions and for rash memorializing, stripped him of rank while keeping him in post, and soon restored it. In the thirteenth year he was promoted to governor-general of Yunnan and Guizhou. At the capital inspection he was recommended for honors for his long service on the frontier and effective pacification. In the eighteenth year he was made associate grand secretary while keeping his post as governor-general. Mu Jixian, a rogue of Qijiang in Sichuan, killed Zhao Yingcai, a military licentiate of Renhuai in Guizhou, in a feud, then rallied a mob and seized Fangjiagou in revolt. Yi Libu led regional commander Yu Buyun, provincial treasurer Qing Lu, and others to destroy their stronghold, killing and capturing more than a thousand. The rebel leaders Mu Jixian and Xie Fazhen were executed and the rest of the bandits were pacified. He was awarded the double-eyed peacock feather. In the nineteenth year he was transferred to governor-general of Liangjiang. In the autumn of the twentieth year, after British troops took Dinghai, he was appointed imperial commissioner and sent to Zhejiang to investigate. Some already argued that the enemy had been provoked by cutting off trade and burning opium. A secret instruction ordered him to investigate the facts without shielding anyone. Qi Shan soon replaced Lin Zexu, and coastal officials were ordered not to engage the enemy. When Yi Libu first reached Zhejiang he was posted at Zhenhai to organize the defenses. He reported sinking enemy ships and taking captives. The court ordered him to assure the British that the attack was a misunderstanding, urge them to withdraw and return occupied territory, and release prisoners once they did. Yi Libu sent his servant Zhang Xi with officers to Dinghai to reward the troops. The British sent gifts in return. When he reported this, the court ordered him to refuse them. He asked for reinforcements from Anhui and the two Hu provinces, and the request was granted. Yu Qian, then acting governor-general of Liangjiang, memorialized: 'Every province may talk of holding defensive positions, but Zhejiang must fight at once. ' He added: 'Cengang on Dinghai's western border is the most critical point and should be occupied first by elite troops. ' The order was sent to Yi Libu to investigate and act accordingly. Qi Shan's negotiations in Guangdong then broke down and fighting resumed. In the first month of the twenty-first year an edict ordered Yi Libu to advance and retake Dinghai. In the second month Elliot, having taken Hong Kong, moved all British ships to Guangdong and offered to return Dinghai. An edict condemned him for echoing Qi Shan, delaying the attack on the pretext that troops and artillery were not ready, and letting the enemy fleet escape. He lost his associate grand secretary rank and double-eyed peacock feather but kept the Liangjiang governorship for the time being. Yu Qian replaced him as imperial commissioner in command of the Zhejiang forces. Yu Qian impeached Yi Libu for sending his servant to the enemy ships. He was relieved of duty, Zhang Xi was brought to the capital, and Yi Libu was interrogated by the Ministry of Justice, stripped of office, and exiled to a military penal station. Before long Dinghai, Zhenhai, and Ningbo fell in succession, and Yu Qian died defending them. In the spring of the twenty-second year Pacification General Yijing marched to relieve Zhejiang and was beaten again. Governor Liu Yunke reported that Zhejiang was in grave danger and recommended Yi Libu as a man without hunger for quick glory—a rare quality in those days—and asked that he be sent to serve in the army to atone for his faults. He was then given seventh-rank insignia, accompanied Hangzhou General Qiying to Zhejiang, and received secret orders to act as circumstances required. When British forces attacked Zhapu, Qiying sent him to find a way to make them withdraw. In the fifth month he served as acting vice commander at Zhapu and again used Zhang Xi as an intermediary. The British left Zhapu, attacked Wusong, entered the Yangzi from the sea, and Zhenjiang fell. Yi Libu was ordered to join Qiying in Jiangning to negotiate peace; the details are given in Qiying's biography. After peace was made and the British withdrew, tariff terms were to be negotiated in Guangdong. He was ordered to consult carefully with Qiying, appointed general of Guangzhou and imperial commissioner, and put in charge of postwar arrangements. In the twenty-third year he reached Guangdong, found the people unreconciled and foreign attitudes crafty and arrogant, and was consumed with worry. A little over a month later he died of illness. He was posthumously made Senior Guardian of the Heir Apparent and given the posthumous name Wenmin. Qiying of the Imperial Clan. Qiying, whose courtesy name was Jiechun, belonged to the Plain Blue Banner. His father Lukang had served as grand secretary of the Eastern Pavilion under Jiaqing. Qiying entered service through the yinsheng privilege as a secretary in the Imperial Clan Court and was later made an administrative officer. He rose step by step to Grand Secretariat academician, concurrently serving as vice banner general and commandant of the guard. In Daoguang 2 he was transferred to vice minister of the Court of Colonial Affairs and then to the Ministry of War. In the fourth year he escorted idle members of the imperial clan to their new settlement at Shuangcheng Fort. In the fifth year he was appointed minister of the Imperial Household Department and later served in the Ministries of Works and Revenue. In the seventh year he was appointed commandant of the metropolitan gendarmerie. In the ninth year he was promoted to minister of Rites, placed in charge of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices, the Court of State Ceremonial, and the Imperial Medical Academy, and concurrently served as banner general. In the twelfth year, during drought in the capital region, he memorialized asking for tighter oversight of officials and lighter punishments. The emperor approved and made him an inner court minister. In the fourteenth year he was recommended for honors for diligent management of the metropolitan gendarmerie. He served successively as minister of Works and minister of Revenue. In the fifteenth year, for surveying the imperial burial site at Longquanyu, he was made Junior Mentor of the Heir Apparent. He was ordered to Guangdong and Jiangxi to investigate local affairs. In the seventeenth year the eunuch Zhang Daozhong was caught gambling. Qiying looked the other way and released him. When the affair came to light he was demoted to vice minister of War. He was soon posted as military governor of Rehe. In the eighteenth year he was appointed general of Shengjing. An edict strictly forbade opium and ordered that imperial clansmen and aisin gioro alike be punished according to law. He memorialized asking that banner households be organized in mutual guarantee groups of ten for inspection. In the twentieth year, as the coast went on alert, he memorialized that Lüshunkou was a critical waterway junction and should be fortified. British ships entered the waters off Fengtian and cruised off Shanhaiguan, Qinhuangdao, and other points. Defenses were raised at Jinzhou and Shanhaiguan. In the first month of the twenty-second year, with Guangdong in crisis and Qi Shan already dismissed, Qiying was transferred to general of Guangzhou, made imperial commissioner, and put in charge of Zhejiang maritime affairs. When Censor Su Tingkui reported that England had been defeated by neighboring powers, the court ordered Qiying to hurry to his post at Guangzhou and strike while the enemy was weak. The story proved false, and he remained in Zhejiang. In the fifth month, after Wusong fell, he was ordered to join Yi Libu in Jiangsu and handle affairs as circumstances required. British troops had entered the Yangzi, passed Tuishan Pass, taken Zhenjiang, and occupied Guazhou. Qiying and Pacification General Yijing both memorialized asking to appease and conciliate the enemy. In the seventh month British troops approached Xiaguan below Jiangning. Yi Libu arrived first. The British demanded twenty-one million taels for opium compensation, merchant debts, and war costs; trade at Guangzhou, Fuzhou, Xiamen, Ningbo, and Shanghai; equal ceremonial status for British and Chinese officials; and terms on customs offsets and the release of Chinese collaborators. Three days later Qiying arrived and raised mild objections. British troops suddenly raised red flags and mounted guns on Zhongshan overlooking the city. He urgently stopped them and sent bodyguard Xianling, Jiangning provincial treasurer Entong, Ning-Shao-Tai circuit intendant Lu Zeliang, and Yi Libu's servant Zhang Xi to the British ships, promising to report the terms to the throne. Emperor Daoguang was furious, but Grand Secretary Mujangga argued that the campaign had wasted funds and manpower to no effect and that fighting and appeasing would cost the same. The terms were then approved. Qiying and his party met the British generals Pottinger and Morrison at Jinghai Temple outside Yifeng Gate and signed the treaty. Six million taels were paid at once and the rest over three years, and peace was made. In the ninth month all British troops left Wusong. He was appointed governor-general of Liangjiang and ordered to organize trade and devise policies suited to local conditions in Zhejiang and Fujian. In the twenty-third year he was made imperial commissioner and sent to Guangdong to negotiate trade regulations. He adjusted Guangdong customs tariffs, opened all ports under the new rules, laid out detailed provisions for tax reform, and submitted them to the court for approval. He also memorialized that the United States, France, and other countries should be allowed to trade on the same terms, and the request was granted. The United States requested an imperial audience in the capital, but the request was refused. In the twenty-fourth year he was transferred to governor-general of the two Guang provinces with concurrent charge of trade affairs. In the twenty-fifth year he was made associate grand secretary while keeping his post as governor-general. Belgium, Denmark, and other countries asked to trade, and he was ordered to investigate and set appropriate limits. At the capital inspection in the twenty-sixth year he was recommended for honors for his devoted stewardship of the maritime frontier. He memorialized on troop training, transcribed Tang minister Lu Zhi's memorial on defense preparedness, and asked that copies be sent to every general and governor to keep at hand. Britain requested boundary demarcation and trade in Tibet. Qiying was instructed to hold to existing treaties and not be swayed. By long-standing practice foreign merchants in Guangdong lived in Macao, trade was confined to fixed areas, and they were not allowed to enter the provincial capital except through the foreign factories. The Jiangning treaty provided for warehouses in the provincial capital and for consuls to enter the city, but Guangdong people clung to the old rules and petitioned the high officials in vain. They then organized militia training; public opinion grew heated, and they refused official control. In the twenty-third year Pottinger was about to enter the city, but Guangdong people would not allow it, and he withdrew after hesitating. In the twenty-fifth year British ships returned. Qiying sent Guangzhou prefect Yu Baochun to negotiate with the merchants, but Guangdong people raised an uproar, and the effort was abandoned after pacification. The British complained in letters to the high officials that their men were humiliated whenever they came ashore. Public anger ran too high to be reasoned with. By the twenty-seventh year British ships suddenly entered the provincial river with firmer demands. Qiying vaguely promised fulfillment in two years, and they withdrew only then. He asked to be punished. He was ordered to strengthen the defenses and leave nothing to chance. Qiying knew trouble was inevitable in the end. In the twenty-eighth year he requested an audience, remained in the capital to serve, was awarded the double-eyed peacock feather, and was placed in charge of the Ministries of Rites and War while concurrently serving as banner general. He was soon appointed grand secretary of the Hall of Literary Deepness, ordered to Shandong to investigate salt affairs, and assigned to inspect Zhejiang military units. In the thirtieth year, when Emperor Wenzong ascended the throne, he responded to an edict with his views, writing in summary: 'Good governance must begin with the great tasks of employing men, managing finances, and running the administration. The way to employ men is to test them clearly by their achievements. Men differ in temperament, and talents differ in strength. Use a man against his talents, and even an upright man may fail; use him fittingly, and even a lesser man can get the job done. Offices and duties exist not to give everyone a place to hide. Those who earnestly take on their duties, even if they are lesser men, should be kept; those unwilling to bear responsibility, even if they are gentlemen, should be set aside. Good administration depends on finding the right men. Pedantic talk does not help present needs, antiquarian argument does not suit the times, and finances cannot be managed without capable people. The tax quota is more than forty million taels—enough to cover expenditures with a surplus—yet collection falls short and creates deficits. The causes of deficit cannot be cleared up comprehensively without tracing them to the root. Rather than raising extra funds outside regular taxes, it is better to plan realistically within the regular tax system. ' When the memorial reached the throne, a special edict declared: 'As chief minister, your every word and act is a model for the whole court. Qiying stated his views too freely. His argument was one-sided, clearly violated ancient teaching, and the harm it could do is beyond measure. ' An imperial message rebuked him. Qiying grew uneasy and repeatedly pleaded illness. In the tenth month of that year the emperor personally issued an edict listing the offenses of Mujangga and Qiying, condemning 'Qiying in Guangdong for suppressing the people to please the foreigners, falsely promising them entry into the city, and nearly causing disaster. He repeatedly told the throne in audience that the foreigners were to be feared, handled affairs by compromise, and sought only to keep his rank and salary. Mujangga worked in the shadows and was hard to pin down; Qiying acted openly for all to see. Both harmed the state, and their crime was the same.' Mindful that he had been constrained by the circumstances of his time, the court showed leniency and demoted him to a subordinate post. He was soon given a supplementary appointment as assistant director in the Ministry of Works. In Xianfeng 3 the southern rebels marched north. Qiying's son Qingxi, brigade general of Malan, memorialized asking that father, sons, and brothers serve together at the front. Qiying was ordered to serve with the frontier defense princes and grandees and was given fourth-rank insignia for contributing military funds. In the fifth year Qingxi was impeached for borrowing money from his subordinates. Qiying was punished for making a private report, stripped of office, and placed under house arrest. In the eighth year Britain assembled French, American, and Russian warships to attack Tianjin and demand treaty revisions. Grand Secretary Guiliang and Minister Huashana were ordered to go and investigate. The frontier defense princes and grandees recommended Qiying as familiar with the situation. Summoned for audience, he said he had always been willing to take on the hard task. He was given vice minister rank and sent to Tianjin to negotiate. When Qiying had been in Guangdong, trade affairs at the five treaty ports had largely been decided by his rulings, and he had yielded on every point. In the winter of the seventh year Guangzhou fell. The British seized the archives and translated Qiying's memorials, many of which concealed the truth. They came to despise him deeply. When he reached Tianjin the British refused to see him. In fear he begged to leave and returned to Tongzhou without waiting for orders. His deceit now stood exposed. The princes and grandees impeached him, a stern edict ordered his arrest, and he was granted suicide. The commentator writes: The decision to stop fighting and sue for peace began with Qi Shan. Abandoning preparedness and courting the enemy was what led to defeat. Yi Libu was willing to endure humiliation and bear heavy burdens, but he lacked a strategy to save the dynasty in crisis. Court policy was unsettled and debate was divided. When the treaty was signed below the walls of Jiangning, he and Qiying closed the peace negotiations, surrendering prestige and authority in a loss that could never be undone. Qiying alone handled the postwar arrangements and left open the question of foreign entry into Guangzhou. Fighting broke out again and eventually led to the Gengshen disaster. All three earned the same ill repute, yet Qiying alone could not save his life—and that was only fitting.

王澍蔣衡徐用錫王文治梁巘梁同書
Wang Shu, Jiang Heng, Xu Yongxi, Wang Wenzhi, Liang Yan, and Liang Tongshu
2
鄧石如錢伯坰吳育楊沂孫吳熙載梅植之楊亮
Deng Shiru, Qian Bokan, Wu Yu, Yang Yisun, Wu Xizai, Mei Zhizhi, and Yang Liang
3
王澍，字若林，號虛舟，江南金壇人。 績學工文，尤以書名。 康熙五十一年進士，入翰林，累遷戶科給事中。 雍正初，詔以六科隸都察院。 澍謂科臣掌封駁，品卑任重，儻隸台臣，將廢科參，偕同官崔致遠、康五端抗疏力爭。 世宗怒。 立召詰之，從容奏對，上意稍解，遂改吏部員外郎。 越二年，告歸，益耽書，名播海內。 摹古名搨殆遍，四體並工。 於唐賢歐、褚兩家，致力尤深​​，輒跋尾自道所得。 後內閣學士翁方綱持論與異，謂其篆書得古法，行書次之，正書又次之。 所著題跋及淳化閣帖考正，並行於世。
Wang Shu, courtesy name Ruolin and style name Xuzhou, was a native of Jintan in Jiangnan. He was deeply learned and accomplished as a writer, but was above all renowned for his calligraphy. Having passed the jinshi examination in the fifty-first year of the Kangxi reign, he entered the Hanlin Academy and was successively promoted to supervising secretary of the Revenue Section. Early in the Yongzheng reign, an edict subordinated the Six Sections to the Censorate. Shu argued that section censors held responsibility for reviewing and rebutting memorials—low in rank yet heavy in duty—and that if they were placed under censorate officials, sectional oversight would be lost. Together with his colleagues Cui Zhiyuan and Kang Wuduan, he submitted a forceful memorial of protest. The Yongzheng Emperor was enraged. Summoned at once for questioning, he answered calmly; the emperor's anger eased somewhat, and he was reassigned as a vice director in the Ministry of Personnel. Two years later he retired home, devoting himself ever more deeply to calligraphy, until his fame spread throughout the realm. He copied nearly every celebrated ancient rubbing and was adept in all four major script forms. He devoted himself above all to the Tang masters Ouyang Xun and Chu Suiliang, and would regularly add colophons explaining his insights. Later the Grand Secretary Weng Fanggang took a different view, holding that Shu's seal script attained the ancient methods, his running script ranked next, and his regular script ranked below both. His colophons and his Examination and Correction of the Chunhua Pavilion Rubbings both circulated widely.
4
自明、清之際，工書者，河北以王鐸、傅山為冠，繼則江左王鴻緒、姜宸英、何焯、汪士鋐、張照等，接踵而起，多見他傳。 大抵淵源出於明文徵明、董其昌兩家，鴻緒、照為董氏嫡派，焯及澍則於文氏為近。 澍論書尤詳，一時所宗。
From the Ming–Qing transition onward, the leading calligraphers of Hebei were Wang Duo and Fu Shan; in the lower Yangzi region Wang Hongxu, Jiang Chenying, He Chuo, Wang Shihong, Zhang Zhao, and others followed in succession—most of whom are treated in other biographies. For the most part their lineage traced to the Ming masters Wen Zhengming and Dong Qichang: Hongxu and Zhao were direct heirs of Dong Qichang's tradition, while He Chuo and Wang Shu stood closer to Wen Zhengming's. Shu's writings on calligraphy were especially thorough, and for a time he was the authority whom all followed.
5
蔣衡，改名振生，字湘帆，晚號拙老人。 與澍同里。 鍵戶十二年，寫十三經。 乾隆中，進上，高宗命刻石國學，授衡國子監學正，終不出。 衡早歲好遊，足跡半海內，觀碑關中，獲晉、唐以來名跡，臨摹三百餘種，曰拙存堂臨古帖。 晚與澍相期斫勝，每臨一書，相從質證。 子驥，孫和，並以書世其家。
Jiang Heng, who later changed his name to Zhensheng, had the courtesy name Xiangfan and in his later years styled himself the Old Man of Clumsiness. He was a fellow townsman of Wang Shu. He shut his doors for twelve years and copied out the entire Thirteen Classics in his own hand. During the Qianlong reign he presented his work to the throne; the Qianlong Emperor ordered it carved in stone at the Imperial Academy and appointed Heng rectifier of studies at the Directorate of Education, but he never left retirement to serve. In his youth Heng loved to travel, and his footsteps reached half the empire. Viewing steles in the Guanzhong region, he acquired celebrated works from the Jin and Tang dynasties onward and made copies of more than three hundred pieces, which he collected under the title Copies of Ancient Rubbings from the Studio of Clumsy Preservation. In his later years he and Wang Shu would arrange friendly contests of skill; whenever one copied a work, the other would join him to examine and test the result. His son Jiang Ji and grandson Jiang He both carried on the family's reputation in calligraphy.
6
驥尤精分隸，著漢隸譌體集、古帖字體、續書法論各一卷，兼工畫。 其言曰：「漢、魏字體不同，性情各異。 書須懸臂中鋒，而用力以和平為主。 作畫之提頓逆折，參差映帶，其理一爾。 」皆闡明其先說。
Ji was especially accomplished in clerical and official script. He wrote one juan each of Collected Erroneous Forms in Han Clerical Script, Character Forms in Ancient Rubbings, and Continued Discourse on Calligraphy, and was also skilled in painting. He wrote: "The character forms of the Han and Wei periods differ, and their temperaments differ with them. In writing one must suspend the arm and use the central brush tip, yet the application of force should above all seek harmony and ease. The lifting, pausing, reversing, and folding in painting, with their uneven overlapping and linking—the principle is the same." These remarks all elaborate the doctrines of his forebears.
7
徐用錫，字壇長，宿遷人，佔籍大興。 登鄉舉，康熙四十八年進士，官翰林院編修。 從李光地遊，究心樂律、音韻、歷數、書法。 五十四年，分校會試，嚴絕請託，銜之者反嗾言官劾其把持闈事，聖祖原之，終以浮議罷歸。 乾隆初，起授翰林院侍讀，年已八十。 尋告歸，卒於家。 用錫鄉舉出姜宸英之門，與何焯同為光地客，論書多與二家相出入。 精於鑑別古人，言筆法亦多心得，著字學劄記二卷，載圭美堂集中。
Xu Yongxi, courtesy name Tanchang, was a native of Suqian who registered his domicile in Daxing. He passed the provincial examination and, in the forty-eighth year of the Kangxi reign, the jinshi examination, after which he served as a compiler in the Hanlin Academy. He studied under Li Guangdi and devoted himself to music theory, phonology, calendrical science, and calligraphy. In the fifty-fourth year he served as an associate examiner for the metropolitan examination and strictly refused all solicitations. Those who resented him incited censorial officials to impeach him for manipulating the examination hall. The Kangxi Emperor forgave him, but he was ultimately dismissed and sent home on account of widespread rumor. Early in the Qianlong reign he was recalled and appointed attendant reader in the Hanlin Academy, by which time he was already eighty years old. Before long he retired home and died there. Yongxi had passed the provincial examination as a pupil of Jiang Chenying, and like He Chuo he was a protégé of Li Guangdi; his views on calligraphy largely aligned with or diverged from those two masters. He was expert at authenticating ancient works, and his remarks on brush technique reflected many insights of his own. He wrote two juan of Notes on Character Studies, preserved in the Guimei Hall collection.
8
王文治，字禹卿，江蘇丹徒人。 生有夙慧，十二歲能詩，即工書。 長游京師，從翰林院侍讀全魁使琉球，文字播於海外。 乾隆三十五年，成一甲三名進士，授翰林院編修。 逾三年，大考第一，擢侍讀。 出為雲南臨安知府，因事鐫級，乞病歸。 後當復官，厭吏事，遂不出，往來吳、越間，主講杭州、鎮江書院。 高宗南巡，至錢塘僧寺，見文治書碑，大賞愛之。 內廷有以告，招之出者，亦不應。
Wang Wenzhi, courtesy name Yuqing, was a native of Dantu in Jiangsu. He was gifted from birth: at twelve he could write poetry and was already accomplished in calligraphy. When he came of age he traveled to the capital and accompanied the Hanlin attendant reader Quan Kui on a mission to Ryukyu, so that his writing became known overseas. In the thirty-fifth year of the Qianlong reign he ranked among the top three jinshi graduates and was appointed a compiler in the Hanlin Academy. More than three years later he ranked first in the palace examination and was promoted to attendant reader. He was appointed prefect of Lin'an in Yunnan, but after an incident his rank was reduced; he pleaded illness and returned home. Later, when he was eligible for reinstatement, he had grown weary of official business and never returned to service, traveling between the Wu and Yue regions and lecturing at academies in Hangzhou and Zhenjiang. On his southern tour the Qianlong Emperor visited a Buddhist temple in Qiantang, saw a stele inscribed by Wenzhi, and admired it greatly. Word reached the inner court, and although he was summoned to serve, he would not come.
9
喜聲伎，行輒以歌伶一部自隨，辨論音律，窮極幽渺。 客至張樂，窮朝暮不倦。 海內求書者，多有餽遺，率費於聲伎。 然客散，默然禪定，夜坐，肋未嘗至席。 持佛戒，自言吾詩與書皆禪理也。 卒，年七十三。
He delighted in music and entertainers and always traveled with a company of singers. In discussions of musical pitch he pursued subtleties to their furthest limit. When guests arrived he would set out music and carry on from morning to night without tiring. Patrons throughout the realm who sought his calligraphy often sent him gifts, which he generally spent on music and entertainers. Yet when his guests had gone he would sit in silent meditation through the night, never letting his sides touch the mat. He observed the Buddhist precepts and said, "My poetry and calligraphy are both expressions of Chan insight." He died at the age of seventy-three.
10
所著詩集外有快雨堂題跋，略見論書之旨。 文治書名並時與劉墉相埒，人稱之曰「濃墨宰相，淡墨探花」。 與姚鼐交最深，論最契，當時書名，鼐不及文治之遠播； 後包世臣極推鼐書，與劉墉並列上品，名轉出文治上。
Besides his collected poems he left Kuaiyu Hall Colophons, which give some sense of his views on calligraphy. Wenzhi's reputation in calligraphy rivaled that of Liu Yong in his own day; people called them "the thick-ink Chancellor and the light-ink Flowers-placed graduate." His closest friendship and most congenial exchanges were with Yao Nai; at the time Yao's calligraphic fame did not spread as widely as Wenzhi's; but later Bao Shichen greatly praised Yao's calligraphy, ranking it with Liu Yong's among the finest, and Yao's reputation came to surpass Wenzhi's.
11
梁巘，字聞山，安徽亳州人。 乾隆二十七年舉人，官四川巴縣知縣。 晚辭官，主講壽春書院，以工李北海書名於世。 初為咸安宮教習，至京師，聞欽天監正何國宗曾以事系刑部，時尚書張照亦以他事在系，得其筆法，因詣家就問。 國宗年已八十餘，病不能對客，遣一孫傳語。 巘質以所聞，國宗答曰：「君已得之矣。 」贈以所臨米、黃二帖。
Liang Yan, courtesy name Wenshan, was a native of Bozhou in Anhui. He passed the provincial examination in the twenty-seventh year of the Qianlong reign and served as magistrate of Baxian in Sichuan. In his later years he resigned from office and lectured at the Shouchun Academy, becoming renowned for his mastery of the Li Yong (Beihai) style. Early in his career he served as an instructor at the Xian'an Palace. On reaching the capital he learned that the Director of the Astronomical Bureau, He Guozong, had once been held in the Ministry of Justice over an affair, and that at the same time the Minister Zhang Zhao was also detained on another charge—both men being masters of brush technique—so he went to their homes to seek instruction. Guozong was already over eighty, too ill to receive visitors, and sent a grandson to convey his replies. Yan questioned him about what he had learned, and Guozong replied, "You have already grasped it. " He then gave him his own copies of works after Mi Fu and Huang Tingjian.
12
後巘以語金壇段玉裁曰：「執筆之法，指以運臂，臂以運身。 凡捉筆，以大指尖與食指尖相對，筆正直在兩指尖之間，兩指尖相接如環，兩指本以上平，可安酒杯。 平其肘，腕不附幾，肘圓而兩指與筆正當胸，令全身之力，行於臂而湊於兩指尖。 兩指尖不圓如環，或如環而不平，則捉之也不緊，臂之力尚不能出，而況於身？ 緊則身之力全湊於指尖，而何有於臂？ 古人知指之不能運臂也，故使指頂相接以固筆，筆管可斷，指鍥痛不可勝，而後字中有力。 其以大指與食指也，謂之單勾； 其以大指與食指中指也，謂之雙勾； 中指者，所以輔食指之力也，總謂之'撥鐙法'。 王獻之七、八歲時學書，右軍從旁掣其筆不得，即謂此法。 捨此法，皆旁門外道。 二王以後，至唐、宋、元、明諸大家，口口相傳如是，董宗伯以授王司農鴻緒，司農以授張文敏，吾聞而知之。 本朝但有一張文敏耳，他未為善。 王虛舟用筆祗得一半，蔣湘帆知握筆而少作字樂趣。 世人但言無火氣，不知火氣使盡，而後可言無火氣也。 如此捉筆，則筆心不偏，中心透紙，紙上颯颯有聲。 直畫粗者濃墨兩分，中如有絲界，筆心為之主也。 如此捉筆，則必堅紙作字，輭薄紙當之易破。 其橫、直、撇、捺皆與今人殊，筆鋒所指，方向迥異，筆心總在每筆之中，無少偏也。 古人所謂屋漏痕、折釵股、錐畫沙、印印泥者，於此可悟入。 」巘少著述，所傳緒論僅此。 當時與梁同書並稱，巘曰「北梁」，同書曰「南梁」。
Later Yan told Duan Yucai of Jintan: "In the method of holding the brush, the fingers move the arm, and the arm moves the whole body. When grasping the brush, the tip of the thumb and the tip of the forefinger face each other, with the brush standing upright between them. The two fingertips meet like a ring, and from the bases of the two fingers upward the hand lies level enough to balance a wine cup. Level the elbow so that the wrist does not rest on the table; with the elbow rounded and the two fingers and brush directly before the chest, let the force of the whole body pass through the arm and gather at the two fingertips. If the fingertips do not form a true ring, or form a ring but are not level, the grasp is not tight enough for the arm's force to emerge—much less the body's. When the grasp is tight, the body's force gathers entirely at the fingertips—what need is there then for the arm alone? The ancients knew that the fingers alone cannot move the arm, so they made the finger tops meet to secure the brush. The shaft may break, but the fingers must ache from the pressure—only then do the characters gain inner strength. Using the thumb and forefinger alone is called the single hook; using the thumb, forefinger, and middle finger is called the double hook; the middle finger assists the forefinger's force; together these are known as the stirrup-brushing method. When Wang Xianzhi was seven or eight and learning to write, Wang Xizhi tried to pull the brush from his hand and could not—this is that very method. Abandon this method and every other way is a side path and heterodox teaching. After the Two Wangs, down through the great masters of the Tang, Song, Yuan, and Ming dynasties, it was transmitted mouth to mouth in just this way: Dong Qichang passed it to Wang Hongxu, Wang Hongxu to Zhang Zhao, and I learned it by hearing it told. In our dynasty there has been only one Zhang Zhao who mastered it; the rest have not done so well. Wang Shu mastered only half of this brush method; Jiang Heng knew how to hold the brush but took little pleasure in writing. People speak only of writing without fiery excess, not understanding that one can speak of being free of fiery excess only after that fiery force has been fully spent. Hold the brush in this way and the brush core stays true; the center penetrates the paper, and the paper rustles audibly under the stroke. In a thick vertical stroke written with heavy ink, the line divides into two parts with a thread-like boundary between them—the brush core governs both. Hold the brush this way and you must write on stiff paper; soft, thin paper will tear easily under the pressure. Its horizontals, verticals, left-falling strokes, and right-falling strokes all differ from modern practice: where the brush tip points, the direction is utterly different, and the brush core always remains at the center of each stroke without the slightest deviation. What the ancients meant by roof-leak traces, folded hairpin shanks, awl lines in sand, and seal impressions in mud—through this one may begin to understand. " Yan wrote little; the transmitted threads of his theory are only this much. In his day he and Liang Tongshu were spoken of as a pair: Yan was called "Northern Liang" and Tongshu "Southern Liang."
13
梁同書，字元穎，晚號山舟，浙江錢塘人，大學士詩正子。 乾隆十七年，會試未第，高宗特賜與殿試，入翰林，大考，擢侍講。 淡於榮利，未老，因疾不出。 晚年重宴鹿鳴，加侍講學士銜。 卒，年九十三。 好書出天性，十二歲能為擘窠大字。 初法顏、柳，中年用米法，七十後乃變化。 名滿天下，求書者紙日數束，日本、琉球皆重之。
Liang Tongshu, courtesy name Yuanying and later style name Shanzhou, was a native of Qiantang in Zhejiang and the son of the Grand Secretary Liang Shizheng. In the seventeenth year of the Qianlong reign he failed the metropolitan examination, but the emperor specially allowed him to sit for the palace examination. He entered the Hanlin Academy, passed the palace examination with distinction, and was promoted to expositor. Indifferent to rank and gain, he retired before old age on account of illness. In his later years he was again honored at the Luming banquet and given the additional title of expositor of the Hanlin. He died at the age of ninety-three. His love of calligraphy was innate; at twelve he could already write large characters in the split-brush style. At first he followed Yan Zhenqing and Liu Gongquan; in middle age he adopted Mi Fu's manner; only after seventy did his style fully mature. His fame filled the world; those who sought his writing sent him several bundles of paper each day, and he was esteemed in Japan and Ryukyu alike.
14
嘗與張燕昌論書，略曰：「古人云『筆力直透紙背』，當與天馬行空參看。 今人誤認透紙，便如藥山所云『看穿牛皮』，終無是處。 蓋透紙者，狀其精氣結撰墨光浮溢耳，彼用筆如游絲者，何嘗不透紙背耶？ 用腕力使極輭之筆自見，譬如人持一彊者，使之直，則無所用力； 持一弱者，慾不使之偃，則全腕之力，自然集於兩指端。 其實書者只知指運，而不知有腕力也。 藏鋒之說，非筆如鈍錐之謂，自來書家從無不出鋒者，只是處處留得筆住，不使直走。 筆耍輭，輭則遒； 筆要長，長則靈； 筆耍飽，飽則腴； 落筆耍快，快則意出。 書家燥鋒曰渴筆，畫家亦有枯筆，二字判然不同。 渴則不潤，枯則死矣。 ​​今人喜用硬筆故枯。 帖教人看，不教人摹。 今人只是刻舟求劍，將古人書摹畫如小兒寫仿本，就便形似，豈復有我？ 字耍有氣，氣須從熟得來。 有氣則有勢，大小、長短、高下、欹整，隨筆所至，自然貫注，成一片段，卻著不得絲毫擺佈，熟後自知。 中鋒之法，筆提得起，自然中，亦未嘗無兼用側鋒處，總為我一縷筆尖所使，雖不中亦中。 亂頭粗服非字也，求逸則野，求舊則拙，此處不可有半點名心在。 」同書平生書旨，與梁巘之異同，具見於此。
He once discussed calligraphy with Zhang Yanchang, writing in summary: "The ancients said that brush force penetrates straight through the paper—this should be read together with the image of the heavenly horse traversing the void. People today mistake penetrating the paper for what Yaoshan meant by 'seeing through ox hide'—in the end that leads nowhere. Penetrating the paper describes how vital spirit gathers and ink-light overflows on the surface. Those who wield the brush like gossamer—have they not also penetrated the paper's back? Use wrist force and even an extremely soft brush reveals its nature. It is like a person holding something stiff: to keep it straight requires no effort; but holding something weak, if you wish to keep it from bending, the full force of the wrist naturally gathers at the two fingertips. In truth the writer thinks only of moving the fingers and does not realize that wrist force is at work. The doctrine of hidden tip does not mean making the brush blunt like an awl. Calligraphers from antiquity have never failed to bring out the tip; they simply keep the brush dwelling at every turn and do not let it run straight through. The brush must be supple; suppleness yields vigor; it must be long; length yields spirit; it must be well loaded with ink; fullness yields richness; the stroke must fall swiftly; swiftness lets intention show through. Calligraphers call a dry tip a "thirsty brush," and painters speak of a "withered brush" as well, but the two terms are clearly not the same. Thirst leaves the stroke unmoistened; withering kills it altogether. People today prefer hard brushes, and that is why their writing turns dry and lifeless. Model books are meant to be studied with the eye, not traced stroke for stroke. People today merely mark the boat to find the sword: they copy the ancients like children filling out copybooks. Even if the likeness is achieved, where is the writer's own presence? Writing must have breath and momentum, and breath can come only from mastery. Where there is breath there is momentum: large and small, long and short, high and low, leaning or upright—all follow wherever the brush goes, flowing naturally into a single passage without the least forced arrangement. Only after long practice does one understand this for oneself. In the central-tip method, if the brush can be lifted freely, the center comes naturally. One may also use the side of the tip at times, but a single thread of brush point governs all—so that even when not literally centered, the stroke is still centered in effect. Scribbled strokes and rough execution are not true writing. Pursue ease and the work turns crude; pursue antiquity and it turns clumsy. Here one must not harbor the slightest thought of reputation. " Liang Tongshu's lifelong principles of calligraphy, and how they agreed or differed from Liang Yan's, are fully visible in these remarks.
15
鄧石如，初名避仁宗諱，遂以字行，改字頑伯，安徽懷寧人。 居皖公山下，又號完白山人。 少產僻鄉，鮮聞見，獨好刻石，仿漢人印篆甚工。 弱冠孤貧，遊壽州，梁巘見其篆書，驚為筆勢渾鷙，而未盡得古法。 介謁江寧梅鏐，都御史成子也。 家多L3藏金石善本，盡出示之，為具衣食楮墨，使專肄習。
Deng Shiru, whose original given name avoided the Jiaqing Emperor's taboo and who therefore went by his style name, later styled himself Wanbo. He was a native of Huaining in Anhui. He lived below Mount Wan and also styled himself the Hermit of Mount Wanbai. Born in a remote village, he had little exposure to the wider world, but he loved carving stone and was exceptionally skilled at imitating Han seal script. Orphaned and poor in his early twenties, he traveled to Shouzhou. Liang Yan saw his seal script and marveled at its deep, fierce brush momentum, though he had not yet fully mastered the ancient methods. He was introduced to Mei Liao of Jiangning, son of the Censor-in-Chief Mei Cheng. The Mei household possessed many fine ancient collections of bronze and stone rubbings, all of which were shown to him. Mei provided food, clothing, paper, and ink and had him devote himself entirely to study.

好石鼓文，李斯嶧山碑、泰山刻石，漢開母石闕，燉煌太守碑，吳蘇建國山碑，皇像天發神讖碑，唐李陽冰城隍廟碑、三墳記，每種臨摹各百本。 又苦篆體不備，寫說文解字二十本。 帝搜三代鐘鼎，秦、漢瓦當、碑額。 五年，篆書成。 乃學漢分，臨史晨前、後碑，華山碑，白石神君，張遷，潘校官，孔羨，受禪，大饗諸碑，各五十本。 三年，分書成。 石如篆法以二李為宗，縱橫闢闔，得之史籀，稍參隸意，殺鋒以取勁折，字體微方，與秦、漢當額為近。 分書結體嚴重，約嶧山、國山之法而為之。 自謂：「吾篆未及陽冰，而分不減梁鵠。 」
He studied the Stone Drum Inscriptions, Li Si's Mount Yi and Mount Tai inscriptions, the Han Opening Mother stone que, the Dunhuang Prefect stele, Wu Su Jian's Guoshan stele, the Huangxiang Tianfa Shenchen stele, and Li Yangbing's City God Temple stele and Record of the Three Mounds from the Tang—making a hundred copies of each. Finding the seal forms at his disposal still incomplete, he copied out the Shuowen Jiezi twenty times. He also gathered examples from the bronzes of the Three Dynasties and from Qin and Han tile ends and stele crowns. After five years his seal script was fully formed. He then studied Han clerical script, copying the front and back Steles of Shi Chen, the Mount Hua stele, the White Stone Spirit Lord, Zhang Qian, Pan the School Official, Kong Xian, the Shou Chan stele, and the Great Feast stele—fifty copies of each. After three more years his clerical script was fully formed. Shiru's seal script took the Two Lis as its models. In its sweeping expansions and contractions he drew on Shizhou, with a touch of clerical influence; he cut the brush tip to achieve forceful angularity, and his characters were slightly square, close to Qin and Han stele crowns. His clerical script was grave and weighty in structure, broadly following the methods of the Mount Yi and Mount Guo inscriptions. He said of himself, "My seal script has not yet reached Li Yangbing, but my clerical script is no inferior to Liang He's." "

客梅氏八年，學既成，遍遊名山水，以書刻自給。 遊黃山，至歙，鬻篆於賈肆。 編修張惠言故深究秦篆，時館修撰金榜家，偶見石如書，語榜曰：「今日得見上蔡真跡。 」乃冒雨同訪於荒寺，榜備禮客之於家。 薦於尚書曹文埴，偕至京師，大學士劉墉、副都御史陸錫熊皆驚異曰：「千數百年無此作矣！ 」時京師論篆、分者，多宗內閣學士翁方綱，方綱以石如不至其門，力詆之。 石如乃去，客兩湖總督畢沅，沅故好客，吳中名士多集節署，裘馬都麗，石如獨布衣徒步。 居三年，辭歸，沅為置田宅，俾終老。 瀕行，觴之，曰：「山人，吾幕府一服清涼散也！ 」石如年四十六始娶，常往來江、淮間，卒，年六十三。
After eight years as a guest in the Mei household, his training complete, he traveled famous mountains and rivers throughout the land, supporting himself by his writing and carving. On a journey to Mount Huang he reached She county and sold his seal script in the market stalls. The compiler Zhang Huiyan was a deep student of Qin seal script and was then staying at the home of the compiler Jin Bang. By chance he saw Shiru's writing and told Jin, "Today I have seen the authentic hand of Cai Yong of Shangcai." " They went together through the rain to find him at a deserted temple, and Jin Bang received him at home with full courtesy. Jin recommended him to the Minister Cao Wenyan, and together they went to the capital. The Grand Secretary Liu Yong and the Vice Censor-in-Chief Lu Xixiong were both astonished and exclaimed, "For more than a thousand years there has been nothing like this!" " At the time most critics of seal and clerical script in the capital followed the Grand Secretary Weng Fanggang, who attacked Shiru fiercely because he had not presented himself at Weng's door. Shiru then left and became a guest of the Governor-General of the Two Lakes, Bi Yuan. Bi was a great host, and many celebrated scholars of the Wu region gathered at his headquarters in splendid dress and mounted retinue, while Shiru alone went about in plain clothes on foot. After three years he took his leave to return home, and Bi Yuan provided him with fields and a house so that he might live out his days in comfort. As he was about to leave, Bi offered him a parting cup and said, "Mountain man, you are the cooling medicine of my staff!" " Shiru did not marry until he was forty-six. He often traveled between the Yangzi and Huai regions and died at the age of sixty-three.

子傳密，初名廷璽，字守之。 從李兆洛學，晚客曾國籓幕。 能以篆書世其家。
His son Deng Chuanmi, originally named Tingxi and courtesy name Shouzhi, studied under Li Zhaoluo and in his later years served as a guest in Zeng Guofan's staff. He was able to carry on the family's reputation in seal script.

當乾、嘉之間，嘉定錢坫、陽湖錢伯坰，皆以書名。 坫自負其篆直接陽冰，嘗遊焦山，見壁間篆書心經，嘆為陽冰之亞。 既而知為石如所作，摭其不合六書者以為詆。 伯坰故服石如篆、分為絕業，及見其行、草，歎曰：「此楊少師神境也！ 」復與論筆法不合，遂助坫詆之尤力。 坫見儒林傳。
During the Qianlong and Jiaqing reigns, Qian Dian of Jiading and Qian Bokan of Yanghu were both renowned for calligraphy. Dian prided himself on a seal script that descended directly from Li Yangbing. Once, visiting Mount Jiao, he saw a Heart Sutra in seal script on a wall and exclaimed that it ranked just below Yangbing's own work. When he learned that it was Shiru's work, he collected instances that did not conform to the Six Scripts and used them to attack him. Bokan had long admired Shiru's seal and clerical script as supreme achievements, but when he saw his running and cursive script he exclaimed, "This is the divine realm of Yang Ningshi!" " Yet when they discussed brush technique and found themselves at odds, he joined Dian in attacking Shiru all the more fiercely. Dian has a separate biography in the Confucian Scholars section.
20
伯坰，字魯斯，自號僕射山人，尚書維城從子。 少孤，力學，工詩嗜酒，廣交遊，以國子監生終。 書學顏平原、李北海，嘗曰：「古人用兔毫，故書有中線，今用羊毫，其精者乃成雙鉤。 吾躭此五十年，才十得三四。 」論者謂自劉墉歿，正、行書以伯坰為第一。 其執筆，虛小指，以三指包管外，與大指相拒，側毫入紙，助怒張之勢。 指腕皆不動，以肘來去，斥古今相承撥鐙之說。 石如作書，則懸腕雙鉤，管隨指轉，兩家法大殊。
Bokan, courtesy name Lusi, styled himself the Hermit of the Vice Director and was a nephew of the Minister Qian Weicheng. Orphaned young, he studied diligently, was accomplished in poetry and fond of wine, and had a wide circle of friends. He ended his days as a student of the Imperial Academy. He studied the styles of Yan Zhenqing and Li Yong. He once said, "The ancients used rabbit-hair brushes, so their writing had a central line; today we use goat hair, and only the finest strokes achieve the double-hook effect." I have devoted myself to this for fifty years and have mastered only three or four parts in ten." " Critics held that after Liu Yong's death, Bokan ranked first in regular and running script. In holding the brush he left the little finger free, wrapped three fingers around the shaft outside while opposing the thumb, and let the side of the tip enter the paper to lend force to an expansive, vigorous stroke. Neither fingers nor wrist moved; he moved only the elbow back and forth, and rejected the stirrup-brushing method handed down through the ages. When Shiru wrote, he suspended the wrist and used the double hook, letting the shaft turn with the fingers—the methods of the two masters were utterly different.

吳育，字山子，江蘇吳江人。 與包世臣、李兆洛遊，能文，工書。 謂：「下筆須使筆毫平鋪紙上，乃四面圓足，此陽冰篆法，書家真秘密語。 」世臣取其說。 育篆書尤工，法與石如差近。
Wu Yu, courtesy name Shanzi, was a native of Wujiang in Jiangsu. He associated with Bao Shichen and Li Zhaoluo, was accomplished as a writer, and was skilled in calligraphy. He said, "When setting the brush one must lay the hairs flat on the paper so that they are round and full on every side. This is Li Yangbing's seal method—the calligrapher's true secret teaching." " Bao Shichen adopted this doctrine. Yu was especially accomplished in seal script, and his method was close to Shiru's.

楊沂孫，字詠春，江蘇常熟人。 道光二十三年舉人，官安徽鳳陽知府。 父憂歸，遂不出，自號濠叟，少學於李兆洛，治週、秦諸子。 耽書法，尤致力於篆、籀，著文字解說問譌，欲補苴段玉裁、王筠所未備。 又考上古逮史籀、李斯，折衷於許慎，作在昔篇。 篆、隸宗石如，而多自得。 嘗曰：「吾書篆、籀，頡頏鄧氏，得意處或過之； 分、隸則不能及也。 」光緒七年，卒，年六十九。 沂孫同時工篆、籀者，又推吳大澂，自有傳。
Yang Yisun, courtesy name Yongchun, was a native of Changshu in Jiangsu. He passed the provincial examination in the twenty-third year of the Daoguang reign and served as prefect of Fengyang in Anhui. After returning home on his father's death he never took office again and styled himself the Old Man of the Moat. In youth he studied under Li Zhaoluo and devoted himself to the philosophers of the Zhou and Qin periods. He was devoted to calligraphy and above all to seal and large-seal script. He wrote Questions and Corrections on Explanations of Characters, intending to supplement what Duan Yucai and Wang Yun had left incomplete. He also investigated writing from high antiquity down through Shizhou and Li Si, reconciling his findings with Xu Shen's system in his Zaixi Pian. His seal and clerical scripts followed Shiru's school yet showed much originality of his own. He once said, "In seal and large-seal script I can stand alongside the Deng school, and in my best passages I sometimes surpass them; but in clerical script I cannot match them." " He died in the seventh year of the Guangxu reign at the age of sixty-nine. Among his contemporaries skilled in seal and large-seal script, Wu Dacheng was also highly regarded and has his own biography.

吳熙載，初名廷颺，以字行，後又字讓之，江蘇儀徵人。 先世居江寧，父明煌，始遊揚州，善相人術。 熙載為諸生，博學多能，從包世臣學書。 世臣創明北朝書派，溯源窮流，為一家之學。 其筆法兼採同時黃乙生、王良士、吳育、硃昂之、鄧石如諸人之說。 執筆，食指高鉤，大指加食指、中指之間，中指內鉤，小指貼名指外拒，管向左迤，後稍偃，若指鼻準。 運鋒，使筆毫平鋪紙上，筆筆斷而後起。 結字計白當黑，使左右牝牡相得，自謂合古人八法、九宮之旨。 熙載恪守師法，世臣真、行、藁草無不工，嗜篆、分而未致力，熙載篆、分功力尤深。 复縱筆作畫，亦有士氣。 咸豐中，卒。
Wu Xizai, originally named Tingyang and known by his style name, later also styled himself Rangzhi. He was a native of Yizheng in Jiangsu. His family had long lived in Jiangning. His father Minghuang first traveled to Yangzhou and was skilled in physiognomy. Xizai was a licentiate, broadly learned and accomplished in many arts, and studied calligraphy under Bao Shichen. Shichen articulated the Northern Dynasties calligraphic school, tracing its sources to their roots and forming a school of his own. His brush methods combined the teachings of his contemporaries Huang Yisheng, Wang Liangshi, Wu Yu, Zhu Angzhi, Deng Shiru, and others. In holding the brush, the forefinger hooks high, the thumb rests between forefinger and middle finger, the middle finger hooks inward, and the little finger presses outward against the ring finger. The shaft slants to the left and then tilts slightly back, as if pointing toward the bridge of the nose. In moving the tip he laid the hairs flat on the paper, breaking each stroke before lifting and starting the next. In structuring characters he balanced white space against black ink and made the left and right sides complement one another, claiming that this fulfilled the ancients' Eight Methods and Nine Palaces principles. Xizai adhered strictly to his teacher's methods. Shichen was accomplished in regular, running, and draft-cursive script alike and loved seal and clerical script without devoting himself fully to them, whereas Xizai's mastery of seal and clerical script was especially profound. He also painted freely with the brush, and his paintings likewise had a scholar's dignity. He died during the Xianfeng reign.

Peizhong is treated in detail in the Confucian Scholars section.

Zhizhi passed the provincial examination in the nineteenth year of the Daoguang reign. He was thoroughly versed in the classics and renowned for his poetry, and Shichen especially praised his calligraphy. Shichen said that his writing was bold and graceful, tempered through old rubbings until blood, breath, essence, and spirit rushed to the wrist—and that Xizai did not dare claim precedence over him. He also learned the qin from Lady Yan, a female disciple of Wu Sibo, and possessed a singular spiritual understanding of the instrument. He corrected Sibo's transmitted scores and understood the historical reasons behind the composition of ancient pieces. He styled his residence the Ji Hermitage. Peizhong shared his passion and wrote two juan of Qin Studies. Zhizhi died at fifty, and his qin methods were never committed to writing.

Liang came from a military family for generations and inherited the hereditary office of Cavalry Commandant. Deeply learned and upright in conduct, he was widely praised by scholars between the Yangzi and Huai. His calligraphy ranked just below Xizai's.

Shen Yongxi of Hefei was the latest to emerge; he did not die until the end of the Guangxu reign, at nearly eighty years of age. He adhered to his teacher's methods to the end and was the senior surviving disciple of the Bao school.

Shichen ranked Qing calligraphers in five grades divided into nine ranks: "Peaceful, level, simple, and still; forceful and beautiful, heaven-formed—the divine grade; brewed without visible effort, horizontal and vertical in harmony—the marvelous grade; following traces to seek their sources, with thought and force fully engaged—the capable grade; singing in one's own Chu mode without straying from the classical tradition—the transcendent grade; faithfully preserving established forms yet maintaining an elegant school of one's own—the fine grade. The divine grade had one master: Deng Shiru in clerical and seal script. In the upper marvelous grade there was one master: Deng Shiru in clerical script and regular script; in the lower marvelous grade two masters: Liu Yong in small regular script and Yao Nai in running-cursive script. " The nine grades listed ninety-seven entries in all; six names appeared more than once, leaving ninety-one distinct masters. He later added one master to the upper capable grade: Zhang Qi in regular, running, and clerical script; three to the lower capable grade: Yu Shudian in running script, Duan Yuli in small regular and cursive script, and Wu Dexuan in running script. Six masters were added to the upper fine grade: Wu Yu in seal and running script, Fang Lüqian in clerical script, Mei Zhizhi in running script, Zhu Angzhi in running script, Li Zhaoluo in running script, and Xu Zhunyi in regular script.

其後包氏之學盛行，咸、同以來，以書名者，何紹基、張裕釗、翁同龢三家最著，並見他傳。 紹基宗顏平原法，晚復出入漢分； 裕釗源出於包氏； 同龢規模閎變，不為諸家所囿，為一代後勁雲。
Afterward the Bao school's teachings flourished widely. From the Xianfeng and Tongzhi reigns onward, the most celebrated calligraphers were He Shaoji, Zhang Yuzhao, and Weng Tonghe—all of whom are treated in other biographies. Shaoji followed the tradition of Yan Zhenqing and in his later years moved freely between that school and Han clerical script; Yuzhao's lineage came from the Bao school; Tonghe's manner was broad and transformative, unconstrained by any single school, and he was regarded as the great sustaining force of his generation in calligraphy.

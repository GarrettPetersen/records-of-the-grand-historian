=宗稷辰=宗稷辰，字滌甫，浙江會稽人。 道光元年舉人，授內閣中書，充軍機章京。 遷起居注主事，再遷戶部員外郎。 咸豐元年，遷御史。 疏請飭各省實行保甲，略言：「州縣宜久任，時日宜寬假，填寫門牌當詳細核對，董事胥役毋派費累民，酌用丞簿以為襄助，先編巨族以為聯屬，並可申明讀法之典，兼收團練、社倉之益。 」詔下直省督撫，各就地體察參酌行之。 又疏言通籌出入，宜崇實去偽，舉清查、報效、生息三端； 又疏請酌改經徵處，分令州縣戴罪嚴催：並下戶部覈議。 五年，聞上將謁陵，未有旨戒行，稷辰疏言：「畿南州縣被水，連歲用兵，民氣甫行休息，籥請展緩一年。 」上諭曰：「每歲謁陵，事同典禮，如果畿輔民力未逮，亦必權衡時勢，暫緩舉行。 今茲並未降旨何日謁陵，宗稷辰揣度陳奏，徒博敢諫之名而無其實。 此風不可長！ 」下部議處。 尋又奏言：「自粵匪竄據長江，數年以來，文臣武將，能戰者稀。 如烏蘭泰、塔齊布、江忠源皆難得之將，而多不盡其用，且以死殉。 如勝保、張亮基、袁甲三皆勇於任事，而亦未盡其用，以罪罷去。 近日支持兩湖，賴有一二書生，如胡林翼、羅澤南，能以練膽為士卒先。 此二人者，實曾國籓有以開之。 此時若開文武兼資一科，誠足濟當時之急，而臣工多不敢薦舉者，一恐其才疏而得過，一恐其遇蹇而罔功。 處愁眉焚頂之時，守蹈常習故之轍，見有敗衄，動以餉匱為辭。 餉固不可不籌，試思用兵乏人，雖斂金百萬，棄如土苴，終歸無用。 臣聞見隘陋，非能盡識天下之才，所知湖南有左宗棠，通權達變，為疆吏所倚重，若使獨當一面，必不下於林翼、澤南。 其屢經論薦，難進易退，肝膽經術，實可取材者，有若湖州之姚承輿。 其策議深沉，才識過人者，有若常州之週騰虎、管晏，桂林之唐啟華，皆關心時務，今尚鬱鬱伏處田間。 誠能破格招賢，連茹並進，則得一人可以平數州，得數人可以清一路。 長江雖阻，當不難分道建功，剋日平定。 伏乞皇上命內外臣工各舉所知，無論已仕未仕，果能文武兼資，皆許徵起，必可網羅而盡得之。 」疏入，下各督撫，命以宗棠等加考送部引見。 宗棠自此膺簡拔，論者謂其知人。 遷給事中。 時京師行大錢，商民苦之。 稷辰上疏請復用制錢，號曰「祖錢」，而大錢改純用鐵鑄，兼行並用。 下部議，格不行。 又以畿輔水患，疏請急賑，從之。 尋授山東運河道，捻匪入境，於濟寧牛頭河濱築戰牆，北岸六千三百丈，南岸八千六百丈，賴以守禦。 以功加鹽運使銜。 同治六年，引疾歸，尋卒。 稷辰父霈正，官湖南零陵知縣，廉無餘貲。 稷辰事母孝。 為學宗王守仁、劉宗周。 罷官後，主餘姚龍山書院、山陰蕺山書院。 官京朝，請祀總兵葛雲飛本籍； 官山東，請修方孝孺祠，並刻正學集：其振勵風教多類此。 =尹耕雲=尹耕雲，字杏農，江蘇桃源人。 道光三十年進士，授禮部主事，再遷郎中。 咸豐五年，粵匪犯畿輔，惠親王綿愉為大將軍，僧格林沁參贊軍務，闢耕雲佐幕府，上書論防務，為文宗所知。 八年，授湖廣道監察御史，署戶科給事中。 時方多事，封章月數上。 直隸總督訥爾經額坐貽誤封疆罷，復起。 耕雲疏言：「訥爾經額之罪，天下共聞共見，未喻其復行起用之故。 方今江、淮、楚、豫軍務未靖，秉鉞之臣，星羅棋布，所以奮不顧身，必欲滅此朝食者，固由篤於忠義，亦以國家信賞必罰，有以畏服其心。 萬一效尤解體，患何可言？ 昔宣宗起用琦善，以陳慶鏞之言而罷。 伏原紹述心傳，收回成命。 」時粵匪复窺武漢，耕雲疏言：「武漢地踞上游，北窺關陝，南脅湖湘，東撼吳越，西製巴蜀，自古南北用兵，皆出死力爭之。 今賊窺伺楚北，分擾廣濟、黃岡，逼近省城，撫臣胡林翼兵勇數千，眾寡懸絕，江路綿遠，首尾不能兼顧。 侍郎曾國籓忠勇樸誠，應請授為欽差大臣，率其所部援湖北，較諸他臣事半功倍。 」粵匪陷定遠，耕雲疏言：「定遠失守，粵、捻新合，必謀北竄，恃山東為之屏蔽。 撫臣崇恩幸其不戕官據城，於賊退後虛報勝仗，內則巧為彌縫，掩一人耳目； 外則恣其朘削，竭萬姓脂膏。 惟懇俯念籓籬重地，立予罷斥，簡大員往代。 於洪湖​​多募水師，兼飭傅振邦全軍移駐固鎮、靈壁，冀收皖北，以固山東。 」及廬州失守，又​​疏言：「昔人建省安慶，與九江、江寧為犄角，控扼長江。 上年徙治廬州，已失形勝，茲並廬州亦不能守。 胡林翼等自武漢進逼九江，而安徽之賊，或自英、霍走湖北，牽我上游，或自徽、歙擾浙西，窺我腹地。 我軍分道救援，罷於奔命。 賊有四達之路，我無三面之圍，雖日克一城，何益？ 撫臣福濟屢挫損威，候補京堂袁甲三素得民心，如以為巡撫，必奮身圖報。 」及國籓進師，疏言：「軍興以來，徵調半天下，糜餉數千萬，卒未能掃穴擒渠，則以屢後時而數失機也。 今曾國籓蓄養精銳，所向克捷。 陳玉成、張洛行率悍賊數十萬，齊向潛山、太湖抗拒，眾寡之數，十倍於我，一有疏虞，關係甚重。 此時廬、鳳、六合賊勢必單，請飭袁甲三、張國樑刻期搗其巢穴，逼令反顧，或令間道為楚師聲援，亦足褫其狂魄。 」別疏劾河道總督庚長，請以甲三兼攝； 又論雲南迴匪不宜專意主撫； 又陳京師本計，平糶、採買、周恤、蓄積諸事宜並舉； 又言錢法積弊：諸疏多見採納。 英、法合軍犯天津，耕雲專疏者七，會疏者二，力主決戰，上命王大臣集議。 與鄭親王端華等議不合，耕云抗辯痛哭而罷。 耕云初在禮部，肅順頗重之，乃是為所憎。 九年，科場獄起，以科道失糾下吏議，而耕雲以充內監試譴獨重，鐫二級調用。 十年，京師戒嚴，上將幸熱河，耕雲代團防大臣草疏諫阻，復自以書抵肅順，卒不聽。 侍郎文祥提督九門，遇耕雲東城，相持哭，因為規畫留守諸事。 胡林翼疏薦耕雲胸有權略，請起用。 會副都御史毛昶熙治河南團練，疏調從軍。 同治元年，率部卒五千，從僧格林沁平金樓寨教匪，又偕提督張曜克張岡捻巢，以道員記名，賜花翎。 三年，署河陝汝道。 西征軍購糧陝州，市斛小，責屬縣償其不足，凡數百萬斤，耕雲悉請罷之。 客軍有不法者，斬以徇。 境多刀匪，請得節制河、陝兵，饋餉以時，兵咸用命。 四年，張總愚犯畿輔，耕雲從巡撫李鶴年進軍磁州，建策築長圍斷賊歸路。 兩署糧儲鹽法道，佐治善後事，濬惠濟河，塞河決，敘勞加布政使銜。 十三年，補河陝汝道。 河、陝徭役重，亞於常賦，耕雲立定制，嚴稽覈，民困稍甦。 光緒三年，大旱，條上救荒七事，未及行，卒於官。 耕雲在言路著直聲，出任監司，巡撫張之萬、李鶴年皆倚重之，軍事多所贊畫。 卒後，巡撫李慶翱以災荒被劾，牽及冒領兵餉事，辭連耕雲，後終得白云。 =王拯=王拯，初名錫振，字定甫，廣西馬平人。 道光二十一年進士，授戶部主事，充軍機章京。 大學士賽尚阿視師廣西，以拯從，拯感時多難，慷慨思有所建白。 咸豐間，自郎中累遷大理寺少卿。 同治二年，降捻宋景詩由陝西還擾直隸、山東，拯奏言：「景詩岡屯磚圩，儼然嵎固，自陝逸回，其黨不過數百。 崇厚等一再養癰，裹脅逾萬。 近复於昌邑、莘、聊城、臨清四州縣，令村莊將所獲麥與佃戶平分，運送岡屯，是其名為降伏，心跡轉益兇悖。 請密敕直隸督臣劉長佑計調來營，暴其罪而誅之。 若抗違不至，直隸官軍猶能越境進剿。 景詩既除，如楊蓬嶺、程順書等首惡，皆可駢誅，以除巨憝，以安畿輔。 」疏入，未行。 其後景詩卒以叛誅。 軍事未定，曾國籓議於廣東籌餉，勞崇光創辦釐金，諸弊叢起。 拯疏言：「兩粵為肇亂之區，岑溪、容縣，數載皆為賊踞。 信宜陳金缸尤為巨憝，群賊相為一氣，滋蔓難圖。 勞崇光舉辦釐金，率令紳商包充墊繳，燃眉剜肉，事何可常？ 及崇光去任，徵收減少。 近乃有釐務委員，或為眾所毆傷，或為民間枷號，雖民情頑獷，而官吏惡劣亦可概見。 以積年久亂之地，有負嵎圜視之賊，當一切利孔、百方搜剔之時，臣竊恐利未十而害已百。 萬一兩粵复糜爛，更不知何所措手足，豈惟釐金不能辦而已？ 」因薦廣東道員唐啟廕、兩淮運使郭嵩燾、浙江運使成孫詒。 旋用嵩燾督廣東釐金，自拯疏發之也。 三年，遷太常寺卿，署左副都御史。 疏論：「總理各國事務大臣侍郎崇綸、恆祺、董恂、薛煥委瑣齷齪，通國皆知，竊恐外邦輕侮，以為中朝卿貳之班，大都不過如若曹等，未免為中朝恥辱。 就令人材難得，或於總理衙門位置為宜，上應量為裁抑，或處以散職，或畀以虛銜，庶外邦服我旌別之嚴。 四方聞之，亦釋然於朝廷宥納群倫、羈縻彼族之意。 」尋遷通政使，仍署左副都御史。 疏言：「近日蘇、杭迭克，直、東肅清。 臣觀從來將興之業，垂成之功，未有不矢以小心，而始能底定者。 金陵賊窟雖計於三四月間可拔，而丹陽與常州犄角，百戰悍賊如李秀成等，麇集死守。 杭、嘉既克，餘黨歸併湖州。 其自皖南竄越江西之賊，蔓延玉山、鉛山、金谿、建昌二三百里，眾號八九萬，並有闌入福建境者。 又聞李世賢自率巨股由淳安、遂安接踵而至，曾國籓、左宗棠等用兵日久，前此屢陳不亟求功旦夕，同一老謀深計，獨於皖、浙毗境豫作防維之策，則國籓意在徽、寧各飭所部分防，宗棠以為不若並力取廣德扼賊竄路。 兩議未及定，賊已由皖竄贛。 賊又草竊已久，人數太眾，勢多不能聚殲而弗使一賊他遁。 臣則以此賊人多勢劇，一意奔突，前股未痛剿，後股又踵接。 萬一深入江西腹地，燼餘复熾，又至燎原。 且由贛逾閩，可以直走汀、潮，為數年來竄匪熟路。 黃文金由此而來，石達開由此而去，前事可為深警。 疊蒙諭旨，曾國籓、左宗棠、李鴻章、沈葆楨及閩、粵各督撫諄諄戒備。 當此大功將竟，惟當效力一心，互籌戰守，務將分竄諸賊，前截後追，必使所至創夷，日就衰殘零落，不得喙息，以成巨患。 臣尤有請者，皖、浙諸軍與賊相持不為不久，所需餉項，國籓、宗棠等各於江、楚等省自為籌畫。 國籓奏於江省設立總台，以一省捐釐之數，為皖軍十萬養命之源。 浙軍固不能分撥，即國籓所部月餉，傳聞亦祗放數成，不得已而籌及廣東釐捐，乃又不能遽辦。 夫民之不能見遠而各為其私者，情也。 廣東有之，江西豈獨不然？ 日前沈葆楨奏請將江西茶稅、牙釐等款歸本省任收，旋用部議允留其半，在國籓等斷不至觖望。 惟軍前將卒，當枕戈喋血切望成功之時，忽聞軍餉來源將減，眾心或生疑懼，何以得飽騰而資鼓舞？ 擬請飭贛、皖、楚、粵各疆臣，值此事機至緊，無論如何變通為難，總當殫竭血誠，同心共濟。 甘肅回氛未戢，中州餘捻尚存，汝南陳大喜等竄逸湖北，自隨、棗逼襄、樊； 張總愚自南台山中出竄內、淅，時虞合併； 漢中之賊，全竄寧、陝、商州一路，聞將會齊襄、樊回援金陵，誠亦未可輕忽。 目前陝省軍務，政出多門，李雲麟追賊商於，忽卷旆而西，其在興安，未能遏賊竄逸，其在漢陰，遇賊避匿，縱勇淫掠，宜量加裁抑。 劉蓉素嘗學問，懷負非常，漢中之賊，本所專辦，而竄擾四出，尤當誓志盪除，方為不負。 多隆阿聲望最優，眾口爭傳為第一名將，乃近日聲望漸損，宜申聖諭訓飭。 雷正綰所向克捷，諒足當一面之寄，顧全甘官吏，未有一二正人支持其間。 現聞蘭州與慶陽隔絕，恩麟權督印，不過使令便闢之材，識見陋劣； 熙麟坐守慶陽、寧夏一區，又為慶昀種種紕繆所誤。 臣愚以為亟宜遴簡公正有為之大臣，鎮撫整飭。 今之天下，何易遽言率土奠安，而南北軍務漸定，西事再能就緒，亦即為大致之澄清。 朝廷者天下之本，宮府清明嚴肅，與疆場奮迅振拔之氣，相感而自通。 天下大勢日轉，而亦正多難鉅之事，或遽以為時局清明，事機暢遂，若已治已安者然。 人情大抵喜新狃常，畏難而務獲，獨有當幾至誠君子，為能深察而切戒之。 昔諸葛亮為三代下一人，史獨稱之以謹慎。 硃子進戒宋孝宗曰：​​'使宴安酖毒之害，日滋而日長； 將臥薪嘗膽之志，日遠而日忘。 '臣不勝私憂過計，冒昧瀝陳。 」疏入，報聞。 尋告歸，卒。 =穆緝香阿=穆緝香阿，字居南，滿洲鑲紅旗人。 由工部主事再遷郎中。 同治四年，授山東道監察御史。 疏請慎擇宦寺，略言：「皇上沖齡御極，聖學日新，知識日開，左右侍從之輩，宜豫加慎選，勿使將來蠱惑聖聰。 溯自漢末及前明，朝政之失，半由宦寺。 蓋宦寺出身之始，每以小忠小信，便捷逢迎，無非售其固寵邀恩之計。 及黨與已成，則驕肆專橫，而箝制其上，雖英明之主，竟有百計不能除之者。 當時臣民，切齒痛恨，終歸無可如何。 我朝列聖相承，遠邁前代，不但不准此輩干預政事，雖應對進退間亦不假以辭色，使無由讒諂面諛，浸潤膚受。 是以二百餘年，從不為患。 雖然如此嚴防，尚有防不勝防之慮。 Zong Jichen, whose courtesy name was Difu, came from Kuaiji in Zhejiang. He earned his provincial degree in the first year of Daoguang (1821), was appointed secretary in the Grand Secretariat, and served as a Grand Council clerk. He was promoted to director in the Office of Daily Annals, then to assistant department director in the Ministry of Revenue. In the first year of Xianfeng (1851) he became a supervising censor. He urged the throne to require every province to enforce the baojia household system, arguing that magistrates should stay in post long enough to see reforms through, that registration deadlines should be realistic, that door placards must be checked carefully, that clerks must not extort the public, that junior officials could help supervise, that leading families should be registered first to anchor neighborhood groups, and that reading the law aloud could reinforce the system while tying in militia drill and community granary benefits. The edict went to every provincial governor to adapt and implement the plan according to local conditions. In another memorial on balancing the budget he urged honesty over window-dressing and highlighted three remedies: rigorous audits, voluntary surcharges, and interest-bearing reserve funds. He also proposed adjusting tax collection districts and ordering each prefecture and county to pursue arrears under threat of disciplinary action; the Ministry of Revenue was instructed to review the plan. In the fifth year of Xianfeng (1855), learning that the emperor planned a tomb visit though no travel ban had been announced, Jichen argued that flood-stricken counties south of the capital had barely recovered from years of war and begged that the journey be deferred a year. The emperor replied that the annual tomb visit was a ritual obligation, but that if the capital region could not bear the burden, the court would weigh the circumstances and postpone it. Yet no date for the visit had even been set; Jichen had merely speculated and spoken up, winning a reputation for bold remonstrance without any real issue at stake. Such posturing must not be encouraged! The ministries were ordered to decide his punishment. He soon submitted another memorial: "Since the Taiping rebels took the Yangtze, few civil or military officials have proved able in the field." Generals as rare as Ulang'a, Taqibu, and Jiang Zhongyuan were often underused and died before their talents were exhausted. Sheng Bao, Zhang Liangji, and Yuan Jiasan were willing to take responsibility, yet they too were sidelined and removed on disciplinary grounds. Hunan and Hubei have lately been sustained chiefly by a few scholar-officers such as Hu Linyi and Luo Zinan, who drilled their men in courage and led from the front. In truth Zeng Guofan had paved the way for both of them. A special recruitment track for men skilled in both civil and military affairs would meet the emergency, yet officials hesitate to recommend anyone—afraid of being blamed if the nominee proves incompetent, or of backing a capable man who still fails through bad luck. At a moment of national crisis they cling to routine; after every defeat they blame lack of funds. Supplies must be found, but without commanders a million taels of silver is worthless rubble on the ground. My own horizon is limited, but in Hunan I know Zuo Zongtang, a flexible strategist provincial governors already trust; given an independent command he would rank with Hu Linyi and Luo Zinan. Repeatedly recommended yet hard to place is Yao Chengyu of Huzhou, whose courage and scholarship make him worth employing. Men of deep strategic insight include Zhou Tenghu and Guan Yan of Changzhou and Tang Qihua of Guilin—all engaged with public affairs, yet still living in obscurity in the countryside. If the court broke precedent and brought such men forward in groups, one appointee could pacify several prefectures and a handful could secure an entire war zone. Even with the Yangtze blocked, coordinated offensives could still bring a swift end to the rebellion. I beg Your Majesty to order every official to recommend men of proven civil and military ability, in or out of office, so that the full pool of talent may be mobilized. The memorial was circulated to the provinces with orders to examine Zuo Zongtang and the others and send them to court for audience. Zuo Zongtang was thus brought into service, and observers credited Jichen with recognizing talent. He was promoted to supervising secretary. Large-denomination coins were then in circulation in the capital, to the distress of merchants and the public. Jichen urged restoring standard copper cash under the name "ancestral coins," recasting large coins in iron only, and circulating both side by side. The ministries reviewed the plan but rejected it. He also urged emergency relief for flood victims in the capital region, and the court agreed. He was soon appointed Shandong grain-transport commissioner; when Nian bandits invaded he built fortified walls along the Niutou River at Jining—6,300 zhang on the north bank and 8,600 on the south—which held the line. For this service he received the brevet rank of salt transport commissioner. In the sixth year of Tongzhi (1867) he retired on grounds of illness and died soon after. His father Peizheng had been magistrate of Lingling in Hunan and died leaving nothing beyond an honest official's meager estate. Jichen was devoted to his mother. In scholarship he followed Wang Yangming and Liu Zongzhou. After leaving office he directed the Longshan Academy in Yuyao and the Jishan Academy in Shanyin. While at court he secured a shrine in Ge Yunfei's home county for the fallen general Ge Yunfei; and in Shandong he restored Fang Xiaoru's shrine and published his Correct Learning collection—typical of his efforts to uphold moral example. Yin Gengyun, whose courtesy name was Xingnong, came from Taoyuan in Jiangsu. He passed the metropolitan examination in the thirtieth year of Daoguang (1850), entered the Ministry of Rites as a director, and was later promoted to department director. In 1855, when Taiping forces threatened the capital, Prince Hui Mianyu took overall command with Sengge Rinchen as his deputy; Gengyun joined the staff, wrote on frontier defense, and came to the attention of Emperor Wenzong. In the eighth year of Xianfeng (1858) he became supervising censor for Huguang and acted as supervising secretary in the Revenue Section. In those crisis years he filed several memorials every month. Zhili governor-general Ne'erjing'e had been dismissed for endangering the frontier, then was brought back to office. Gengyun protested: "Ne'erjing'e's failures are notorious, and no one understands why he has been restored." With fighting still raging along the Yangtze, Huai, Chu, and Henan, field commanders risk everything not only from loyalty but because they trust the throne to reward merit and punish failure. If others follow his example the armies may fall apart—what then? Emperor Xuanzong once reappointed Qishan, only to dismiss him after Chen Qingyong's remonstrance. I beg Your Majesty to honor that precedent and revoke the appointment. When the rebels again threatened Wuhan, Gengyun wrote that the city commanded the upper Yangtze, threatened Shaanxi to the north, Hunan and Hubei to the south, the lower Yangtze to the east, and Sichuan to the west—and that north-south wars had always been decided there. Rebel columns now probed northern Hubei, raided Guangji and Huanggang, and closed on the provincial capital; Governor Hu Linyi had only a few thousand men on a long river line and could not cover every threat. Vice President Zeng Guofan is loyal, brave, and steadfast; he should be made imperial commissioner to lead his army to Hubei's aid—no other choice would achieve so much so quickly. After Dingyuan fell, Gengyun warned that Taiping and Nian forces had united and would drive north behind Shandong's shield. Governor Chong'en was relieved the rebels had not slaughtered officials or held cities; after they withdrew he filed false victory reports and papered over failures to deceive his superiors; while outwardly he extorted the people until their resources were drained dry. I beg Your Majesty to dismiss him at once and send a senior replacement to this critical frontier post. Recruit more river forces at Hong Lake, move Fu Zhenbang's entire army to Guzhen and Lingbi, recover northern Anhui, and thereby secure Shandong. After Luzhou fell he argued that the province had been wisely seated at Anqing, forming a triangle with Jiujiang and Nanjing to control the Yangtze. Moving the capital to Luzhou the year before had already sacrificed strategic advantage; now Luzhou itself was lost. While Hu Linyi pressed Jiujiang from Wuhan, Anhui rebels might strike into Hubei from Yingzhou and Huozhou or raid western Zhejiang from Huizhou and Shexian, threatening the empire's heartland. Our armies were scattered on relief columns and worn out chasing every alarm. The rebels can move in every direction while we cannot surround them; capturing a city a day accomplishes nothing. Governor Fuji had lost prestige through repeated defeats; Yuan Jiasan, though only an expectant official, enjoyed popular trust and would throw himself into service if made governor. When Zeng Guofan marched, Gengyun noted that half the empire had been mobilized and millions spent, yet the rebellion endured because commanders repeatedly arrived too late. Zeng Guofan had now trained an elite force that won wherever it struck. Chen Yucheng and Zhang Luoxing massed hundreds of thousands at Qianshan and Taihu—ten times Zeng's strength—and any slip would be catastrophic. Rebel forces at Luzhou, Fengyang, and Liuhe were surely weakened; he urged Yuan Jiasan and Zhang Guoliang to strike their bases on schedule or march by side routes to relieve Zeng's Hunan army and break rebel morale. In another memorial he impeached waterways director-general Gengchang and proposed that Yuan Jiasan hold the post concurrently; he also argued against relying solely on appeasement toward the Yunnan Muslim rebels; he also laid out a comprehensive capital relief program covering fair-price grain sales, procurement, charity relief, and reserves; and exposed long-standing abuses in the currency system; many of his proposals were adopted. When Anglo-French forces attacked Tianjin he filed seven individual memorials and co-signed two more, all urging war; the emperor convened the princes and senior ministers to debate. He clashed with Prince Zheng Duanhua and his faction, argued fiercely, wept, and the council broke up. Gengyun had once been valued by Sushun in the Ministry of Rites, which now made Sushun his enemy. In the ninth year (1859) the examination scandal broke; censors were generally punished for lax oversight, but Gengyun alone bore the brunt for having supervised the eunuchs' examination and was demoted two ranks and transferred. In 1860, with the capital under martial law and the emperor preparing to flee to Rehe, Gengyun drafted a remonstrance for the capital-defense commissioner and wrote personally to Sushun, but the court would not be moved. Vice President Wen Xiang, commanding the Nine Gates, met Gengyun in the eastern city; they wept in each other's arms and planned the defense of the capital left behind. Hu Linyi recommended Gengyun as a strategist and asked that he be recalled to service. Vice Censor-in-Chief Mao Changxi, organizing Henan militia, then had Gengyun transferred to his staff. In 1862 he led five thousand men with Sengge Rinchen against the Jinlou Stockade sectarians and with Brigadier Zhang Yao stormed a Nian stronghold at Zhanggang; he was marked for circuit-intendant promotion and awarded the peacock feather. In 1864 he served as acting intendant of the He-Shaan-Ru circuit. The western expedition bought grain at Shaanzhou in undersized measures and forced counties to make up millions of jin in shortages; Gengyun had the practice abolished entirely. He executed offending allied troops as a public warning. With banditry rife he secured command over Henan and Shaanxi troops, paid them on time, and restored discipline. In 1865, when Zhang Zongyu threatened the capital region, Gengyun marched with Governor Li Henian to Cizhou and proposed a long encirclement to cut the rebels off. He twice acted as grain and salt intendant, helped manage reconstruction, dredged the Huiji River and sealed breaches, and earned the brevet rank of provincial administration commissioner. In 1887 he received permanent appointment to the He-Shaan-Ru intendant post. Corvée burdens in Henan and Shaanxi rivaled regular taxes; he standardized levies and audited them strictly, easing popular distress. In 1877 a severe drought struck; he submitted seven famine-relief measures but died in office before they could be implemented. Gengyun was known for blunt memorials; as a provincial commissioner he was trusted by governors Zhang Zhiwan and Li Henian and advised on many campaigns. After his death Governor Li Qing'ao was impeached over famine relief and false military-pay claims that implicated Gengyun, but Gengyun was eventually cleared. Wang Zheng, originally named Xizhen and courtesy-named Dingfu, came from Maping in Guangxi. He passed the metropolitan examination in 1841, entered the Ministry of Revenue as a director, and served as a Grand Council clerk. When Grand Secretary Sai Shang'a took command in Guangxi, Zheng accompanied him, stirred by the crisis to seek some way to serve. During the Xianfeng era he rose from department director to vice president of the Court of Judicial Review. In the second year of Tongzhi (1863), Song Jingshi, a Nian leader who had surrendered, slipped back from Shaanxi to raid Zhili and Shandong. In a memorial, Zheng wrote: "Song Jingshi has fortified Gang Village with brick ramparts and holds it like a beast at bay. Since his escape from Shaanxi, his core band has numbered only a few hundred." Yet Chonghou and others repeatedly indulged the problem, until coercion swelled his ranks to more than ten thousand. Recently, in Changyi, Shen, Liaocheng, and Linqing, he ordered villages to divide the wheat harvest with tenant farmers and send the shares to Gang Village. Though he bore the name of a surrendered man, his conduct grew ever more brutal and rebellious. I ask that a secret order be sent to Liu Changyou, governor of Zhili, to arrange his summons to camp, expose his crimes, and put him to death. If he defies the order and does not appear, Zhili's regular forces can still cross the border to attack him. Once Song Jingshi is removed, ringleaders such as Yang Pengling and Cheng Shunshu can be executed together, eliminating a major menace and securing the capital region. The memorial was received, but nothing was done. Song Jingshi was eventually executed for treason. While the war was still unresolved, Zeng Guofan proposed raising funds in Guangdong. Lao Chongguang established the lijin levy, and abuses multiplied. Zheng submitted a memorial stating: "The two Guang provinces were the cradle of rebellion. Cenxi and Rongxian had been held by bandits for years." Chen Jingang of Xinyi was an especially formidable enemy, and the bandits operated as one force, spreading in ways hard to uproot. In setting up lijin, Lao Chongguang routinely forced gentry and merchants to contract for collections and advance payments — flesh cut away to douse a fire. Such a method cannot long endure. After Chongguang left office, collections fell off. Recently, lijin agents have been beaten by crowds or paraded in cangues by local people. However unruly the populace may be, the vileness of the officials is plain for all to see. In a region long ravaged by chaos, with bandits entrenched and watching for opportunity, and every possible revenue stream squeezed dry from every side, I fear that for every ten parts of gain there will be a hundred parts of harm. If the two Guang provinces were devastated again, the Court would scarcely know what to do — and the failure would be far more than an inability to collect lijin. He then recommended Tang Qiyin, Guangdong circuit intendant; Guo Songtao, Liangjiang transport commissioner; and Cheng Sunyi, Zhejiang transport commissioner. Guo Songtao was soon put in charge of Guangdong lijin, a direct result of Zheng's memorial. In the third year of Tongzhi, he was promoted to vice minister of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices and served concurrently as acting left vice censor-in-chief. In a memorial he wrote: "Chonglun, Hengqi, Dong Xun, and Xue Huan of the Zongli Yamen are petty and corrupt — a fact known throughout the realm. I fear foreign powers will despise us, believing that even the Court's highest ministers are mostly men of that sort — a humiliation to the dynasty." Even if capable men are scarce, or even if some must remain in the Zongli Yamen, Your Majesty should restrain them appropriately — by assigning nominal posts or empty titles — so that foreign states may respect the strict standards by which we distinguish rank. When the empire hears of this, it will also understand the Court's purpose in embracing all classes of men while keeping foreign powers under control. He was soon made commissioner of the Transmission Office while continuing to serve as acting left vice censor-in-chief. In another memorial he reported: "Suzhou and Hangzhou have recently fallen in turn, and Zhili and Shandong have been pacified." I have observed that undertakings on the verge of success are never brought to completion except through the most scrupulous caution. Although Jinling's rebel stronghold may fall within three or four months, Danyang and Changzhou support each other like horns on a beast, and hardened fighters such as Li Xiucheng have gathered there for a fight to the death. Once Hangzhou and Jiaxing were captured, the remaining rebels consolidated at Huzhou. Rebels slipping from southern Anhui into Jiangxi have spread across two or three hundred li through Yushan, Qianshan, Jinxi, and Jianchang, with reported numbers of eighty or ninety thousand, and some have crossed into Fujian. It is also reported that Li Shixian is leading a large force close behind through Chun'an and Suian. Zeng Guofan and Zuo Zongtang have fought for years and repeatedly urged against seeking quick victory — they share the same long view — yet on defense along the Anhui-Zhejiang border they differ: Guofan would have Huizhou and Ningguo each assign partial garrisons, while Zongtang argued for concentrating forces to seize Guangde and cut the rebels' escape routes. Before the two plans could be decided, the rebels had already broken from Anhui into Jiangxi. The rebels have long lived by plunder, and their numbers are so great that it is often impossible to destroy them in one blow without letting some escape elsewhere. Because these rebels are numerous and their momentum fierce, they press forward relentlessly: before one column is hard struck, another follows on its heels. If they drive deep into the interior of Jiangxi, smoldering embers may flare up again and burn across the land. From Jiangxi they can cross into Fujian and strike directly toward Tingzhou and Chaozhou — a well-worn path for fleeing rebels in recent years. Huang Wenjin came this way; Shi Dakai went out the same way. Past events should serve as a stern warning. Imperial edicts have repeatedly charged Zeng Guofan, Zuo Zongtang, Li Hongzhang, Shen Baozhen, and the governors of Fujian and Guangdong to stand ready. At this moment, when the great victory is nearly won, all should act with one purpose, coordinating offense and defense, cutting off fleeing rebels in front and pursuing from behind, so that wherever they go they are battered and daily weakened until they cannot regroup — lest they become a catastrophe anew. I have another concern: the Anhui and Zhejiang armies have fought the rebels for a long time, and Guofan and Zongtang have each raised funds from Jiangsu, Huguang, and other provinces. Guofan proposed establishing a central bureau in Jiangsu, using that province's levy contributions as the lifeline for a hundred thousand Anhui troops. The Zhejiang army cannot spare funds; even the monthly pay for Guofan's own troops, it is said, is disbursed at only a fraction. Out of necessity they turned to Guangdong levies, yet those too cannot be raised quickly. That the people cannot see far ahead and each pursues his own interest is only to be expected. If Guangdong behaves thus, can Jiangxi be any different? Recently Shen Baozhen asked that Jiangxi's tea tax, brokerage lijin, and similar revenues be retained for collection by the province. The ministry approved keeping half, and Guofan and the others would surely not take offense. Yet the men at the front, sleeping on their arms and shedding blood in eager hope of victory, may suddenly hear that their pay is to be cut. Doubt and fear may spread through the ranks — how then can they be fed, roused, and kept fighting? I propose that the governors of Jiangxi, Anhui, Huguang, and Guangdong be ordered that, at this critical moment, however hard it may be to rearrange affairs, they must give their utmost and act in full concert. Muslim rebellion in Gansu is not yet subdued; remnant Nian bands still roam the Central Plains; Chen Daxi of Runan and others have fled into Hubei, advancing from Suizhou and Zaoyang toward Xiangyang and Fan; Zhang Zongyu has broken out of the Nantai Mountains into Henan and Shaanxi, and there is constant fear that these forces may join together; Rebels from Hanzhong have all fled along the route through Ningqiang, southern Shaanxi, and Shangzhou, and it is reported they will gather at Xiangyang and Fan to reinforce Jinling — a threat that must not be taken lightly. At present Shaanxi's military affairs are divided among too many hands. Li Yunlin pursued rebels at Shangluo, then suddenly marched west. At Xing'an he failed to stop their escape; at Hanyin he shunned battle and allowed his men to loot at will. He should be checked. Liu Rong is a scholar of real ability, and the Hanzhong rebels were originally his responsibility alone. Yet they have broken out in every direction, and he above all should vow to destroy them — or fail in his charge. Duolong'a once enjoyed the highest reputation and was widely hailed as the empire's foremost general, yet lately his standing has declined. An imperial admonition should be sent to him. Lei Zhenguan has won victories wherever he has been sent and is surely fit to hold an independent command, yet among Gansu's officials there are scarcely one or two upright men to support him. It is now reported that Lanzhou is cut off from Qingyang. Enlin holds the governor's seal in an acting capacity, but he is no more than a man skilled at convenient orders, narrow and inferior in judgment; Xilin holds Qingyang and Ningxia, yet is misled by Qingyun's repeated errors. I respectfully submit that upright and capable ministers should be chosen at once to restore order there. In the empire today, one cannot lightly speak of universal peace. Yet as military affairs in north and south gradually settle, if the western campaigns can also be brought under control, that will mark a broad restoration of order. The Court is the root of the realm. When the palace and government are clear and disciplined, that spirit resonates with the vigor of the battlefield and the two reinforce each other. The empire's great tide turns day by day, yet grave difficulties remain. Some may too quickly assume that the times are clear, affairs smooth, and the realm already governed and secure. Human nature tends to delight in novelty, cling to routine, fear hardship, and chase easy gain. Only the truly sincere man who grasps the moment can see this clearly and warn against it. Of old, Zhuge Liang was called the greatest man since the Three Dynasties, yet history praises him above all for caution. Zhu Xi warned Emperor Xiaozong of Song: "Let the poison of complacent ease daily grow stronger, and the resolve to sleep on brushwood and taste gall daily fade and be forgotten." Unable to contain my private anxiety, perhaps overstated, I venture to lay these thoughts before Your Majesty. The memorial was received and noted. He soon asked to retire and died. Mu Jixiang'a, courtesy name Junan, was a Manchu of the Bordered Red Banner. He rose from principal clerk in the Ministry of Works to director. In the fourth year of Tongzhi (1865), he was appointed supervising censor for the Shandong circuit. He submitted a memorial urging careful selection of eunuchs, writing in part: "The Emperor came to the throne at a tender age. His sacred learning and understanding deepen daily. Those who attend him should be chosen with the greatest care, lest in time they beguile and corrupt his judgment." Looking back from the late Han through the former Ming, half the failures of court governance arose from eunuchs. At the start of their careers, eunuchs win favor through small acts of loyalty and quick compliance — all of it designed to secure patronage and reward. Once their faction is formed, they grow arrogant and overbearing, constraining their superiors; even wise rulers have found them impossible to remove by any means. Officials and common people alike hated them bitterly, yet in the end could do nothing. Our dynasty's successive sage emperors, surpassing earlier ages, not only barred eunuchs from affairs of state but also refused them even the courtesy of pleasant conversation, leaving no opening for slander, flattery, or slow corruption. For more than two hundred years, they therefore never became a menace. Yet even with such strict precautions, there is fear that vigilance may not suffice. In the Guiyou uprising of the Jiaqing era, eunuchs were still found colluding with rebels — clear proof of their unreliability. Today the Empress Dowager reigns from behind the curtain, fully aware of these evils, stopping trouble at its root and taking warning from the past, so that petty schemers cannot obscure her judgment. Hence she knows men and uses them well, and court governance is clear and disciplined. Even when the Emperor assumes personal rule in a few years, he will surely not indulge such men to the ruin of state affairs. Why should a subject worry overmuch? Yet the loyalty of one who would offer even his small service cannot be suppressed. At this time, when the Emperor's sacred learning is expanding, all ministers may serve with full devotion, but eunuchs above all require deliberate scrutiny. I hold that eunuchs exist only to run errands and obey commands. Young, quick-witted men must never be kept constantly at the Emperor's side. I ask that the Empress Dowager choose loyal, upright, and mature men to attend the Emperor day and night, so that when he later rules in person he will not be deceived or led astray, and his boundless virtue will rest on this foundation. In the fifth year of Tongzhi, he memorialized that Grand Secretary Zeng Guofan, having long commanded the campaign against the Nian without success, should be censured. The Emperor noted that Guofan had repeatedly accepted blame and had been specially ordered back to his post to manage supplies; though he had not achieved full victory, this was not the same as dereliction in the field. He dismissed the memorial as excessive and took no action. He was transferred out to serve as prefect of Puzhou in Shanxi and soon died. Mu Jixiang'a was thoroughly versed in state affairs, and his family's collection of court gazettes was nearly complete from the founding of the dynasty. You Baichuan, courtesy name Huidong, came from Binzhou in Shandong. He passed the jinshi examination in the first year of Tongzhi (1862), was selected as a Hanlin bachelor, and appointed a compiler. In the sixth year of Tongzhi, he was made a censor and assigned to inspect the West City. The imperial clansman Kuanhe and others had acted lawlessly in many ways. Baichuan memorialized for their impeachment and punishment, and for a time the privileged at court checked their behavior. In the seventh year, Nian rebels fled from Shandong into Zhili. Baichuan asked that commanding generals be ordered to suppress them swiftly and also urged a strict ban on opium cultivation in every province. The Emperor accepted both proposals. He memorialized on the longstanding abuses of yamen clerks throughout the central and provincial administrations, and an edict was issued ordering strict prohibition. He further wrote: "To eliminate clerk abuses, one must first cleanse official ranks, and above all revive the morale of the scholar-gentry." I ask that the heads of each ministry and department select several able men in every bureau, grant them real authority, and hold them personally responsible. When clerks are found guilty of misconduct, report the facts truthfully, and judge merit and failure by diligence or neglect. When rewards and punishments are clear, capable men will naturally exert themselves. Provincial officials already have the power to discipline clerks. Each governor-general and governor should be strictly ordered to fit men to places, and not use places to test unfit men. Recommend the worthy and impeach the unworthy; appoint upright officials to conduct regular inspections; and when corrupt officials or predatory clerks are found, report them in detail to the throne. The Yellow River shifted north, and Shandong's prefectures and counties suffered repeated floods. Baichuan urged relief measures; Waterways Director Wen Bin and Governor Ding Baozhen proposed restoring the old Huai-Xu route, and the court ordered a ministerial conference. Baichuan argued: "The Yellow River may flow south or north, but the court must settle on one coherent policy." If the plan is to restore the old course, three issues cannot be rushed: taking the Daqing River as the main channel would overflow the old bed, requiring a wider riverbed — how would farmland and homes be relocated? Land surveys must be thorough; even a widened channel might not hold the flow, forcing branch cuts — the Tuo, Majia, Goupan, and Gejin routes are named candidates, but each opening must be weighed for benefit and harm; northward diversion would be unprecedented; poor management could stir unrest and public outcry, so timing and method demand caution. He asked that a senior minister survey the entire river before any final decision. In the twelfth year of Tongzhi (1873) the Emperor took personal rule and ordered repairs to the Old Summer Palace for the Empress Dowager's residence. Censor Shen Huai asked to defer the work; the Emperor issued a special edict citing filial duty to both palaces, limiting repairs to the Anyou Palace for ancestral portraits and the Empress Dowager's residence and offices, insisting on frugality and forbidding further construction. Baichuan remonstrated again; summoned and rebuked, he spoke without flinching and moved the Emperor. For a time his reputation as a bold remonstrator resounded through the capital. He soon left for mourning, and after the mourning period returned to office as supervising secretary. In 1879 he became Hunan's Heng-Yong-Chen-Gui circuit intendant, then Sichuan judicial commissioner, Shuntian metropolitan prefect, and vice president of the Grain Granaries. In 1883 a breach on the Shandong river flooded dozens of counties; Baichuan was sent to assist Governor Chen Shijie with relief and river works. Riding light, he toured both banks and the full course of the river, distributing emergency relief first. He jointly proposed distant dikes on both banks with inner thread dikes to contain the Yellow River's flood; and dredging the Xiaoqing River to divert Yellow River water to the sea — both measures were adopted. Back in the capital, a fire in the granaries led to his dismissal. He lived at home several more years and died. (Supplementary biography) Shen Huai, courtesy name Dongchuan, came from Yin County in Zhejiang. He earned his provincial degree in the twenty-ninth year of Daoguang (1849), entered the Grand Secretariat, and served as a Grand Council clerk. In 1860, when Emperor Wenzong fled to Rehe, Shen could not accompany him; he wept and tried to throw himself into a well, but his family restrained him. He rose to director and assistant department director in the Ministry of Justice, then became supervising censor for the Shaanxi circuit. He impeached Revenue Ministry director Yang Hongdian for graft and abuse of power; Yang was arrested but punished only with a minor demotion, and was not dismissed until Yan Jingming became minister. When palace garden construction began, Shen led the memorials opposing it and was as celebrated as Baichuan. In 1875 he supervised the Shuntian provincial examination; though ill he persevered, and died soon after the exams closed. His family had been comfortably off, but in Beijing he sold nearly all he had; contemporaries especially honored his integrity. Commentary: In wartime circumstances change by the hour, and capital officials who argue from rumor often miss the mark. Zong Jichen stressed finding the right men; Yin Gengyun judged generals' failures; Wang Zheng urged frontier officials to cooperate and focus on defeating the rebels — each saw the larger strategic picture. Wang Zheng's proposals were especially thorough because he served on the Grand Council and read military dispatches rather than relying on rumor. Mu Jixiang'a urged caution in choosing eunuchs; You Baichuan and others opposed rebuilding the Old Summer Palace — their reputations for fearless remonstrance were well earned.

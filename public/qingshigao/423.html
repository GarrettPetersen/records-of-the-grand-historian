=宗稷辰=宗稷辰，字滌甫，浙江會稽人。 道光元年舉人，授內閣中書，充軍機章京。 遷起居注主事，再遷戶部員外郎。 咸豐元年，遷御史。 疏請飭各省實行保甲，略言：「州縣宜久任，時日宜寬假，填寫門牌當詳細核對，董事胥役毋派費累民，酌用丞簿以為襄助，先編巨族以為聯屬，並可申明讀法之典，兼收團練、社倉之益。 」詔下直省督撫，各就地體察參酌行之。 又疏言通籌出入，宜崇實去偽，舉清查、報效、生息三端； 又疏請酌改經徵處，分令州縣戴罪嚴催：並下戶部覈議。 五年，聞上將謁陵，未有旨戒行，稷辰疏言：「畿南州縣被水，連歲用兵，民氣甫行休息，籥請展緩一年。 」上諭曰：「每歲謁陵，事同典禮，如果畿輔民力未逮，亦必權衡時勢，暫緩舉行。 今茲並未降旨何日謁陵，宗稷辰揣度陳奏，徒博敢諫之名而無其實。 此風不可長！ 」下部議處。 尋又奏言：「自粵匪竄據長江，數年以來，文臣武將，能戰者稀。 如烏蘭泰、塔齊布、江忠源皆難得之將，而多不盡其用，且以死殉。 如勝保、張亮基、袁甲三皆勇於任事，而亦未盡其用，以罪罷去。 近日支持兩湖，賴有一二書生，如胡林翼、羅澤南，能以練膽為士卒先。 此二人者，實曾國籓有以開之。 此時若開文武兼資一科，誠足濟當時之急，而臣工多不敢薦舉者，一恐其才疏而得過，一恐其遇蹇而罔功。 處愁眉焚頂之時，守蹈常習故之轍，見有敗衄，動以餉匱為辭。 餉固不可不籌，試思用兵乏人，雖斂金百萬，棄如土苴，終歸無用。 臣聞見隘陋，非能盡識天下之才，所知湖南有左宗棠，通權達變，為疆吏所倚重，若使獨當一面，必不下於林翼、澤南。 其屢經論薦，難進易退，肝膽經術，實可取材者，有若湖州之姚承輿。 其策議深沉，才識過人者，有若常州之週騰虎、管晏，桂林之唐啟華，皆關心時務，今尚鬱鬱伏處田間。 誠能破格招賢，連茹並進，則得一人可以平數州，得數人可以清一路。 長江雖阻，當不難分道建功，剋日平定。 伏乞皇上命內外臣工各舉所知，無論已仕未仕，果能文武兼資，皆許徵起，必可網羅而盡得之。 」疏入，下各督撫，命以宗棠等加考送部引見。 宗棠自此膺簡拔，論者謂其知人。 遷給事中。 時京師行大錢，商民苦之。 稷辰上疏請復用制錢，號曰「祖錢」，而大錢改純用鐵鑄，兼行並用。 下部議，格不行。 又以畿輔水患，疏請急賑，從之。 尋授山東運河道，捻匪入境，於濟寧牛頭河濱築戰牆，北岸六千三百丈，南岸八千六百丈，賴以守禦。 以功加鹽運使銜。 同治六年，引疾歸，尋卒。 稷辰父霈正，官湖南零陵知縣，廉無餘貲。 稷辰事母孝。 為學宗王守仁、劉宗周。 罷官後，主餘姚龍山書院、山陰蕺山書院。 官京朝，請祀總兵葛雲飛本籍； 官山東，請修方孝孺祠，並刻正學集：其振勵風教多類此。 =尹耕雲=尹耕雲，字杏農，江蘇桃源人。 道光三十年進士，授禮部主事，再遷郎中。 咸豐五年，粵匪犯畿輔，惠親王綿愉為大將軍，僧格林沁參贊軍務，闢耕雲佐幕府，上書論防務，為文宗所知。 八年，授湖廣道監察御史，署戶科給事中。 時方多事，封章月數上。 直隸總督訥爾經額坐貽誤封疆罷，復起。 耕雲疏言：「訥爾經額之罪，天下共聞共見，未喻其復行起用之故。 方今江、淮、楚、豫軍務未靖，秉鉞之臣，星羅棋布，所以奮不顧身，必欲滅此朝食者，固由篤於忠義，亦以國家信賞必罰，有以畏服其心。 萬一效尤解體，患何可言？ 昔宣宗起用琦善，以陳慶鏞之言而罷。 伏原紹述心傳，收回成命。 」時粵匪复窺武漢，耕雲疏言：「武漢地踞上游，北窺關陝，南脅湖湘，東撼吳越，西製巴蜀，自古南北用兵，皆出死力爭之。 今賊窺伺楚北，分擾廣濟、黃岡，逼近省城，撫臣胡林翼兵勇數千，眾寡懸絕，江路綿遠，首尾不能兼顧。 侍郎曾國籓忠勇樸誠，應請授為欽差大臣，率其所部援湖北，較諸他臣事半功倍。 」粵匪陷定遠，耕雲疏言：「定遠失守，粵、捻新合，必謀北竄，恃山東為之屏蔽。 撫臣崇恩幸其不戕官據城，於賊退後虛報勝仗，內則巧為彌縫，掩一人耳目； 外則恣其朘削，竭萬姓脂膏。 惟懇俯念籓籬重地，立予罷斥，簡大員往代。 於洪湖​​多募水師，兼飭傅振邦全軍移駐固鎮、靈壁，冀收皖北，以固山東。 」及廬州失守，又​​疏言：「昔人建省安慶，與九江、江寧為犄角，控扼長江。 上年徙治廬州，已失形勝，茲並廬州亦不能守。 胡林翼等自武漢進逼九江，而安徽之賊，或自英、霍走湖北，牽我上游，或自徽、歙擾浙西，窺我腹地。 我軍分道救援，罷於奔命。 賊有四達之路，我無三面之圍，雖日克一城，何益？ 撫臣福濟屢挫損威，候補京堂袁甲三素得民心，如以為巡撫，必奮身圖報。 」及國籓進師，疏言：「軍興以來，徵調半天下，糜餉數千萬，卒未能掃穴擒渠，則以屢後時而數失機也。 今曾國籓蓄養精銳，所向克捷。 陳玉成、張洛行率悍賊數十萬，齊向潛山、太湖抗拒，眾寡之數，十倍於我，一有疏虞，關係甚重。 此時廬、鳳、六合賊勢必單，請飭袁甲三、張國樑刻期搗其巢穴，逼令反顧，或令間道為楚師聲援，亦足褫其狂魄。 」別疏劾河道總督庚長，請以甲三兼攝； 又論雲南迴匪不宜專意主撫； 又陳京師本計，平糶、採買、周恤、蓄積諸事宜並舉； 又言錢法積弊：諸疏多見採納。 英、法合軍犯天津，耕雲專疏者七，會疏者二，力主決戰，上命王大臣集議。 與鄭親王端華等議不合，耕云抗辯痛哭而罷。 耕云初在禮部，肅順頗重之，乃是為所憎。 九年，科場獄起，以科道失糾下吏議，而耕雲以充內監試譴獨重，鐫二級調用。 十年，京師戒嚴，上將幸熱河，耕雲代團防大臣草疏諫阻，復自以書抵肅順，卒不聽。 侍郎文祥提督九門，遇耕雲東城，相持哭，因為規畫留守諸事。 胡林翼疏薦耕雲胸有權略，請起用。 會副都御史毛昶熙治河南團練，疏調從軍。 同治元年，率部卒五千，從僧格林沁平金樓寨教匪，又偕提督張曜克張岡捻巢，以道員記名，賜花翎。 三年，署河陝汝道。 西征軍購糧陝州，市斛小，責屬縣償其不足，凡數百萬斤，耕雲悉請罷之。 客軍有不法者，斬以徇。 境多刀匪，請得節制河、陝兵，饋餉以時，兵咸用命。 四年，張總愚犯畿輔，耕雲從巡撫李鶴年進軍磁州，建策築長圍斷賊歸路。 兩署糧儲鹽法道，佐治善後事，濬惠濟河，塞河決，敘勞加布政使銜。 十三年，補河陝汝道。 河、陝徭役重，亞於常賦，耕雲立定制，嚴稽覈，民困稍甦。 光緒三年，大旱，條上救荒七事，未及行，卒於官。 耕雲在言路著直聲，出任監司，巡撫張之萬、李鶴年皆倚重之，軍事多所贊畫。 卒後，巡撫李慶翱以災荒被劾，牽及冒領兵餉事，辭連耕雲，後終得白云。 =王拯=王拯，初名錫振，字定甫，廣西馬平人。 道光二十一年進士，授戶部主事，充軍機章京。 大學士賽尚阿視師廣西，以拯從，拯感時多難，慷慨思有所建白。 咸豐間，自郎中累遷大理寺少卿。 同治二年，降捻宋景詩由陝西還擾直隸、山東，拯奏言：「景詩岡屯磚圩，儼然嵎固，自陝逸回，其黨不過數百。 崇厚等一再養癰，裹脅逾萬。 近复於昌邑、莘、聊城、臨清四州縣，令村莊將所獲麥與佃戶平分，運送岡屯，是其名為降伏，心跡轉益兇悖。 請密敕直隸督臣劉長佑計調來營，暴其罪而誅之。 若抗違不至，直隸官軍猶能越境進剿。 景詩既除，如楊蓬嶺、程順書等首惡，皆可駢誅，以除巨憝，以安畿輔。 」疏入，未行。 其後景詩卒以叛誅。 軍事未定，曾國籓議於廣東籌餉，勞崇光創辦釐金，諸弊叢起。 拯疏言：「兩粵為肇亂之區，岑溪、容縣，數載皆為賊踞。 信宜陳金缸尤為巨憝，群賊相為一氣，滋蔓難圖。 勞崇光舉辦釐金，率令紳商包充墊繳，燃眉剜肉，事何可常？ 及崇光去任，徵收減少。 近乃有釐務委員，或為眾所毆傷，或為民間枷號，雖民情頑獷，而官吏惡劣亦可概見。 以積年久亂之地，有負嵎圜視之賊，當一切利孔、百方搜剔之時，臣竊恐利未十而害已百。 萬一兩粵复糜爛，更不知何所措手足，豈惟釐金不能辦而已？ 」因薦廣東道員唐啟廕、兩淮運使郭嵩燾、浙江運使成孫詒。 旋用嵩燾督廣東釐金，自拯疏發之也。 三年，遷太常寺卿，署左副都御史。 疏論：「總理各國事務大臣侍郎崇綸、恆祺、董恂、薛煥委瑣齷齪，通國皆知，竊恐外邦輕侮，以為中朝卿貳之班，大都不過如若曹等，未免為中朝恥辱。 就令人材難得，或於總理衙門位置為宜，上應量為裁抑，或處以散職，或畀以虛銜，庶外邦服我旌別之嚴。 四方聞之，亦釋然於朝廷宥納群倫、羈縻彼族之意。 」尋遷通政使，仍署左副都御史。 疏言：「近日蘇、杭迭克，直、東肅清。 臣觀從來將興之業，垂成之功，未有不矢以小心，而始能底定者。 金陵賊窟雖計於三四月間可拔，而丹陽與常州犄角，百戰悍賊如李秀成等，麇集死守。 杭、嘉既克，餘黨歸併湖州。 其自皖南竄越江西之賊，蔓延玉山、鉛山、金谿、建昌二三百里，眾號八九萬，並有闌入福建境者。 又聞李世賢自率巨股由淳安、遂安接踵而至，曾國籓、左宗棠等用兵日久，前此屢陳不亟求功旦夕，同一老謀深計，獨於皖、浙毗境豫作防維之策，則國籓意在徽、寧各飭所部分防，宗棠以為不若並力取廣德扼賊竄路。 兩議未及定，賊已由皖竄贛。 賊又草竊已久，人數太眾，勢多不能聚殲而弗使一賊他遁。 臣則以此賊人多勢劇，一意奔突，前股未痛剿，後股又踵接。 萬一深入江西腹地，燼餘复熾，又至燎原。 且由贛逾閩，可以直走汀、潮，為數年來竄匪熟路。 黃文金由此而來，石達開由此而去，前事可為深警。 疊蒙諭旨，曾國籓、左宗棠、李鴻章、沈葆楨及閩、粵各督撫諄諄戒備。 當此大功將竟，惟當效力一心，互籌戰守，務將分竄諸賊，前截後追，必使所至創夷，日就衰殘零落，不得喙息，以成巨患。 臣尤有請者，皖、浙諸軍與賊相持不為不久，所需餉項，國籓、宗棠等各於江、楚等省自為籌畫。 國籓奏於江省設立總台，以一省捐釐之數，為皖軍十萬養命之源。 浙軍固不能分撥，即國籓所部月餉，傳聞亦祗放數成，不得已而籌及廣東釐捐，乃又不能遽辦。 夫民之不能見遠而各為其私者，情也。 廣東有之，江西豈獨不然？ 日前沈葆楨奏請將江西茶稅、牙釐等款歸本省任收，旋用部議允留其半，在國籓等斷不至觖望。 惟軍前將卒，當枕戈喋血切望成功之時，忽聞軍餉來源將減，眾心或生疑懼，何以得飽騰而資鼓舞？ 擬請飭贛、皖、楚、粵各疆臣，值此事機至緊，無論如何變通為難，總當殫竭血誠，同心共濟。 甘肅回氛未戢，中州餘捻尚存，汝南陳大喜等竄逸湖北，自隨、棗逼襄、樊； 張總愚自南台山中出竄內、淅，時虞合併； 漢中之賊，全竄寧、陝、商州一路，聞將會齊襄、樊回援金陵，誠亦未可輕忽。 目前陝省軍務，政出多門，李雲麟追賊商於，忽卷旆而西，其在興安，未能遏賊竄逸，其在漢陰，遇賊避匿，縱勇淫掠，宜量加裁抑。 劉蓉素嘗學問，懷負非常，漢中之賊，本所專辦，而竄擾四出，尤當誓志盪除，方為不負。 多隆阿聲望最優，眾口爭傳為第一名將，乃近日聲望漸損，宜申聖諭訓飭。 雷正綰所向克捷，諒足當一面之寄，顧全甘官吏，未有一二正人支持其間。 現聞蘭州與慶陽隔絕，恩麟權督印，不過使令便闢之材，識見陋劣； 熙麟坐守慶陽、寧夏一區，又為慶昀種種紕繆所誤。 臣愚以為亟宜遴簡公正有為之大臣，鎮撫整飭。 今之天下，何易遽言率土奠安，而南北軍務漸定，西事再能就緒，亦即為大致之澄清。 朝廷者天下之本，宮府清明嚴肅，與疆場奮迅振拔之氣，相感而自通。 天下大勢日轉，而亦正多難鉅之事，或遽以為時局清明，事機暢遂，若已治已安者然。 人情大抵喜新狃常，畏難而務獲，獨有當幾至誠君子，為能深察而切戒之。 昔諸葛亮為三代下一人，史獨稱之以謹慎。 硃子進戒宋孝宗曰：​​'使宴安酖毒之害，日滋而日長； 將臥薪嘗膽之志，日遠而日忘。 '臣不勝私憂過計，冒昧瀝陳。 」疏入，報聞。 尋告歸，卒。 =穆緝香阿=穆緝香阿，字居南，滿洲鑲紅旗人。 由工部主事再遷郎中。 同治四年，授山東道監察御史。 疏請慎擇宦寺，略言：「皇上沖齡御極，聖學日新，知識日開，左右侍從之輩，宜豫加慎選，勿使將來蠱惑聖聰。 溯自漢末及前明，朝政之失，半由宦寺。 蓋宦寺出身之始，每以小忠小信，便捷逢迎，無非售其固寵邀恩之計。 及黨與已成，則驕肆專橫，而箝制其上，雖英明之主，竟有百計不能除之者。 當時臣民，切齒痛恨，終歸無可如何。 我朝列聖相承，遠邁前代，不但不准此輩干預政事，雖應對進退間亦不假以辭色，使無由讒諂面諛，浸潤膚受。 是以二百餘年，從不為患。 雖然如此嚴防，尚有防不勝防之慮。 Zong Jichen, whose style name was Difu, came from Kuaiji in Zhejiang. He earned his juren degree in Daoguang 1 (1821), was appointed a Grand Secretariat drafter, and served as a clerk on the Grand Council. He was promoted to director in the Office of the Imperial Diary, then again to assistant director in the Ministry of Revenue. In Xianfeng 1 (1851) he was made a censor. He submitted a memorial urging the provinces to enforce the baojia household-registration system in earnest. He argued that magistrates needed long tenures and reasonable deadlines; door placards needed careful verification; clerks must not pad fees at the people's expense; deputy prefects and registrars could serve as aides; great clans should be registered first to form mutual ties; collective reading rituals could be proclaimed; and militia drill together with community granaries would bring further benefit. The throne ordered each provincial governor to assess local conditions and implement the plan as appropriate. In another memorial on balancing revenue and expenditure, he urged scrapping pretense for solid facts, and set forth three measures: rigorous audits, voluntary buyouts of office, and interest-bearing reserves. He also asked that tax collection be reorganized and that prefects and magistrates be ordered to press collection under personal liability; the proposal was sent to the Ministry of Revenue for review. In his fifth year as censor, learning that the emperor planned a tomb visit though no travel restriction had been announced, Jichen memorialized that the flooded districts south of the capital, after years of war, had only begun to catch their breath, and he begged a year's postponement. The emperor replied that the annual tomb pilgrimage was a ritual obligation, but if the capital region's people could not bear it, the court must weigh the times and defer the rite. Yet no date had even been set for this year's visit—Jichen had only guessed and spoken up, winning a name for bold remonstrance without the substance to match. Such conduct must not be indulged! The case was referred to the ministries for disciplinary handling. He soon submitted another memorial: "Since the Taiping rebels seized the Yangtze, capable civil and military commanders have been scarce for years." Men such as Ulanga, Taqibu, and Jiang Zhongyuan were generals of rare quality, yet the court often failed to use them fully, and they died in the field. Shengbao, Zhang Liangji, and Yuan Jiasan were all willing to take on hard duties, yet they too were not fully used and were removed on charges. Recently the defense of Hunan and Hubei has rested on a few scholar-officers such as Hu Linyi and Luo Zinan, who led by example in drilling and hardening their men. In fact Zeng Guofan had opened the way for both of them. Opening a special examination for men skilled in both civil and military affairs would truly meet the urgent need of the hour, yet many officials dare not recommend anyone—fearing blame if the nominee proves incompetent, or futility if fortune turns against him. In an hour when worry presses from every side, they cling to routine; at every defeat they plead empty coffers. Supplies must be planned, but without capable commanders even a million in silver is worthless—money alone cannot win a war. My own knowledge is limited and cannot compass every talent in the empire, but in Hunan I know Zuo Zongtang—resourceful, adaptable, and trusted by frontier governors. Given independent command, he would not rank below Hu Linyi or Luo Zinan. Among men often discussed in memorials—slow to advance yet quick to withdraw, but possessed of courage and classical learning—Yao Chengyu of Huzhou is truly fit for service. Men of deep policy insight and outstanding ability include Zhou Tenghu and Guan Yan of Changzhou and Tang Qihua of Guilin—all engaged with public affairs, yet still living in obscurity in the countryside. If the court would break precedent to recruit talent in clusters, one able man could pacify several prefectures, and several such men could clear an entire circuit. Though the Yangtze stands in the way, forces could strike on separate fronts and bring the rebellion to an end in due course. I humbly beg Your Majesty to order officials everywhere to recommend those they know—whether already in office or not—and to summon all who truly combine civil and military talent, so that no able man is left out. When the memorial arrived, the throne ordered the governors to examine Zuo Zongtang and the others further and send them to the capital for audience. From this Zuo Zongtang won imperial selection, and commentators praised Jichen for knowing talent when he saw it. He was promoted to supervising secretary. At the time the capital was circulating large-denomination coins, to the merchants' and people's distress. Jichen memorialized to restore standard copper cash under the name "ancestral coin," while large coins would be recast in iron only, both denominations to circulate side by side. The ministries deliberated and rejected the proposal. When flooding struck the capital region, he memorialized for urgent relief, and the court agreed. He was soon appointed commissioner of the Shandong grain-transport circuit. When Nian bandits crossed the border, he built defensive walls along the Niutou River at Jining—6,300 zhang on the north bank and 8,600 on the south—which held the line. For his service he received the brevet rank of salt transport commissioner. In Tongzhi 6 (1867) he retired on grounds of illness and soon died. Jichen's father Peizheng had been magistrate of Lingling in Hunan—honest and leaving no surplus wealth behind. Jichen was devoted to his mother. In scholarship he followed Wang Yangming and Liu Zongzhou. After leaving office he directed Longshan Academy in Yuyao and Jishan Academy in Shanyin. While at court he petitioned for a shrine to Brigadier Ge Yunfei in Ge's home district; in Shandong he had Fang Xiaoru's shrine restored and the Correct Learning Collection printed—his efforts to revive moral culture were largely of this kind. Yin Gengyun, whose style name was Xingnong, came from Taoyuan in Jiangsu. He passed the jinshi examination in Daoguang 30 (1850), was appointed a director in the Ministry of Rites, and was later promoted to bureau director. In Xianfeng 5 (1855), when Taiping forces threatened the capital region, Prince Hui Mianyu was appointed grand general with Sengge Rinchen as his deputy; Gengyun joined the staff, wrote on defense, and came to Emperor Xianfeng's attention. In the eighth year he was made investigating censor for the Huguang circuit and served concurrently as acting supervising secretary of the Revenue Section. In those troubled times he submitted several sealed memorials each month. Zhili governor-general Ne'erjing'e had been dismissed for mismanaging the frontier, then was reappointed. Gengyun memorialized: "Ne'erjing'e's failures are known to all, yet no one understands why he has been brought back into office." With war still raging along the Yangtze, Huai, Chu, and Henan, commanders are posted everywhere. Men who risk all to crush the enemy act from loyalty—but also because they trust the throne to reward merit and punish failure. If others follow his example and the armies lose heart, the harm is beyond words. Emperor Daoguang once reappointed Qishan, but withdrew the order after Chen Qingyong remonstrated. I humbly beg Your Majesty to follow that worthy precedent and rescind the appointment. " When the rebels again threatened Wuhan, Gengyun memorialized that the city commanded the upper Yangtze—overlooking Shaanxi to the north, threatening Hunan and Hubei to the south, menacing Jiangsu and Zhejiang to the east, and holding Sichuan to the west—and that from antiquity every north–south war had been fought to the death for its possession. Now the rebels probe northern Hubei, raid Guangji and Huanggang, and press the provincial capital. Governor Hu Linyi has only a few thousand men—far too few—and the long river line cannot be held at both ends. Vice Minister Zeng Guofan is loyal, brave, and steadfast; he should be made imperial commissioner to lead his troops to relieve Hubei—no other commander could do half as much. " After the rebels took Dingyuan, Gengyun warned that Taiping and Nian forces had joined and would surely drive north, using Shandong as their shield. Governor Chong'en was lucky the rebels did not slaughter officials or hold the cities; after they withdrew he filed false victory reports, inwardly papering over the truth to deceive his superiors; outwardly he squeezed the people until their wealth was exhausted. I beg Your Majesty to consider this vital frontier post, dismiss him at once, and send a senior replacement. Recruit more river troops at Hong Lake, and order Fu Zhenbang's entire army to garrison Guzhen and Lingbi, to recover northern Anhui and secure Shandong. " When Luzhou fell as well, he memorialized again that the provincial seat had once been at Anqing, forming a strategic triangle with Jiujiang and Nanjing to control the Yangtze. Moving the seat to Luzhou last year had already forfeited the strategic advantage; now Luzhou itself cannot be held. Hu Linyi was pressing Jiujiang from Wuhan, while Anhui rebels might strike into Hubei from Yingzhou and Huozhou to tie down our upper Yangtze forces, or raid western Zhejiang from Huizhou and Shexian to threaten the interior. Our armies were split among rescue columns and exhausted from rushing to every alarm. The rebels could move in every direction while we could not surround them on three sides—even taking a city a day would accomplish little. Governor Fuk'ichi had suffered repeated defeats and lost authority; Yuan Jiasan, an expectant capital official, had long enjoyed popular trust—made governor, he would surely repay the appointment with his utmost effort. " When Zeng Guofan took the field, Gengyun memorialized that since the war began half the empire had been mobilized and tens of millions spent, yet the rebel leadership remained at large—because commanders had repeatedly arrived too late and lost their chance. Now Zeng Guofan had trained elite troops and was winning wherever he fought. Chen Yucheng and Zhang Luoxing were leading hundreds of thousands of hardened rebels against Qianshan and Taihu—outnumbering Zeng's force ten to one; any slip would have grave consequences. Rebel strength at Luzhou, Fengyang, and Liuhe must now be thin; I beg that Yuan Jiasan and Zhang Guoliang be ordered to strike their bases on a fixed schedule, forcing them to turn back, or sent by side routes to support Zeng's Hunan army—either move would break their confidence. " In a separate memorial he impeached Grand Canal director Gengchang and asked that Yuan Jiasan hold the post concurrently; he argued that Yunnan's Hui rebels should not be handled through appeasement alone; he laid out the capital's fundamental policies on grain price stabilization, procurement, relief, and reserves; and he exposed long-standing abuses in the coinage—many of his memorials were adopted. When British and French forces attacked Tianjin, Gengyun submitted seven individual memorials and two joint ones, urging all-out resistance; the emperor ordered the princes and grand ministers to deliberate. He clashed with Prince Zheng Duanhua and others, argued fiercely, wept, and the council broke up. Gengyun had once served in the Ministry of Rites, where Sushun had valued him highly—for which Sushun now came to hate him. In the ninth year the examination scandal broke; censors were punished for failing to investigate, but Gengyun alone bore the heavier penalty for having served as examiner for the eunuch examination—demoted two grades and transferred. In the tenth year, with the capital under martial law and the emperor preparing to flee to Rehe, Gengyun drafted a remonstrance for the defense commissioner and wrote personally to Sushun—the court would not listen. Vice Minister Wenxiang commanded the Nine Gates; he met Gengyun in the eastern city, and they wept in each other's arms while planning the defense of the capital. Hu Linyi memorialized that Gengyun possessed strategic talent and begged for his reappointment. Meanwhile Vice Censor-in-chief Mao Changxi was organizing Henan militia and requested that Gengyun join his staff. In Tongzhi 1 (1862) he led five thousand men with Sengge Rinchen to suppress the sect rebels at Jinlou Stockade, then with Regional Commander Zhang Yao captured the Nian base at Zhanggang; he was registered for circuit intendant and awarded the peacock feather. In the third year he served as acting intendant of the Henan-Shaanxi-Ru circuit. The western expedition bought grain at Shaanzhou; when the measure fell short, subordinate counties were forced to make up millions of jin; Gengyun had all such exactions cancelled. When allied troops broke discipline, he executed offenders as a warning to the rest. The region was rife with armed bandits; granted command over Henan and Shaanxi troops and paying them on time, he brought the soldiers to full obedience. In the fourth year Zhang Zongyu threatened the capital region; Gengyun marched with Governor Li Henian to Cizhou and proposed a long encirclement to cut off the rebels' retreat. He twice acted as intendant of grain and salt affairs, helped with postwar recovery, dredged the Huiji River and sealed breaches, and for his service received the brevet rank of provincial administration commissioner. In the thirteenth year he was confirmed in the Henan-Shaanxi-Ru intendant post. Corvée in Henan and Shaanxi was nearly as heavy as regular tax; Gengyun fixed the regulations and enforced strict audits, and the people's burden eased somewhat. In Guangxu 3 (1877), during a severe drought, he submitted seven famine-relief measures; before they could be carried out, he died in office. Gengyun was famed for blunt memorials; as a provincial official, governors Zhang Zhiwan and Li Henian relied on him heavily for military planning. After his death, Governor Li Qing'ao was impeached over famine relief and implicated in false claims on military pay that dragged in Gengyun's name; Gengyun was eventually cleared. Wang Zheng, originally named Xizhen and styled Dingfu, came from Maping in Guangxi. He passed the jinshi examination in Daoguang 21 (1841), was appointed a director in the Ministry of Revenue, and served as a Grand Council clerk. When Grand Secretary Saishanga took command in Guangxi, Zheng accompanied him; stirred by the nation's troubles, Zheng was eager to offer constructive counsel. During the Xianfeng era he rose from bureau director to vice minister of the Court of Judicial Review. In Tongzhi 2 (1863), the surrendered Nian leader Song Jingshi fled Shaanxi to raid Zhili and Shandong; Zheng memorialized that Jingshi's fortified stockade at Gangtun was a rebel stronghold, and that since his escape from Shaanxi his following numbered only a few hundred. Chonghou and others repeatedly indulged the threat until, through coercion, his force swelled past ten thousand. Recently he ordered villages in Changyi, Shen, Liaocheng, and Linqing to split the wheat harvest with tenants and send the grain to Gangtun—though nominally surrendered, his actions grew ever more defiant. I beg a secret order to Zhili governor Liu Changyou to lure him to camp, expose his crimes, and execute him. If he refuses, Zhili troops can still cross the border to attack him. Once Jingshi is eliminated, ringleaders such as Yang Pengling and Cheng Shunshu can be executed together, removing the chief threat and securing the capital region. The memorial was received but not acted upon. Jingshi was eventually executed for rebellion. While the war continued, Zeng Guofan proposed raising funds in Guangdong; Lao Chongguang introduced the lijin transit tax, and abuses multiplied. Zheng memorialized that the two Guang provinces were where the rebellion had begun, and that Cenxi and Rongxian had been held by rebels for years. Chen Jingang of Xinyi was the chief scourge; the rebel bands acted as one, spreading in ways difficult to suppress. Lao Chongguang ran the lijin by forcing gentry and merchants to contract and advance payments—a desperate expedient that could not endure. After Chongguang left office, collections fell sharply. Recently lijin commissioners had been beaten by mobs or pilloried by villagers; though the people were unruly, the conduct of officials was plainly abominable. In a land long torn by rebellion, with cornered rebels still at large, and every source of revenue squeezed by a hundred devices, I fear the profit will be trifling while the harm will be immense. If the two Guang provinces collapsed into chaos again, the court would not know which way to turn—and lijin would be the least of its worries. He recommended Guangdong circuit intendant Tang Qizhao, Liang-Huai grain transport commissioner Guo Songtao, and Zhejiang grain transport commissioner Cheng Sunyi. Guo Songtao was soon appointed to supervise Guangdong lijin on the strength of Zheng's memorial. In the third year he was made minister of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices and acting left vice censor-in-chief. He memorialized that Vice Ministers Chonglun, Hengqi, Dong Xun, and Xue Huan of the Foreign Affairs Yamen were petty and contemptible—known to all—and that foreign powers would despise the court if such men represented its senior ranks. Even if talent was scarce and the yamen needed staff, the throne should restrain such men, assign them nominal posts or empty titles, so foreign powers would respect the court's standards of rank. The empire would then understand the court's intent to embrace all subjects while keeping foreign powers on a tight rein. He was soon made transmission commissioner while continuing as acting left vice censor-in-chief. He memorialized that Suzhou and Hangzhou had recently fallen in succession and that Zhili and Shandong had been cleared. I observe that enterprises nearing success have never been completed without the utmost caution. Though Nanjing was expected to fall within three or four months, Danyang and Changzhou formed a defensive triangle, and hardened rebels such as Li Xiucheng massed for a last stand. With Hangzhou and Jiaxing taken, the remaining rebels converged on Huzhou. Rebels fleeing south from Anhui into Jiangxi spread over two or three hundred li through Yushan, Qianshan, Jinxi, and Jianchang—reportedly eighty or ninety thousand strong—and some even crossed into Fujian. Li Shixian was said to be leading a large force through Chun'an and Suian. Zeng Guofan and Zuo Zongtang, veteran commanders who had long urged patience, disagreed on the Anhui-Zhejiang border: Zeng wanted separate defenses for Huizhou and Ningguo, while Zuo preferred concentrating forces to seize Guangde and block the rebels' escape. Before the two plans could be settled, the rebels had already fled from Anhui into Jiangxi. Long accustomed to guerrilla warfare, the rebels were too numerous to annihilate in one blow while preventing any from escaping. I hold that the rebels were numerous and fierce, driving forward relentlessly—before one column was crushed, another followed close behind. If they penetrated deep into Jiangxi, the embers could blaze again across the land. From Jiangxi they could cross into Fujian and march straight to Tingzhou and Chaozhou—a well-worn rebel escape route for years. Huang Wenjin had come this way, Shi Dakai had gone this way—the precedent was a grave warning. Imperial orders repeatedly warned Zeng Guofan, Zuo Zongtang, Li Hongzhang, Shen Baozhen, and the Fujian and Guangdong governors to prepare. With final victory near, all must act as one, coordinate attack and defense, cut off fleeing bands fore and aft, and break them wherever they appear—daily weakening them until they cannot regroup, lest a great threat revive. I further urge that the Anhui and Zhejiang armies, long at war, depend on pay that Zeng and Zuo each raise from Jiangsu, Huguang, and other provinces. Zeng had established a central lijin office in Jiangsu, using the province's levies to sustain his hundred-thousand-man Anhui army. The Zhejiang army could not share its funds; even Zeng's monthly pay was reportedly issued only in part; forced to turn to Guangdong lijin, he could not raise it promptly. It is human nature that people cannot see far ahead and each pursues private interest. Guangdong behaves thus—would Jiangxi be any different? Shen Baozhen had recently asked that Jiangxi tea tax and broker lijin be collected locally; the ministries allowed half to remain in the province—Zeng and others would surely not object. But troops at the front, fighting in keen hope of victory, hearing that pay would shrink might lose heart—how could they fight on full stomachs and high spirits? I propose ordering the governors of Jiangxi, Anhui, Huguang, and Guangdong that, at this critical juncture, however difficult the arrangements, they must give their utmost and act in concert. Gansu's Hui rebellion was not yet suppressed, Nian remnants lingered in the central plains, Chen Daxi of Runan had fled into Hubei from Suizhou and Zaoyang toward Xiangyang and Fancheng; Zhang Zongyu had emerged from the Nan Tai Mountains into Henan and Zhejiang, at times feared to join them; Hanzhong rebels had fled through Ningxia, Shaanxi, and Shangzhou, reportedly to join forces at Xiangyang and Fancheng to relieve Nanjing—a threat not to be taken lightly. Shaanxi's military affairs were fragmented; Li Yunlin pursued rebels in Shangyu then suddenly marched west—in Xing'an he failed to stop rebel flight, in Hanyin he hid from the enemy and let his troops plunder—he should be restrained. Liu Rong was a scholar of unusual capacity charged with Hanzhong—yet rebels were spreading in every direction; he must vow to eliminate them or fail his charge. Duolong'a enjoyed the highest reputation—hailed as the foremost general—but his prestige had lately declined; imperial admonition was in order. Lei Zhengwan won wherever he fought and was fit for independent command, yet among Gansu officials not one or two upright men supported him. Lanzhou and Qingyang were now cut off; Enlin held the governor's seal in proxy—a man fit only for petty orders, of coarse judgment; Xilin held Qingyang and Ningxia but was misled by Qingyun's repeated blunders. I hold that an upright and capable senior minister should urgently be sent to pacify and reorganize the region. The empire is far from settled, yet as north and south gradually calm, if the west can be brought to order, the realm will approach general peace. The court is the root of the realm—when the palace is clear and strict, it resonates with the vigor of the frontiers and the two reinforce each other. The realm's situation turns daily, yet grave difficulties remain; some may hastily think the times clear and affairs smooth, as if order were already won. People delight in novelty yet cling to routine, fear hardship and seek quick gain—only the utterly sincere can see this deeply and warn against it. Zhuge Liang was the greatest man since the Three Dynasties, and history praises him above all for caution. Zhu Xi admonished Emperor Xiaozong of Song: 'Let the harm of ease and complacency grow day by day; the resolve to endure hardship for revenge will daily fade and be forgotten.' I cannot restrain my private anxiety and venture this frank statement. The memorial was received and acknowledged. He soon retired and died. Mu Jixianga, whose style name was Junan, was a Manchu of the Bordered Red Banner. He rose from director in the Ministry of Works to bureau director. In Tongzhi 4 (1865) he was made investigating censor for the Shandong circuit. He memorialized for careful selection of eunuchs: "The emperor ascended the throne as a child; his learning and understanding grow daily—those who attend him must be chosen with care, lest they later mislead his judgment." From the end of Han through the Ming, half the court's failures arose from eunuchs. At first eunuchs win favor through petty loyalty, quick compliance, and flattery—all to secure lasting patronage. Once their faction is formed, they grow arrogant and dominate their superiors—even an enlightened sovereign may find them impossible to remove. Officials and people gnashed their teeth in hatred, yet could do nothing. Our dynasty's successive emperors far surpassed former ages—not only barring eunuchs from government, but refusing even courteous words in daily encounters, leaving no opening for slander or flattery. Thus for more than two hundred years they had never been a scourge. Yet even with such strict precautions, there remained the fear that guard could not fully prevail. In the Jiaqing guiyou incident some eunuchs had communicated with rebels—clear proof of their treachery. The empress dowager now held the curtain audience, understood these abuses, guarded against gradual encroachment, learned from the past, and would not let petty men deceive the throne. She knew men and employed them well, and court governance was clear and strict. When the emperor assumes personal rule in a few years, he will surely not favor such men to the court's harm—why should officials fret excessively? Yet my earnest desire to offer counsel could not be stilled. At this time of expanding imperial learning, though officials can assist with full devotion, eunuchs especially merit careful selection. Eunuchs exist only to run errands and take orders—young and quick-witted men must never attend the emperor constantly. I beg the empress dowager to choose loyal and mature attendants for the emperor day and night, so that when he assumes personal rule he will not be deceived or misled—and his boundless virtue will rest on this foundation! In the fifth year he memorialized that Grand Secretary Zeng Guofan, commanding the Nian campaign, had achieved nothing after a long campaign and should be censured. The emperor noted that Zeng had repeatedly accepted blame and was ordered back to handle supplies exclusively; though he had not finished the campaign, he had not harmed military affairs—the memorial was rejected as excessive and set aside. He was sent out as prefect of Puzhou in Shanxi and soon died. Mu Jixianga was well versed in state affairs; his family collected court gazettes nearly complete from the dynasty's founding. You Baichuan, whose style name was Huidong, came from Binzhou in Shandong. He passed the jinshi examination in Tongzhi 1 (1862), was selected as a Hanlin bachelor, and appointed compiler. In the sixth year he was made censor and assigned to patrol the western city. Imperial clansmen such as Kuanhe acted unlawfully; he impeached and punished them, and for a time the privileged restrained themselves. In the seventh year Nian bandits fled from Shandong into Zhili; Baichuan urged the commanding general to suppress them swiftly and asked for a strict ban on opium cultivation in every province—the emperor adopted both proposals. He memorialized on longstanding abuses by clerks in government offices; the throne issued a universal order of strict prohibition. He stated further: "Eliminating clerk abuses requires strict official conduct above all, and raising the spirit of the scholar class." I beg that heads of ministries and courts select several able men in each bureau, entrust them with real authority, and fix their responsibility. If clerks are found corrupt, report the facts; judge officials by diligence or sloth. When rewards and punishments are clear, talent will strive of itself. Provincial officials already had power to punish clerks; governors should be ordered to choose men for the post, not assign posts to test men. Recommend the worthy and impeach the unworthy; select upright officials for periodic inspection; when corrupt officials or parasitic clerks are found, report them to the throne. The Yellow River shifted north, repeatedly flooding Shandong's prefectures and counties. Baichuan memorialized for relief; Grand Canal director Wenbin and Governor Ding Baozhen asked to restore the old Huai–Xu channel, and the court ordered deliberation. Baichuan memorialized that the Yellow River might flow south or north, but policy must settle on one course. If the old course were restored, engineering, cost, and using Yellow River water for the grain transport raised three unsettled questions: using the Daqing River as the main channel would require widening the riverbed—the old course could not hold it—and relocating countless farms and homes would need careful survey; even widened, the channel might not hold the flow; branch channels such as Tu Hai, Ma Jia, Gou Pan, and Ge Jin might be unavoidable—whether to open them and what harm might follow required careful weighing; Sending the Yellow River north would be unprecedented; mismanagement could stir popular unrest and public outcry—timing must be handled with utmost caution. I beg that a senior minister be sent to survey the entire river course before policy is fixed. In the twelfth year the emperor assumed personal rule, ordered repairs to the Old Summer Palace, and installed the empress dowager there. Censor Shen Huai asked to defer repairs; the emperor proclaimed his filial intent to honor both palaces, limiting work to the Anyou Palace for ancestral portraits, the empress dowager's residence, and offices of state—frugal and without extravagance, with no further construction. Baichuan remonstrated again; summoned and rebuked by the emperor, he spoke frankly without yielding; the emperor was moved, and his name for bold remonstrance resounded through the court. He soon retired for mourning; after the mourning period he resumed office and was promoted to supervising secretary. In Guangxu 5 (1879) he became intendant of the Heng-Yong-Chen-Gui circuit in Hunan, then Sichuan provincial judge, prefect of Shuntian, and vice minister of the Granary Circuit. In the ninth year the Shandong river breached, inundating dozens of prefectures and counties; Baichuan was sent to join Governor Chen Shijie in relief and repair work. Baichuan rode through Henan's both banks and the upper and lower reaches, distributing emergency relief first. They jointly memorialized for distant dikes on both banks and inner thread dikes within, to contain the Yellow River; and to dredge the Xiaoqing River to divert Yellow River water to the sea—the proposal was adopted. Back in the capital, he was dismissed after granary storehouses burned. After several years at home he died. (Supplement) Shen Huai, styled Dongchuan, came from Yin County in Zhejiang. He earned his juren degree in Daoguang 29 (1849), was appointed a Grand Secretariat drafter, and served as a Grand Council clerk. In Xianfeng 10 (1860), when Emperor Xianfeng fled to Rehe, Huai could not accompany him; he wept and tried to throw himself into a well, but his family prevented his death. He was promoted to director in the Ministry of Justice, then assistant director, and made investigating censor for the Shaanxi circuit. He impeached Revenue director Yang Hongdian for seizing power and taking bribes; the case went to the Ministry of Justice, but Yang was merely demoted for a minor fault—only when Yan Jingming became minister was he finally dismissed. When palace garden works began, Huai submitted the first remonstrance; at the time he was as famed as Baichuan. In Guangxu 1 (1875) he served as examiner for the Shuntian provincial examination, working through illness; he died soon after leaving the examination hall. His family had been of modest means; in office at the capital he sold nearly all he owned, and people especially admired his integrity. [Commentary] In wartime affairs change constantly; capital officials who memorialized on hearsay often missed the mark. Zong Jichen stressed recruiting talent; Yin Gengyun judged generals' failures; Wang Zheng urged coordinating frontier officials to focus on suppressing rebels—they saw the larger picture. Zheng spoke with especial detail, for he served on the Grand Council and saw military reports—unlike those who relied on rumor. Mu Jixianga urged careful selection of eunuchs; You Baichuan and others blocked repairs to the Old Summer Palace—their reputations for blunt remonstrance were well deserved.

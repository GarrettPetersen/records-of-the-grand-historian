向雄段灼閻纘
Xiang Xiong; Duan Zhuo; Yan Zuan.
2
向雄，字茂伯，河內山陽人也。 父韶，彭城太守。 雄初仕郡為主簿，事太守王經。 及經之死也，雄哭之盡哀，市人咸為之悲。 後太守劉毅嘗以非罪笞雄，及吳奮代毅為太守，又以少譴系雄於獄。 司隸鐘會於獄中辟雄為都官從事，會死無人殯斂，雄迎喪而葬之。 文帝召雄而責之曰：「往者王經之死，卿哭王經於東市，我不問也。 今鐘會躬為叛逆，又輒收葬，若復相容，其如王法何！ 」雄曰：「昔者先王掩骼埋胔，仁流朽骨，當時豈先卜其功罪而後葬之哉！ 今王誅既加，於法已備。 雄感義收葬，教亦無闕。 法立於上，教弘於下，何必使雄違生背死以立于時！ 殿下仇枯骨而捐之中野，為將來仁賢之資，不亦惜乎！ 」帝甚悅，與談宴而遣之。
Xiang Xiong, courtesy name Maobo, came from Shanyang in Henei commandery. His father Xiang Shao had governed Pengcheng. He began as the commandery chief clerk under Governor Wang Jing. When Wang Jing was executed, Xiang Xiong mourned him openly until every onlooker in the market wept. Later Governor Liu Yi had him flogged on a trumped-up charge; when Wu Fen succeeded Liu, he jailed Xiang Xiong again over a petty slight. Metropolitan Governor Zhong Hui pulled him from jail as capital clerk; after Zhong Hui died unmourned, Xiang Xiong claimed the body and gave him burial. Emperor Wen called him in: "You openly mourned Wang Jing at the eastern market—I let it pass. Zhong Hui was a traitor, yet you buried him too—keep indulging you and what becomes of the law?" Xiang Xiong replied: "Ancient kings gathered the fallen dead—mercy reached whitening bones—did they weigh guilt before digging graves? Imperial justice has already struck; the statute is satisfied. I buried him from duty; moral teaching loses nothing by it. Law rules from above; morals spread below—why force me to choose between the living and the dead? Would you leave bones for scavengers and teach posterity that decency is wasted effort?" Delighted, Sima Zhao feasted him and sent him off.
3
累遷黃門侍郎。 時吳奮、劉毅俱為侍中，同在門下，雄初不交言。 武帝聞之，敕雄令復君臣之好。 雄不得已，乃詣毅，再拜曰：「向被詔命，君臣義絕，如何？ 」於是即去。 帝聞而大怒，問雄曰：「我令卿復君臣之好，何以故絕？ 」雄曰：「古之君子進人以禮，退人以禮； 今之進人若加諸膝，退人若墜諸川。 劉河內於臣不為戎首，亦已幸甚，安復為君臣之好！ 」帝從之。
He rose through posts to Gentleman of the Yellow Gate. Wu Fen and Liu Yi both served as Palace Attendants alongside him—he refused to speak to either. Emperor Wu ordered him to mend fences with his old superiors. Cornered, he visited Liu Yi, bowed twice, and said, "The edict tells me to pretend we are friends—how can I?" He turned on his heel and left. The emperor thundered, "I told you to reconcile—why did you walk out?" Xiang Xiong said, "Ancient worthies advanced and dismissed men through ritual; today favorites ride on your lap; the dismissed land in the abyss. Liu Yi stopped short of assassinating me—that is mercy enough—how can we play at cordiality?" The emperor let it drop.
4
泰始中，累遷秦州刺史，假赤幢、曲蓋、鼓吹，賜錢二十萬。 咸甯初，入為御史中丞，遷侍中，又出為征虜將軍。 太康初，為河南尹，賜爵關內侯。 齊王攸將歸籓，雄諫曰：「陛下子弟雖多，然有名望者少。 齊王臥在京邑，所益實深，不可不思。 」帝不納。 雄固諫忤旨，起而徑出，遂以憤卒。
During Taishi he rose to Inspector of Qin with crimson banner, curved shade, military band, and two hundred thousand cash. Early in Xianning he became Palace Assistant Clerk, then Palace Attendant, then General Who Subdues Captives in the field. Early in Taikang he governed Henan as a passes marquis. When Prince Sima You prepared to leave for his princedom, Xiang Xiong warned, "Your sons are numerous but few enjoy real prestige. Keeping Prince You in the capital pays enormous dividends—you must think twice. The emperor refused. Xiang Xiong pressed until the emperor snapped; he stormed out and died of rage.

6
段灼，字休然，敦煌人也。 世為西土著姓，果直有才辯。 少仕州郡，稍遷鄧艾鎮西司馬，從艾破蜀有功，封關內侯，累遷議郎。 武帝即位，灼上疏追理艾曰：
Duan Zhuo, courtesy name Xiuran, hailed from Dunhuang. His clan was an old Dunhuang house—blunt, talented, and silver-tongued. He rose through local posts to Deng Ai's western army adjutant, earned a passes marquisate in the Shu campaign, and climbed to Consultant. When Emperor Wu took the throne, Duan Zhuo memorialized to clear Deng Ai.
7
故征西將軍鄧艾，心懷至忠，而荷反逆之名； 平定巴、蜀，而受三族之誅，臣竊悼之。 惜哉，言艾之反也！ 以艾性剛急，矜功伐善，而不能協同朋類，輕犯雅俗，失君子之心，故莫肯理之。 臣敢昧死言艾所以不反之狀。
General Deng Ai was utterly loyal yet branded a traitor; he conquered Ba-Shu yet his three clans were extirpated—I grieve for him. They called it rebellion—what a lie! Deng Ai was brusque and vain, rubbed peers wrong, offended polite society—so nobody defended him. I risk my neck to show why Deng Ai never meant to rebel.
8
艾本屯田掌犢人，宣皇帝拔之于農吏之中，顯之於宰府之職。 處內外之官，據文武之任，所在輒有名績，固足以明宣皇帝之知人矣。 會值洮西之役，官兵失利，刺史王經困於圍城之中。 當爾之時，二州危懼，隴右懍懍，幾非國家之有也。 先帝以為深憂重慮，思惟可以安邊殺敵莫賢于艾，故授之以兵馬，解狄道之圍。 圍解，留屯上邽。 承官軍大敗之後，士卒破膽，將吏無氣，倉庫空虛，器械殫盡。 艾欲積穀強兵，以待有事。 是歲少雨，又為區種之法，手執耒耜，率先將士，所統萬數，而身不離僕虜之勞，親執士卒之役。 故落門、段谷之戰，能以少擊多，摧破強賊，斬首萬計。 遂委艾以廟勝成圖，指授長策。 艾受命忘身，龍驤麟振，前無堅敵。 蜀地阻險，山高穀深，而艾步乘不滿二萬，束馬懸車，自投死地，勇氣陵雲，將士乘勢，故能使劉禪震怖，君臣面縛。 軍不逾時，而巴、蜀蕩定，此艾固足以彰先帝之善任矣。
Deng Ai began as a pasture clerk; Emperor Xuan pulled him from the fields into high office. In civil and military posts he scored triumph after triumph—proof that Emperor Xuan could judge talent. When the Tao river campaign collapsed, Inspector Wang Jing was trapped in siege. Both provinces panicked; Longyou shook—the northwest nearly slipped away. The late emperor knew only Deng Ai could save the frontier—he gave him troops and lifted the siege at Didao. After the relief he camped at Shanggui. After that rout morale shattered, arsenals emptied, stores vanished. Deng Ai meant to rebuild grain and troops against whatever came next. Rain failed that year, so he tilled in grid-plots, hoe in hand, outworking his men—ten thousand under his command, yet he sweated like a conscript. Hence at Luomen and Dangu he routed superior forces and piled tens of thousands of heads. The court handed him the blueprint for final victory. He obeyed orders without sparing himself—charging like dragon or kirin—nothing stood firm before him. Shu was sheer cliffs and deep defiles; Deng Ai led fewer than twenty thousand foot and horse, hoisted carts up cliffs, marched into death ground—courage pierced the clouds—until Liu Shan surrendered himself. In mere months Ba-Shu fell—a testament to the late emperor's trust.
9
艾功名已成，亦當書之竹帛，傳祚萬世。 七十老公，復何所求哉！ 艾以禪初降，遠郡未附，矯令承制，權安社稷。 雖違常科，有合古義，原心定罪，事可詳論。 故鎮西將軍鐘會，有吞天下之心，恐艾威名，知必不同，因其疑似，構成其事。 艾被詔書，即遣強兵，束身就縛，不敢顧望。 誠自知奉見先帝，必無當死之理也。 會受誅之後，艾參佐官屬、部曲將吏，愚戇相聚，自共追艾，破壞檻車，解其囚執。 艾在困地，是以狼狽失據。 夫反非小事，若懷噁心，即當謀及豪傑，然後乃能興動大眾，不聞艾有腹心一人。 臨死口無惡言，獨受腹背之誅，豈不哀哉！ 故見之者垂涕，聞之者歎息。 此賈誼所以慷慨于漢文，天下之事可為痛哭者，良有以也。
His fame deserved bamboo slips and silk—for ages to come. A man of seventy—what more could he want? Liu Shan had just yielded and outlying districts wavered—Deng Ai issued emergency orders to steady the state. He bent regulations on purpose—yet matched classical precedent—judge his intent, not just the letter. Zhong Hui meant to seize the realm; he feared Deng Ai's prestige and framed him on suspicion. When the summons arrived Deng Ai sent escort troops and submitted to bonds—never hesitating. He trusted that facing the late emperor he could clear himself. After Zhong Hui died, Deng Ai's staff mobbed together, smashed his cage, and freed him. Cornered and desperate, he stumbled. Rebellion takes planning—yet Deng Ai had no inner circle. He died without a bitter word—executed front and back—can anyone not weep? Witnesses wept; listeners groaned. This is Jia Yi's grief for Emperor Wen—cause for the realm to wail.
10
陛下龍興，闡弘大度，受誅之家，不拘敘用，聽艾立後，祭祀不絕。 昔秦人憐白起之無罪，吳人傷子胥之冤酷，皆為之立祠。 天下之人為艾悼心痛恨，亦由是也。 謂可聽艾門生故吏收艾屍柩，歸葬舊墓，還其田宅，以平蜀之功，繼封其後，使艾闔棺定諡，死無所恨。 赦冤魂于黃泉，收信義於後世，則天下徇名之士，思立功之臣，必投湯火，樂為陛下死矣！
Your dragon ascent brought mercy—even struck families find posts; Deng Ai's heir may yet inherit offerings. Qin mourned Bai Qi; Wu mourned Wu Zixiu—the world raised shrines. The empire aches for Deng Ai for the same reasons. Let his old aides recover his body, bury him at home, restore his lands, enfeoff his heirs for the Shu conquest—give him a verdict and peace in the grave. Right the ghost in Hades, teach posterity good faith, and every ambitious hero will leap through fire for you!

臣聞天時不如地利，地利不如人和。 三里之城，五里之郭，圜圍而攻之，有不克者，此天時不如地利。 城非不高，池非不深，穀非不多，兵非不利，委而去之，此地利不如人和。 然古之王者，非不先推恩德，結固人心。 人心苟和，雖三里之城，五里之郭，不可攻也。 人心不和，雖金城湯池，不能守也。 臣推此以廣其義，舜彈五弦之琴，詠《南風》之詩，而天下自理，由堯人可比屋而封也。 曩者多難，奸雄屢起，攪亂眾心，刀鋸相乘，流死之孤，哀聲未絕。 故臣以為陛下當深思遠念，杜漸防萌，彈琴詠詩，垂拱而已。 其要莫若推恩以協和黎庶，故推恩足以保四海，不推恩不足以保妻子。 是故唐堯以親睦九族為先，周文以刑于寡妻為急，明王聖主莫不先親後疏，自近及遠。 臣以為太宰、司徒、衛將軍三王宜留洛中鎮守，其餘諸王自州征足任者，年十五以上悉遣之國。 為選中郎傅相，才兼文武，以輔佐之。 聽于其國繕修兵馬，廣布恩信。 必撫下猶子，愛國如家，君臣分定，百世不遷，連城開地，為晉、魯、衛。 所謂磐石之宗，天下服其強矣。 雖云割地，譬猶囊漏貯中，亦一家之有耳。 若慮後世強大，自可豫為制度，使得推恩以分子弟。 如此則枝分葉布，稍自削小，漸使轉至萬國，亦後世之利，非所患也。
Heaven's timing, earth's terrain, and human unity—Mencius ranked them just so. Surround a tiny fortress yet fail—timing loses to terrain. Strong walls and deep moats fall when the people turn away. Ancient kings won hearts before ramparts. United hearts make even a hamlet untakable. Divided hearts doom metal walls and boiling moats. Shun sang "South Wind" and order followed—Yao's subjects were fit for neighborly rule. Years of warlords shredded loyalty; blade on blade left orphans still keening. Think deep, nip plots early—rule like Shun with lyre and ode alone. Nothing matters like kindness to the people—kindness secures the realm; its absence imperils your family. Yao harmonized nine kin; Wen instructed his consort first—every sage honored family before strangers. Keep the Grand Mentor, Minister of Education, and Guards general in Luoyang; send every other prince older than fifteen to his fief. Give each prince tutors both civil and martial. Let them drill troops and earn loyalty at home. They will treat subjects as nephews and realms as households—fixed roles for generations—strong as Jin, Lu, or Wei. That is how you forge bedrock kin and awe the realm. Splitting domains only moves revenue within the imperial purse. Fear strong cadet lines? Lay laws now for junior splits. Branches thin over time until myriad manageable states—posterity gains, not grief.
13
昔在漢世，諸呂自疑，內有硃虛、東牟之親，外有諸侯九國之強，故不敢動搖。 於今之宜，諸侯強大，是為太山之固。 非我族類，其心必異。 而魏法禁錮諸王，親戚隔絕，不祥莫大焉。 間者無故又瓜分天下，立五等諸侯。 上不象賢，下不議功，而是非雜糅，例受茅土。 似權時之宜，非經久之制，將遂不改，此亦煩擾之人，漸亂之階也。 夫國之興也，由於九族親睦，黎庶協和； 其衰也，在於骨肉疏絕，百姓離心。 故夏邦不安，伊尹歸殷； 殷邦不和，呂氏入周。 殷監在於夏後，去事之誡，誠來事之鑒也。
Han's Lü clan hesitated because Liu kin and outer kings boxed them in. Today strong princes are your Mount Tai bulwark. "Alien hearts breed alien plots"—the Spring and Autumn saw it. Wei caged princes and severed kin—no worse omen. Lately you carved the realm again into five noble ranks. Those at court did not look to exemplars for how to behave, nor did merit matter in discussion at lower levels—but right and wrong were jumbled anyway, while everyone routinely took their share of enfeoffment. It looks like stopgap pragmatism, not enduring institutions. If nothing is ever corrected, restless officials will seize on every inconsistency, and disorder will creep in by degrees. States flourish when imperial kin knit close ties and ordinary people pull together. States rot when flesh-and-blood kin grow cold and estranged and the populace loses heart altogether. So when Xia could not govern its own realm, Yi Yin withdrew to Shang territory. When Shang fractured, ministers of the Lü line crossed over into Zhou. Shang drew its moral from Xia's aftermath: what has already ended is genuinely the precedent for what comes next.

15
昔伐蜀，募取涼州兵馬、羌胡健兒，許以重報，五千餘人，隨艾討賊，功皆第一。 而《乙亥詔書》，州郡將督，不與中外軍同，雖在上功，無應封者。 唯金城太守楊欣所領兵，以逼江由之勢，得封者三十人。 自金城以西，非在欣部，無一人封者。 苟在中軍之例，雖下功必侯； 如在州郡，雖功高不封，非所謂近不重施，遠不遺恩之謂也。
When Shu was overrun, recruits from Liangzhou—Qiang horsemen, Hu recruits—were summoned with extravagant promises of pay. More than five thousand accompanied Deng Ai, and each company earned top marks. But the decree of Yihai set provincial generals apart from armies under the metropolitan command—even first-class feats did not merit a feudal patent. Only the detachment of Jincheng governor Yang Xin, which pressed the Jiangyou corridor, saw thirty men actually ennobled. West of Jincheng, outside Xin's command, nobody was given a title at all. Had the same men been tallied as central-army troops, even middling performance would have won them a marquisate. Caught in the provincial tally, lofty deeds went unrewarded—which contradicts the principle that insiders should not be spared honors twice while outsiders go empty-handed.
16
臣聞魚懸由於甘餌，勇夫死於重報。 故荊軻慕燕丹之義，專諸感闔閭之愛，匕首振于秦庭，吳刀耀於魚腹，視死如歸，豈不有由也哉！ 夫功名重賞，士之所競，不平致怨，由來久矣。 《詩》云：「屍鳩在桑，其子七兮。 淑人君子，其儀一兮。 」臣以為此等宜蒙爵封。
Sweet bait hangs the fish; big pay draws brave men willing to gamble their necks. So Jing Ke answered Prince Dan's call, Zhuan Zhu repaid King Helü's esteem—one waved a dagger in the Qin court, the other drew a blade from a salted fish—and both met death as calmly as going home. Such deeds always have their reasons. Glory and gold are what officers chase; jealousy over uneven reward is as old as government itself. The Book of Songs reads: 'The turtle-dove rests in the mulberry; it nurtures seven chicks beneath its wings.' The virtuous man's bearing is steadfast and even. Your servant submits that men of this stature deserve feudal patents as well.'

18
臣受恩三世，剖符守境，試用無績，沈伏數年，犬馬之力，無所復堪。 陛下弘廣納之聽，采狂夫之言，原臣侵官之罪，不問幹忤之愆，天地恩厚，於臣足矣。 臣聞忠臣之于其君，猶孝子之於其親：進則有欣然之慶，非貪官也； 退則有戚然之憂，非懷祿也。 其意在於不忘光君榮親，情所不能已已者也。 臣伏自悼，私懷至恨：生長荒裔，而久在外任，自還抱疾，未嘗覲見，陛下竟不知臣何人，此臣之恨一也。 遭運會之世，值有事之時，而不能垂功名於竹帛，此臣之恨二也。 逮事聖明之君，而尪悴羸劣，陳力又不能，當歸死於地下，此臣之恨三也。 哀二親早亡隕，兄弟並凋喪，孝敬無復施於家門，此臣之恨四也。 夏之日忽以過，冬之夜尋復來，人生百歲，尚以為不足，而臣中年嬰災，此臣之恨五也。 慚日月之所養，愧昊蒼而無報，此臣之所以懷五恨而歎息，臨歸路而自悼者也。
I have borne three sovereigns' favor, wielded tally on the marches yet produced no deeds, sunk in office for seasons without rise—whatever strength a petty retainer retains is spent. Your Majesty listens widely and weighs even a crank's counsel—forgiving my overstep of jurisdiction and never punishing my blunt defiance. Heaven and earth could heap no greater kindness on me. A faithful minister resembles a dutiful child: advancing to office sparks honest joy—not greed for place. Leaving office brings mourning faces—not clinginess to paychecks. The point is only to brighten ruler and forbears—to stop would betray every feeling altogether. I mourn inwardly: raised beyond the pale yet always sent to distant posts—recalled only to fall ill, never summoned to court—so Your Majesty still does not know who I am. That is my first regret. I lived stirring times yet never carved deeds into the historians' slips—that is sorrow two. I serve a luminous throne while my body wastes—wish to exert myself yet cannot—weighs as if buried already—that is sorrow three. Father and mother left early and brothers vanished too—nowhere remains to practise filial devotion—that is sorrow four. Summer afternoons vanish overnight; winters return swiftly; a century still feels brief—yet midway I am blasted by affliction—that is sorrow five. I blush under sun and moon, shame myself before Heaven with nothing to repay—five resentments haunt me sighing toward the journey home.

First: deeds of good lie spelled out in the canon. Wrongs live under the lash of written law. From farthest antiquity through Qin and Han you can tally bright rulers, dark losers, conquerors gone under. You can also name steadfast ministers beside conniving villains. Courts stocked with quarrelsome straight men always flourished. Courts manned by yes-men vanished without exception. Every sovereign wants loyalty to prop him up and genius to sharpen him. Yet ruin follows ruin because the wrong faces fill the mats. They call allies worthy who are worthless, loyal who are thieves. I recount how predecessors rose on talent and sank on villains. Late Yao let four beasts squat in council while ignoring the Eight Paragons, yet harmony held—credit belongs to Shun as minister. Jie of Xia ended in defeat at Mingtiao; Zhou of Shang hung as a trophy at Muye—each commanded ten thousand chariots yet lost throne and life for heeding women rather than ministers, wallowing in debauch: mounds of mash, pools of wine, oxen drinking like men, groves of roasting meat—until loyal Guan Longpang died for speaking truth and blunt Bi Gan lost his heart—all under Heaven turned their hatred toward them. Tai Jia trampled Tang's ordinances, so Yi Yin confined him in the Tong Palace until he repented; three years later he restored him to Bo, the royal seat. Once restored, the Shang line rallied from decline and every lord submitted; he earned the temple epithet Taizong—thanks wholly to Yi Yin's single-minded devotion. When Zhou sank, feudatories warred together, kings grew feeble and policy slid downhill. Duke Huan of Qi lived as a voluptuary. Yet he knit nine alliances and forced order on the empire—chiefly because of Guan Zhong. Dead, his corpse maggotted into the hallway—surely Shu Diao's fault. The same man's body—with Guan Zhong he soared. With Shu Diao he crashed. Glory or ruin hinges on ministers chosen—inspect that choice fiercely. Qin traced back to Boyi—once a petty stockade—until Zhong enlarged it toward chariotry and court music. From Duke Mu onward every ruler hunted talent—You Yu from the west, five rams swapped in Nanyang, Pi Leopard torn from Jin, Jian Shu coaxed down from Zong village. As a result, brave and capable men flocked in from every quarter, so that Qin could remain a great power for generations: it devoured the regional states, held the whole realm, and took the imperial title as well—and all of that rested on the strength of its strategists. Moral transformation had not yet settled when the First Emperor died at Shaqiu. Ershi rode his father's violence, schemed his way to the throne and trapped himself, failed to shore up the legitimate line of rule or finish the edifice his father had raised, and instead trampled human decency until its venom seeped through the common people. So Chen Sheng and Wu Guang raised their fists and shouted to the world, and the empire answered from every side. Then Zhao Gao turned traitor, Yan Le did his bidding, the Second Emperor was cornered, and cut his own throat at Wangyi. When Ziying finally took charge he demoted himself from emperor to mere king—too isolated and too late—and within forty days the house was gone. Vicious favourites seized the government, called a deer a horse, and in doing so they hurried Qin to its ruin. When Qin dropped the prize, every warlord lunged for it. Xiang Yu seized the advantage and threw it away—blame the execution of his honest adviser Han Sheng and his refusal to heed Fan Zeng. Had he brushed aside Xiang Bo's bad advice, killed Liu Bang at the Hongmen banquet, and ruled from Xianyang with a finger on every rival, no one in the realm could have stood against him. Instead he spurned Han Sheng's blunt counsel, ignored Fan Zeng's designs, decided the hegemony was already his, lounged back in Pengcheng and his native haunt, flaunting brocaded silks like a suburban parvenu—and he preened over it. Five years later Han had him bound, and even then his only excuse was ‘Heaven has doomed me—not my generalship!’—there is bitterness in hearing it. Besides, gentlemen flock to humane rule as water runs downhill or game seeks open ground. The classic line runs: ‘Otters harry fish into the deep pools; goshawks harry sparrows into the brush; tyrants like Jie and Zhou chase the people straight into the arms of founders like Tang and Wu.’ Emperor Gaozu of Han began as a commoner, took up a short sword, and won the realm with the wealth of the six former kingdoms—there was no gracious handover like the legends of Yao and Shun. His victory was not only Zhang Liang's and Chen Ping's cunning or the muscle of every adventurer; the Xiang clan itself herded the population into Liu Bang's camp. His line held the mandate for over two centuries until Emperor Cheng handed the real work to his mother's kin and watched real authority drift beyond the palace. Zhang Yu of Anchang sat among the three highest ministers and had nursed Emperor Cheng as tutor; the sovereign made personal calls on his estate, kowtowed beneath the old man's daybed, and pressed him for guidance on portents and policy. A man in his station should have spoken for the dynasty, weighed the fate of the altars in good faith, and laid the dangers plain; if he had, the Wangs could never have hoarded every lever, Wang Mang would never have climbed the dragon's mist into power, and the Han mandate might not have broken halfway. Instead Zhang Yu flattered his way through the Wang circle, trimming his sails between the Five Marquises and angling only for smiles from the throne. So Zhu Yun defied protocol to demand the palace armory's horse-beheading blade, ready to strike Zhang Yu's head off as an example—it was fidelity of the courageous sort. Even then Emperor Cheng would not wake up: Zhu Yun was somehow a criminal for shaming tutor and emperor alike, deserved execution without appeal, so the clerks hurried him toward the boiling cauldron. Zhu Yun had to clutch the hall balustrade and snap the marble rail before General of the Left Xin Qingji could batter his brow bloody and buy his life with his own safety. Without that intervention he would have been torn apart on the spot. Later they left the broken rail unrepaired to advertise a straight speaker—fine theatre for the chronicles, but it did nothing to cure the illness that would kill Han. Later writers treat Wang Mang as the worst traitor imaginable, much as moralists pretend King Zhou's evil was unmatched—both judgments overstate the case. Records say Wang Mang began as an in-law who cosplayed austerity, curried a spotless name until kinsmen praised his filial piety and friends swore to his kindness. By the Cheng and Ai reigns he looked busy on the state's behalf, and every gesture drew applause from the chroniclers. Memorials praising him stacked up at the palace gate faster than anyone could count, and every courtier, inside or out, sang of his genius and virtue. Han was already hollow, the succession had failed three times over, and the aged Grand Empress Dowager remained the ritual figurehead—so Mang could stage the script for the boy ruler and walk off with the talisman. Even Tang and Wu had seized power by force before they secured it through just rule. Suppose Wang Mang had studied how early dynasties won and kept the throne: he might have cultivated genuine benevolence, kept his word, shed the theatrical virtue, and spent eighteen years actually feeding the realm until the people cherished him and bold men rallied. The altars might still stand; even a genius like Guangwu would have had no opening. Once enthroned he decided Heaven and humanity were his sponsors, bragged that he outshone the Three Kings and rivalled Yao and Shun, then turned swaggering and cruel, peddled forged omens, and ruled by terror until men and gods alike cursed him—winter lightning slapped his ears, summer tremors nagged at his gut. He still would not learn: he doubled down on senseless edicts, chained neighbours into collective guilt, promoted flatterers, and slaughtered anyone who told the truth. The realm boiled over, revolt flared inside and out, and when the frontiers collapsed he died at a commoner's knife—an empire's joke. How could it be otherwise? The failure was not that he had stolen the throne but that he guarded it without rectitude. When Wang Mang's butcheries had left the populace raw, turmoil rolled through every quarter: Liu Xuan styled himself emperor but never imposed order; Liu Penzi inherited the sham and collapsed; then Gongsun Shu declared another court in the southwest. The realm belongs to everyone who dwells under Heaven, not to a single occupant of the throne. 臣素寒門，無力仕宦，不經東宮，情不私遹。 念昔楚國處女諫其王曰「有龍無尾」，言年四十，未有太子。 臣嘗備近職，雖未得自結天日，情同閽寺，悾悾之誠，皆為國計。 臣老母見臣為表，乃為臣卜卦，云「書禦即死」。 妻子守臣，涕泣見止。 臣獨以為頻見拔擢，嘗為近職，此恩難忘，何以報德？ 唯當陳誠，以死獻忠。 輒具棺絮，伏須刑誅。
I am a nobody from a cold gate with no pull at court; I never served the Eastern Palace. I do not plead for Yu out of private fondness. I remember the Chu girl who warned her king he was 'a dragon without a tail'—past forty with no heir. I once held a post close to the throne; though I never won a private audience, my heart is as exposed as a gatekeeper's—every word is for the dynastic plan. When my mother saw this draft she cast the yarrow for me: 'If the memorial reaches the throne, you die.' My wife and children clung to me, weeping, begging me to stop. Yet I have been raised from nothing and once stood near the radiance of the throne; that grace is not forgotten. How else could I repay it? I can only speak my mind and offer loyalty unto death. I have coffin and shroud ready and await the executioner's stroke.
36
書禦不省。
The memorial reached the throne and went unread.
37
及張華遇害，賈謐被誅，朝野震悚，纘獨撫華屍慟哭曰：「早語君遜位而不肯，今果不免，命也夫！ 」過叱賈謐屍曰：「小兒亂國之由，誅其晚矣！」
When Zhang Hua was killed and Jia Mi cut down, the capital shook; Zuan alone cradled Zhang Hua's body and wept, 'I told you to yield your post and you refused—now see what fate costs.' Passing Jia Mi's corpse he spat, 'You child—this is why the realm burned. They should have killed you sooner.'
38
皇太孫立，纘復上疏曰：
When the imperial great-grandson was named heir, Zuan filed another memorial:
39
臣前上書訟太子之枉，不見省覽。 昔壺關三老陳衛太子之冤，而漢武築思子之台。 高廟令田千秋上書，不敢正言，托以鬼神之教，而孝武大感，月中三遷，位至丞相，乘車入殿，號曰車氏。 恨臣精誠微薄，不能有感，竟使太子流離，沒命許昌。 向令陛下即納臣言，不致此禍。 天贊聖意，三公獻謀，庶人賜死，罪人斯得，太子以明，臣恨其晚，無所復及。 詔書慈悼，迎喪反葬，復其禮秩，誠副眾望，不意呂、霍之變復生於今日！ 伏見詔書建立太孫，斯誠陛下上順先典以安社稷，中慰慈悼冤魂之痛，下令萬國心有所系。 追惟庶人，所為無狀，幾傾宗廟，賴相國、太宰至忠憤發，潛謀俱斷，奉贊聖意，以成神武。 雖周誅二叔，漢掃諸呂，未足以喻。 臣願陛下因此大更厘改，以為永制。 禮置太子，居以士禮，與國人齒，為置官屬，皆如朋友，不為純臣。 既使上厭至望，以崇孝道，又令不相嚴憚，易相規正。
Your servant once petitioned for the crown prince's innocence; it was never read. The elder of Huguan pleaded for Prince Ju, and Emperor Wu answered by building the Terrace for the Longing Son. Tian Qianqiu, caretaker of Gaozu's shrine, dared not speak plainly and framed his plea as a ghost's warning—yet Emperor Wu was so moved that within a month he promoted him three times to chancellor, let him ride a carriage into the hall, and nicknamed his line the House of the Cart. I blame my own thin eloquence: I could not move you, and the heir wandered to his death in Xuchang. Had you taken my words when I first offered them, this disaster would never have come. Heaven shifted your heart, the Three Dukes plotted, the culprit was ordered to die, the crime was proved, and the crown prince's name was cleared—I mourn only that justice came too late to save him. Your merciful edict welcomed his bier home, restored his rites, and matched what the people wanted—who imagined we would see another Lü or Huo drama in our own day? I see you have proclaimed the great-grandson heir: above, you heed the old pattern and steady the temples; within, you comfort wronged ghosts; below, you give the realm something to cling to. Remember the stripped conspirators whose crimes nearly overturned the altars—it took the chief minister's and grand tutor's fierce loyalty and their secret strikes, all in accord with your will, to finish the coup as cleanly as a god of war. Even the Zhou executions of Guan and Cai and Han's massacre of the Lü clan fade beside this righteousness. I beg Your Majesty to ride this tide into sweeping reform—write it as permanent statute. By classical rites the heir should live as a knight among the people; his staff should be friends, not mere vassals. That satisfies the court's loftiest hopes and crowns filial duty, yet keeps tutor and pupil from freezing in fear so they can correct one another openly.
40
昔漢武既信奸讒，危害太子，復用望氣之言，欲盡誅詔獄中囚。 邴吉以皇孫在焉，閉門距命，後遂擁護皇孫，督罰乳母，卒至成人，立為孝宣皇帝。 苟志於忠，無往不可。 曆觀古人雖不避死，亦由世教寬以成節。 吉雖距詔書，事在於忠，故宥而不責。 自晉興已來，用法太嚴，遲速之間，輒加誅斬。 一身伏法，猶可強為，今世之誅，動輒滅門。 昔呂後臨朝，肆意無道。 周昌相趙，三召其王而昌不遣，先征昌入，乃後召王。 此由漢制本寬，得使為快。 假令如今，呂後必謂昌已反，夷其三族，則誰敢復為殺身成義者哉！ 此法宜改，可使經遠。 又漢初廢趙王張敖，其臣貫高謀弑高祖，高祖不誅，以明臣道。 田叔、孟舒十人為奴，髡鉗隨王，隱親侍養，故令平安。 向使晉法得容為義，東宮之臣得如周昌，固護太子得如邴吉，距詔不坐，伏死諫爭，則聖意必變，太子以安。 如田叔、孟舒侍從不罪者，則隱親左右，奸凶毒藥無緣得設，太子不夭也。
Emperor Wu of Han listened to poison and ruined his heir, then listened to geomancy and meant to butcher every prisoner named in an edict. Bing Ji barred the gate because the imperial grandson was inside, then shielded the boy, disciplined the wet-nurse, and raised him to the throne as Emperor Xuan. Where loyalty is the aim, no road is barred. The ancients did not shun death, but their age still forgave enough of it to let integrity stand. Bing Ji defied an edict for loyalty's sake—so he was spared and never charged. Since Jin arose the code has swung toward cruelty: hesitate a moment or move too fast and the headsman appears. A man may force himself through a solo execution, but nowadays a sentence means an exterminated lineage. Empress Lü held court and indulged every cruelty. Zhou Chang governed Zhao for the throne: three summonses for the king, and Zhou Chang refused each time until the court called Zhou Chang in first—only then could they summon the king. Han law was loose enough to allow that boldness. Under our code Empress Lü would call it revolt, flatten three clans, and no one would ever die for principle again. Change that law if you want the dynasty to last. Early Han deposed King Zhang Ao of Zhao when his retainer Guan Gao tried to murder Gaozu; Gaozu spared the king to teach what true vassalage means. Tian Shu, Meng Shu, and eight others shaved their heads, wore the neck-iron, and followed their lord as slaves to hide and serve him—so he lived. Had our statutes blessed such loyalty—had Eastern Palace aides been another Zhou Chang, guardians another Bing Ji, free to defy forged orders and die remonstrating—the throne would have turned and the heir would stand today. If men like Tian Shu and Meng Shu could attend a disgraced prince without guilt, trusted hands would stay at his side and poisoners would find no seam—no early death for the heir.
41
臣每責東宮臣故無侍從者，後聞頗有于道路望車拜辭，而有司收付洛陽獄，奏科其罪。 然臣故莫從，良有以也。 又本置三率，盛其兵馬，所以宿衛防虞。 而使者卒至，莫有警嚴覆請審者，此由恐畏滅族。 今皇孫沖幼，去事多故。 若有不虞，強臣專制，奸邪矯詐，雖有相國保訓東宮，擁佑之恩同于邴吉，適可使玉體安全，宜開來防，可著於令：自今已後，諸有廢興倉卒，群臣皆得輒嚴，須錄詣殿前，面受口詔，然後為信，得同周昌不遣王節，下聽臣子隱親，得如田叔、孟舒，不加罪責，則永固儲副，以後安嗣之遠慮也。 來事難知，往事可改。 臣前每見詹事裴權用心懇惻，舍人秦戢數上疏啟諫； 而爰倩贈以九列，權有忠意，獨不蒙賞。 謂宜依倩為比，以寵其魂。 推尋表疏，如秦戢輩及司隸所奏，諸敢拜辭于道路者，明詔稱揚，使微異於眾，以勸為善，以獎將來也。
I condemned the Eastern Palace staff for leaving no companions—then learned many bowed farewell along the roadside and were shackled into Luoyang jail on memorialized charges. Small wonder none of his old friends dared walk with him. You stationed the three Household guards and swollen their horses and spears precisely to ward the palace overnight. Yet when couriers crashed the gates nobody armed the watch or demanded proof—terror of clan extinction did that. The imperial grandson is a child in a season of fresh disasters. If crisis comes—a strongman's junta, forged edicts—even a Bing Ji at the Eastern Palace saves only flesh. Open permanent safeguards: decree that sudden depositions force every minister to lock the precincts until registers reach the stair and edicts are heard mouth-to-ear; match Zhou Chang's refusal to surrender the tally; forgive hidden service like Tian Shu and Meng Shu. Then the heir survives and the lineage endures beyond our sight. Tomorrow is unknowable—yesterday can still be mended. I have often watched Crown Prince Household Superintendent Pei Quan serve with wrenching earnestness and Gentleman Qin Ji memorial after memorial beseech the throne. Yet Yuan Qian won posthumous honours among the Nine Ministers while Pei Quan, though loyal as any of them, received nothing. Treat Pei Quan by the same measure granted Yuan Qian and let his ghost know favour. Survey the petitions—Qin Ji's and the Metropolitan Shepherd's—praising men who dared surrender their seals in the highroad dust; your luminous edict singled them out so virtue would spread and posterity take heart.
42
纘又陳：
Yan Zuan went on:
43
今相國雖已保傅東宮，保其安危。 至於旦夕訓誨，輔導出入，動靜劬勞，宜選寒苦之士，忠貞清正，老而不衰，如城門校尉梁柳、白衣南安硃沖比者，以為師傅。 其侍臣以下文武將吏，且勿復取盛戚豪門子弟，若吳太妃家室及賈、郭之黨。 如此之輩，生而富溢，無念修己，率多輕薄浮華，相驅放縱，皆非所補益於吾少主者也。 皆可擇寒門篤行、學問素士、更履險易、節義足稱者，以備群臣，可輕其禮儀，使與古同，於相切磋為益。
The Chancellor of State is already grand and junior tutor—he guards the heir's life and death. For the daily coaching, the comings and goings, the bone-wearying round of duty, pick men of chilly gates and proven honor—men like Gates Colonel Liang Liu or the hemp-robed recluse Zhu Chong of Nan'an—and set them over him as tutors. Do not staff his attendants and guards from the riot of princely in-laws—no more of Consort Wu's clan or the Jia and Guo packs. Men soaked in gold from the cradle never trouble to temper themselves; they glide through life on perfume and dare—none of them strengthens a boy who must wear the crown. Fill the household instead with poor gate scholars of tested fiber, men who have ridden fortune's switchbacks and earned a name for constancy; relax the court drill so they may speak as elders did and sharpen one another like whetstones.
44
昔魏文帝之在東宮，徐幹、劉楨為友，文學相接之道並如氣類。 吳太子登，顧譚為友，諸葛恪為賓，臥同床帳，行則參乘，交如布衣，相呼以字，此則近代之明比也。 天子之子不患不富貴，不患人不敬畏，患於驕盈，不聞其過，不知稼穡之艱難耳。 至於甚者，乃不知名六畜，可不勉哉！ 昔周公親撻伯禽，曹參笞窋二百，聖考慈父皆不傷恩。 今不忍小相維持，令至闕失頓相罪責，不亦誤哉！
When Emperor Wen of Wei was heir, Xu Gan and Liu Zhen were his companions; their brush and book traffic breathed the same air as his own. Crown Prince Deng of Wu kept Gu Tan as friend and Zhuge Ke as mentor; they shared a couch, rode the second chariot at his side, called one another by courtesy name like village neighbors—a mirror for our own age. A Son of Heaven's boy need not fear poverty or want of awe; he must dread arrogance, deafness to blame, and ignorance of what the plough costs. Worst of all are heirs who cannot tell ox from horse—can we afford not to drill them? The Duke of Zhou flogged Bo Qin; Cao Can caned his son Cao Jiao two hundred strokes—holy fathers broke skin without breaking love. If you cannot bear a tutor's small discipline now, you will only watch the boy fail and flay his keepers later—what folly is that?
45
在禮太子朝夕視膳，昏定晨省，跪問安否，於情得盡。 五日一朝，于敬既簡，於恩亦疏，易致構間。 故曰「一朝不朝，其間容刀」。 五日之制，起漢高祖，身為天子，父為庶人，萬機事多，故闕私敬耳。 今主上臨朝，太子無事，專主孝養，宜改此俗。 《文王世子》篇曰：「王季一飯亦一飯，再飯亦再飯。 」安有逸豫五日一覲哉！
Ritual sets the heir beside his father's tray morning and night, evening his rest and dawn his health, kneeling to ask after him until duty is spent. An audience every five days slights filial punctilio and thins affection—an open door for slander between you. The adage runs, 'Miss a single morning audience and a blade can slide into the breach.' The five-day rule began with Han Gaozu, Son of Heaven whose father was still a common busyman and whose desk overflowed—so private courtesy gave way. Today the emperor governs alone while the heir has no war to fight; nothing should stop him honoring a father daily—discard that Han habit. The Documents of Ritual recall King Wen's heir: Wang Ji lifted his bowl once and the prince matched him; Wang Ji lifted it twice and the prince matched again. Who under heaven excuses lounging toward a father's kitchen with audiences only every fifth dawn?
46
纘又陳：
Yan Zuan continued:
47
今迎太子神柩，孤魂獨行，太孫幼沖，不可涉道。 謂可遣妃奉迎遠路，令其父衍隨行衛護。 皇太子初見誣陷，臣家門無祐，三世假親，具嘗辛苦，以家觀國，固知太子有變。 臣故求副監國，欲依邴吉故事，距違來使，供養擁護，身親飲食醫藥，冀足救危。 主者以臣名資輕淺，不肯見與。 世人見笑，謂為此職進退難居，有必死憂。 臣獨以為苟全儲君，賈氏所誅，甘心所願。 今監國御史直副皆當三族，侍衛無狀，實自宜然。 臣謂其小人，不足具責。 故孔子曰：「可以托六尺之孤，臨大節而不可奪。 」是以聖王慎選。 故河南尹向雄，昔能犯難葬故將鐘會，文帝嘉之，始拔顯用，至於先帝，以為右率。 如間之事，若得向雄之比，則豈可觸哉！ 此二使者，但為愚怯，亦非與謀，但可誅身，自全三族。 如郭俶、郭斌，則于刑為當。
The crown prince's catafalque is coming home unattended, while the imperial great-grandson is too young for the bitter road east. Send his consort to meet the cortège on the distant road and set her father Yang Yan at her side as escort. When they first framed the heir, my house—three generations joined to the throne by paper marriages—had no luck and knew every grief; what our kin suffered told me the palace would turn on him. So I begged to serve as vice overseer of the realm, to replay Bing Ji's old trick of barring false couriers, to feed and shield the boy with my own hands and physic—anything to snatch him from ruin. The bureaus refused: my name was too slight for such trust. The world sneered that the post was a trap—advance or retreat, you died. I alone reckoned that saving the storied heir—even at the price of every Jia head—would be joy enough. Now the inspectors and aides who guarded the regency heir stand sentenced to three-generation extinction—the household guard rightly shares the scaffold on paper. Yet they were frightened little men—not worth exhausting your wrath upon. Hence the Master's words: Charge him with a boy in mourning; drive him toward the breaking wave—and he cannot be peeled from integrity. That is why sage-kings sift appointments as if their lives hung on them. Consider Xiang Xiong of Henan, who braved edict to bury his old commander Zhong Hui; Emperor Wen exalted him, pulled him from obscurity, and the late emperor named him right-side guide to the heir. Had the latest summons found a conscience like Xiang Xiong's clasped to the boy, what hand could harm him? The two couriers were craven dolts, not plotters—behead them if you must, but spare their kin. Guo Xu and Guo Bin are another matter—here the blade fits.
48
又東宮亦宜妙選忠直亮正，如向雄比。 陛下千秋萬歲之後，太孫幼沖，選置兵衛，宜得柱石之士如周昌者。 世俗淺薄，士無廉節，賈謐小兒，恃寵恣睢，而淺中弱植之徒，更相翕習，故世號魯公二十四友。 又謐前見臣表理太子，曰：「閻兒作此為健，然觀其意，欲與諸司馬家同。 」皆為臣寒心。 伏見詔書，稱明滿奮、樂廣。 侍郎賈胤，與謐親理，而亦疏遠，往免父喪之後，停家五年，雖為小屈，有識貴之。 潘岳、繆征等皆謐父党，共相沈浮，人士羞之，聞其晏然，莫不為怪。 今詔書暴揚其罪，並皆遣出，百姓咸雲清當，臣獨謂非。 但嶽征二十四人，宜皆齊黜，以肅風教。
The Eastern Palace itself needs men as straight as Xiang Xiong. After Your Majesty mounts the crane, when the great-grandson is still milk-teeth small, ring him with Zhou Chang granite—soldiers sworn to blunt any blade turned inward. The age is vulgar, its men brittle; young Jia Mi struts on favor while shallow-root hangers-on imitate him—the world mocks them as Duke of Lu's twenty-four sworn companions. When I petitioned for the heir, Mi once sneered—'Brave work for Yan's cub—but study his motive: he means to clasp hands with every Sima branch alive.' —and my blood ran cold hearing it. I applaud the edict that singles out Man Fen and Yue Guang for clarity. Attendant Gentleman Jia Yin was tethered to Mi by marriage brokerage yet edged away; five years mourning at home looked like setback and men of judgment prized him for it. Pan Yue, Miao Zheng, and the rest floated in Jia Chong's faction; gentlemen held their nose at them yet marveled how lightly they ride the purge. The court now parades their guilt and casts them wholesale; the lanes cheer—yet Your servant disagrees. Dismiss Pan Yue, Miao Zheng, and the entire score of twenty-four together—only that scours the lesson home.
49
朝廷善其忠烈，擢為漢中太守。 趙王倫死，既葬，纘以車轢其塚。 時張華兄子景後徙漢中，纘又表宜還。 纘不護細行，而慷慨好大節。 卒于官，時年五十九。 纘五子，皆開朗有才力。
The court praised his fearless loyalty and raised him governor of Hanzhong. After Prince Zhao Sima Lun was buried, Zuan drove a wagon across his mound. Later, when Zhang Hua's nephew Jing was shifted to Hanzhong, Zuan argued he belonged back at court. Zuan shrugged at petty reputability but prized the grand virtues. He died in office at fifty-nine. His five sons were open-hearted men of parts.
50
長子亨為遼西太守，屬王濬自用其人，亨不得之官。 依青州刺史苟晞，刑政苛虐，亨數切諫，為晞所害。
Eldest son Yan Heng was named to Liaoxi, yet Wang Jun planted his own men and barred Heng from ever taking command. He took refuge with Qingzhou inspector Gou Xi, whose rule was savage; Heng rebuked him again and again until Gou murdered him.
51
史臣曰：湣懷之廢也，天下稱其冤。 然皆懼亂政之參夷，懾淫嬖之凶忍，遂使謀臣懷忠而結舌，義士蓄憤而吞聲。 閻續伯官既微于侍郎，位不登於執戟，輕生重義，視死如歸，伏奏而待嚴誅，輿棺以趨鼎鑊，察言觀行，豈非忠直壯乎！ 顧視晉朝公卿，曾不得與其徒隸齒也。 茂伯篤終，哭王經以全節。 休然追遠，理鄧艾以成名。 故得義感明時，仁流枯骨。 雖硃勃追論新息，欒布奏事彭王，弗之尚也。
The historians write: when Minhuai fell, the realm called it murder by edict. Yet terror of junta butchery and the favorite's knives sealed every planner's mouth and turned every honest man's shout inward. Yan Zuan, style Xubo, sat lower than a palace gentleman and never rose to halberdier rank—yet he weighed righteousness above breath, rolled his coffin toward the cooking cauldron, and lay prone awaiting the blade: read his words, watch his deeds—was that not stalwart loyalty? Beside him the gilt ministers of Jin hardly rated beside his carriage lackeys. Maobo finished as he lived—mourning Wang Jing to seal his honor. Xiuran prosecuted a dead man's case until Deng Ai was posthumously cleared—that was how he earned fame. Their justice lit the dynasty and spilled mercy even on mouldering bones. Zhu Bo's plea for the Marquis of Xinxi after Ma Yuan's fall and Luan Bu's memorial pleading for King Peng of Liang before Gaozu still do not surpass them.
52
贊曰：感義收會，篤終理艾。 道既相侔，名亦俱泰。 續伯區區，輿櫬陳謩。 偪茲淫嬖，弗遂良圖。 啜其泣矣，何嗟及乎！
The verdict sings: Moved by principle, Xiang assembled Zhong Hui's bones; steadfast to the grave, Duan vindicated Deng Ai. Their paths marched in step—their reputations loom together. Lowly Xubo shouldered his bier yet schooled the throne. Vicious favorites boxed him round—sound counsel never ripened. He ends like the ode—'they sip their tears; why sigh when it is too late?'

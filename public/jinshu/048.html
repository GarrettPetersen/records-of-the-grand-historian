向雄段灼閻纘
Biographies of Xiang Xiong, Duan Zhuo, and Yan Zuan.
2
向雄，字茂伯，河內山陽人也。 父韶，彭城太守。 雄初仕郡為主簿，事太守王經。 及經之死也，雄哭之盡哀，市人咸為之悲。 後太守劉毅嘗以非罪笞雄，及吳奮代毅為太守，又以少譴系雄於獄。 司隸鐘會於獄中辟雄為都官從事，會死無人殯斂，雄迎喪而葬之。 文帝召雄而責之曰：「往者王經之死，卿哭王經於東市，我不問也。 今鐘會躬為叛逆，又輒收葬，若復相容，其如王法何！ 」雄曰：「昔者先王掩骼埋胔，仁流朽骨，當時豈先卜其功罪而後葬之哉！ 今王誅既加，於法已備。 雄感義收葬，教亦無闕。 法立於上，教弘於下，何必使雄違生背死以立于時！ 殿下仇枯骨而捐之中野，為將來仁賢之資，不亦惜乎！ 」帝甚悅，與談宴而遣之。
Xiang Xiong, courtesy name Maobo, came from Shanyang in Hanoi. His father, Xiang Shao, had served as governor of Pengcheng. Xiang Xiong began his career as chief clerk of the commandery under Prefect Wang Jing. When Wang Jing was put to death, Xiang Xiong mourned him bitterly, and the crowds at the execution ground wept with him. Later, Prefect Liu Yi had him flogged without real cause; when Wu Fen succeeded Liu Yi, he jailed Xiang Xiong over a minor reprimand. Metropolitan Commissioner Zhong Hui recruited him from jail as an aide in judicial affairs; after Zhong Hui died with no one to lay him out, Xiang Xiong claimed the body and gave him burial. King Wen summoned Xiang Xiong and rebuked him: When Wang Jing died, you mourned him publicly at the eastern market; I let that pass. Zhong Hui turned traitor with his own hands, yet you took it on yourself to bury him. If such behavior keeps getting a pass, what becomes of the law? Xiang Xiong replied: The kings of old gathered exposed bones and buried the dead; their kindness extended even to decayed remains. Did they first weigh each person's guilt or merit before granting burial? Royal justice has already been carried out; the sentence under law is finished. I buried him out of a sense of moral duty, and that breaks no principle of moral teaching. Law comes from above and moral teaching spreads below; why must you force me to dishonor the living and betray the dead just to cut a figure in this world? If Your Highness leaves dried bones as enemies and abandons them in the wilderness, you supply posterity's humane and worthy men with a sad precedent—is that not a pity? The king was greatly pleased, talked with him over a feast, and dismissed him.
3
累遷黃門侍郎。 時吳奮、劉毅俱為侍中，同在門下，雄初不交言。 武帝聞之，敕雄令復君臣之好。 雄不得已，乃詣毅，再拜曰：「向被詔命，君臣義絕，如何？ 」於是即去。 帝聞而大怒，問雄曰：「我令卿復君臣之好，何以故絕？ 」雄曰：「古之君子進人以禮，退人以禮； 今之進人若加諸膝，退人若墜諸川。 劉河內於臣不為戎首，亦已幸甚，安復為君臣之好！ 」帝從之。
He rose through several posts to become a gentleman attendant at the palace Yellow Gates. Wu Fen and Liu Yi were both palace attendants serving together at court, yet Xiang Xiong still refused to speak to them. When Emperor Wu learned of this, he ordered Xiang Xiong to mend relations with his former superiors. Reluctantly Xiang Xiong went to Liu Yi, bowed twice, and said: I have just been commanded to restore cordiality, but the moral bond between lord and minister is already broken—what am I to do? With that he turned and left. The emperor flew into a rage and demanded: I told you to patch things up with your former chief—why did you sever ties instead? Xiang Xiong answered: Men of virtue in antiquity brought others forward courteously and parted from them courteously; today favorites are lifted onto one's lap, and cast-offs are hurled into the ditch. Liu Yi did not make himself my mortal enemy as governor of Hanoi; I already count that lucky. How could we pretend to warm lord-and-minister feelings? The emperor let the matter drop.
4
泰始中，累遷秦州刺史，假赤幢、曲蓋、鼓吹，賜錢二十萬。 咸甯初，入為御史中丞，遷侍中，又出為征虜將軍。 太康初，為河南尹，賜爵關內侯。 齊王攸將歸籓，雄諫曰：「陛下子弟雖多，然有名望者少。 齊王臥在京邑，所益實深，不可不思。 」帝不納。 雄固諫忤旨，起而徑出，遂以憤卒。
During the Taishi era he advanced to governor of Qin Province, was granted the crimson banner, curved ceremonial canopy, and military musicians, and received a gift of two hundred thousand cash. Early in Xianning he was recalled to serve as vice director of the imperial secretariat, promoted to palace attendant, then sent out again as general for barbarian suppression. Early in Taikang he became prefect of the Henan metropolitan district and was ennobled as a secondary marquis. When Prince Qi, Sima You, was ordered back to his princedom, Xiang Xiong urged against it: You have many imperial clansmen, but few who truly command respect. Keeping Prince Qi at the capital does profound good for the realm; you must weigh this carefully. The emperor would not heed him. Xiang Xiong pressed his advice until he angered the throne, then rose and walked straight out of court; he died soon afterward, consumed by indignation.

His younger brother Xiang Kuang became protector-general under Emperor Hui.
6
段灼，字休然，敦煌人也。 世為西土著姓，果直有才辯。 少仕州郡，稍遷鄧艾鎮西司馬，從艾破蜀有功，封關內侯，累遷議郎。 武帝即位，灼上疏追理艾曰：
Duan Zhuo, courtesy name Xiuran, was a native of Dunhuang. His family had long been a powerful clan of the northwest; he was blunt, upright, and a gifted debater. He entered local administration young, rose to become Deng Ai's chief of staff on the western front, earned distinction in the conquest of Shu, was made a secondary marquis, and eventually reached the post of advisory gentleman. After Emperor Wu took the throne, Duan Zhuo submitted a memorial arguing for Deng Ai's rehabilitation:
7
故征西將軍鄧艾，心懷至忠，而荷反逆之名； 平定巴、蜀，而受三族之誅，臣竊悼之。 惜哉，言艾之反也！ 以艾性剛急，矜功伐善，而不能協同朋類，輕犯雅俗，失君子之心，故莫肯理之。 臣敢昧死言艾所以不反之狀。
The late Deng Ai, former general of the western expedition, was utterly loyal at heart yet was branded a traitor; he conquered Ba and Shu for the court, then suffered the extirpation of three generations of his kin—I mourn him in private. How regrettable that people still call Deng Ai a rebel! Because Deng Ai was blunt and impatient, crowed over his deeds and vaunted his superiority, refused to get along with colleagues, and casually offended respectable opinion, he forfeited the sympathy of good men—so nobody would plead his innocence. I risk execution to explain why Deng Ai could not have been plotting rebellion.
8
艾本屯田掌犢人，宣皇帝拔之于農吏之中，顯之於宰府之職。 處內外之官，據文武之任，所在輒有名績，固足以明宣皇帝之知人矣。 會值洮西之役，官兵失利，刺史王經困於圍城之中。 當爾之時，二州危懼，隴右懍懍，幾非國家之有也。 先帝以為深憂重慮，思惟可以安邊殺敵莫賢于艾，故授之以兵馬，解狄道之圍。 圍解，留屯上邽。 承官軍大敗之後，士卒破膽，將吏無氣，倉庫空虛，器械殫盡。 艾欲積穀強兵，以待有事。 是歲少雨，又為區種之法，手執耒耜，率先將士，所統萬數，而身不離僕虜之勞，親執士卒之役。 故落門、段谷之戰，能以少擊多，摧破強賊，斬首萬計。 遂委艾以廟勝成圖，指授長策。 艾受命忘身，龍驤麟振，前無堅敵。 蜀地阻險，山高穀深，而艾步乘不滿二萬，束馬懸車，自投死地，勇氣陵雲，將士乘勢，故能使劉禪震怖，君臣面縛。 軍不逾時，而巴、蜀蕩定，此艾固足以彰先帝之善任矣。
Deng Ai began as a lowly clerk overseeing ox teams on the military farms; Emperor Xuan pulled him from that obscurity and advanced him to responsible posts at headquarters. Whether at court or in the field, in civil or military roles, he produced a distinguished record everywhere he served—clear proof that Emperor Xuan knew how to spot talent. Then came the disaster on the Tao River: imperial forces were routed and Inspector Wang Jing was trapped inside a siege. Both provinces were panic-stricken; the Longyou frontier shook; the region nearly ceased to belong to the state. The late emperor worried deeply and concluded that no one could secure the frontier and strike the enemy better than Deng Ai, so he gave him an army and lifted the siege of Didao. After the siege broke, Deng Ai stayed on to garrison Shanggui. Coming right after a shattering defeat, the soldiers were demoralized, officers had no fight left, stores stood empty, and equipment was gone. Deng Ai wanted to stockpile grain and rebuild the army against whatever came next. Rain was scarce that year, so he introduced pit planting, took hoe and plough in his own hands, and led tens of thousands of troops by example, sharing the dirty work of common soldiers and bondsmen. That is why at Luomen and Duangu he could attack greatly superior forces, shatter powerful enemies, and pile up tens of thousands of counted heads. The court then entrusted him with the grand strategy drawn up in council and gave him wide discretionary powers. Deng Ai obeyed orders as if his life meant nothing; he swept forward like a charging dragon or bounding unicorn and met no foe who could stand firm. Shu was a maze of precipices and deep defiles, yet Deng Ai took fewer than twenty thousand foot and horse, packed horses and hoisted carts over cliffs, and marched into what looked like certain death; courage pierced the clouds, and his men rode that surge until Liu Shan shook with terror and Shu rulers came out bound at their own bidding. In short order Ba and Shu were pacified—clear proof that the late emperor had chosen the right man.
9
艾功名已成，亦當書之竹帛，傳祚萬世。 七十老公，復何所求哉！ 艾以禪初降，遠郡未附，矯令承制，權安社稷。 雖違常科，有合古義，原心定罪，事可詳論。 故鎮西將軍鐘會，有吞天下之心，恐艾威名，知必不同，因其疑似，構成其事。 艾被詔書，即遣強兵，束身就縛，不敢顧望。 誠自知奉見先帝，必無當死之理也。 會受誅之後，艾參佐官屬、部曲將吏，愚戇相聚，自共追艾，破壞檻車，解其囚執。 艾在困地，是以狼狽失據。 夫反非小事，若懷噁心，即當謀及豪傑，然後乃能興動大眾，不聞艾有腹心一人。 臨死口無惡言，獨受腹背之誅，豈不哀哉！ 故見之者垂涕，聞之者歎息。 此賈誼所以慷慨于漢文，天下之事可為痛哭者，良有以也。
Deng Ai had already secured enduring fame; his deeds belonged on bamboo slips and silk for ten thousand generations. What more could a man of seventy possibly want? Because Liu Shan had just surrendered and outlying districts had not yet submitted, Deng Ai issued orders in the emperor's name as an emergency measure to stabilize the state. That bent the usual rules but matched ancient precedent; his motives deserve careful weighing before we call it a crime. Zhong Hui, general of the western garrison, dreamed of seizing the empire; he feared Deng Ai's prestige and knew they could never cooperate, so he seized on ambiguous evidence and framed him. When the summons reached him, Deng Ai dismissed his heavy guard, let himself be tied, and never hesitated or looked back. He believed that once he stood before the late emperor he could not possibly deserve death. After Zhong Hui was executed, Deng Ai's staff officers and unit leaders—simple, stubborn men—rallied, chased down their commander, smashed his cage cart, and freed him from bonds. Caught in that desperate position, Deng Ai floundered without a foothold. Rebellion is no trifle; a true traitor first wins over bold leaders and only then can stir the masses—yet no one ever named a single confidant of Deng Ai. He spoke no treason even facing death, yet was destroyed from front and back—is that not heartbreaking? Witnesses wept; everyone who heard the tale groaned. That is why Jia Yi poured out his grief to Emperor Wen of Han: some national wrongs truly merit bitter tears.
10
陛下龍興，闡弘大度，受誅之家，不拘敘用，聽艾立後，祭祀不絕。 昔秦人憐白起之無罪，吳人傷子胥之冤酷，皆為之立祠。 天下之人為艾悼心痛恨，亦由是也。 謂可聽艾門生故吏收艾屍柩，歸葬舊墓，還其田宅，以平蜀之功，繼封其後，使艾闔棺定諡，死無所恨。 赦冤魂于黃泉，收信義於後世，則天下徇名之士，思立功之臣，必投湯火，樂為陛下死矣！
Since Your Majesty ascended the dragon throne you have shown magnificent generosity: families that suffered execution are not forever barred from office, and Deng Ai has been allowed an heir so his sacrifices continue. The people of Qin mourned Bai Qi as innocent; the people of Wu ached over Wu Zixu's cruel fate—and shrines were raised to both. The empire's grief and outrage over Deng Ai spring from the same sentiment. Let his students and old subordinates recover his remains, bury him in the family plot, restore his lands and houses, ennoble his heirs for the Shu campaign, and settle his posthumous title once the lid is closed—then he can rest without resentment. Free an unjust ghost in the underworld and win good faith for generations to come, and every reputation-minded gentleman and ambitious commander will rush through fire for Your Majesty!
11
帝省表，甚嘉其意。 灼後復陳時宜曰：
The emperor read the memorial and warmly approved its spirit. Later Duan Zhuo submitted further advice on policy of the moment:
12
臣聞天時不如地利，地利不如人和。 三里之城，五里之郭，圜圍而攻之，有不克者，此天時不如地利。 城非不高，池非不深，穀非不多，兵非不利，委而去之，此地利不如人和。 然古之王者，非不先推恩德，結固人心。 人心苟和，雖三里之城，五里之郭，不可攻也。 人心不和，雖金城湯池，不能守也。 臣推此以廣其義，舜彈五弦之琴，詠《南風》之詩，而天下自理，由堯人可比屋而封也。 曩者多難，奸雄屢起，攪亂眾心，刀鋸相乘，流死之孤，哀聲未絕。 故臣以為陛下當深思遠念，杜漸防萌，彈琴詠詩，垂拱而已。 其要莫若推恩以協和黎庶，故推恩足以保四海，不推恩不足以保妻子。 是故唐堯以親睦九族為先，周文以刑于寡妻為急，明王聖主莫不先親後疏，自近及遠。 臣以為太宰、司徒、衛將軍三王宜留洛中鎮守，其餘諸王自州征足任者，年十五以上悉遣之國。 為選中郎傅相，才兼文武，以輔佐之。 聽于其國繕修兵馬，廣布恩信。 必撫下猶子，愛國如家，君臣分定，百世不遷，連城開地，為晉、魯、衛。 所謂磐石之宗，天下服其強矣。 雖云割地，譬猶囊漏貯中，亦一家之有耳。 若慮後世強大，自可豫為制度，使得推恩以分子弟。 如此則枝分葉布，稍自削小，漸使轉至萬國，亦後世之利，非所患也。
I have heard that opportunity from Heaven counts less than terrain, and terrain counts less than unity among the people. Take a tiny citadel ringed by attackers who still cannot crack it—that shows weather and luck lose to strong ground. High walls, deep moats, full granaries, and keen weapons still abandoned mean superior terrain cannot save a divided people. Yet true kings of old always began by spreading kindness and binding hearts together. When hearts are united, even a pocket fortress cannot be stormed. When hearts are divided, neither bronze ramparts nor boiling moats will hold. From this I broaden the point: Shun could rule the realm simply by playing his five-string lute and singing the South Wind ode, because Yao had left the people so virtuous that every household deserved a noble title. Recent generations have suffered endless turmoil: warlords rose again and again, minds were poisoned, executions followed one after another, and the wailing of refugees has barely faded. So I urge Your Majesty to think deep and far, choke troubles at the bud, and govern chiefly by moral influence rather than constant interference. Nothing matters more than spreading kindness to unite the common people: generous rule secures the realm; stinginess cannot even protect one's own household. Hence Yao of Tang began by harmonizing his nine clans, King Wen of Zhou by setting an example for his consort; no wise ruler ever neglected kin near at hand before reaching outward. I propose keeping the three princes who hold the posts of grand tutor, minister of education, and guard general in Luoyang to anchor the capital, while every other prince assessed at the provincial level as fit for duty and aged fifteen or older should be sent out to his fief. Appoint each a mentor and chief minister chosen from the palace corps, men competent in both civil and military affairs. Let them drill troops and spread benevolent authority within their domains. They will cherish subjects like children and the realm like their own household; lord and vassal roles stay fixed for generations; wide domains will stand like the old houses of Jin, Lu, and Wei. That is the true rock-solid royal house, and the empire will respect its strength. Granting territory sounds like loss, yet it is wealth shifting inside one purse—it still stays within the imperial clan. If you fear future over-mighty princes, lay down rules now that allow estates to be subdivided among heirs. Branches will spread and holdings shrink step by step until they resemble the myriad states of old—a benefit to later reigns, not a danger.
13
昔在漢世，諸呂自疑，內有硃虛、東牟之親，外有諸侯九國之強，故不敢動搖。 於今之宜，諸侯強大，是為太山之固。 非我族類，其心必異。 而魏法禁錮諸王，親戚隔絕，不祥莫大焉。 間者無故又瓜分天下，立五等諸侯。 上不象賢，下不議功，而是非雜糅，例受茅土。 似權時之宜，非經久之制，將遂不改，此亦煩擾之人，漸亂之階也。 夫國之興也，由於九族親睦，黎庶協和； 其衰也，在於骨肉疏絕，百姓離心。 故夏邦不安，伊尹歸殷； 殷邦不和，呂氏入周。 殷監在於夏後，去事之誡，誠來事之鑒也。
Under Han the Lü clan hesitated because inside court stood princes like Liu Zhang of Zhu Xu and Liu Xingju of Dong Mou, while outside nine powerful kingdoms watched—so they dared not strike. For present needs, strong regional princes are the surest bulwark—solid as Mount Tai. Those outside the royal kin will never share the same loyalty. Yet Wei law penned princes like criminals and severed family ties—nothing could be more ominous. Lately the realm has again been carved into feudal allotments for no compelling reason, reviving the five-grade nobility. The throne mirrors no sage; the ranks earn no fair tally of deeds—yet rumor and truth are stirred into one pot, and everyone walks away clutching a slice of realm. It answers the panic of the hour, not the architecture of generations; let it harden uncorrected and you hand troublemakers the bottom step toward ruin. Realms climb when kin rings true and the common people breathe as one lung. They fall when blood thins to strangers and the crowd turns its face away. So when Xia shook apart, Yi Yin crossed home to Yin. When Yin quarreled with itself, the Lü came west to Zhou. Shang learned staring at Xia's corpse; yesterday's lecture is tomorrow's lantern.

15
昔伐蜀，募取涼州兵馬、羌胡健兒，許以重報，五千餘人，隨艾討賊，功皆第一。 而《乙亥詔書》，州郡將督，不與中外軍同，雖在上功，無應封者。 唯金城太守楊欣所領兵，以逼江由之勢，得封者三十人。 自金城以西，非在欣部，無一人封者。 苟在中軍之例，雖下功必侯； 如在州郡，雖功高不封，非所謂近不重施，遠不遺恩之謂也。
When Shu fell, Liangzhou riders and lean Qiang and Hu fighters were sworn thick pay; five thousand marched with Deng Ai, and each brought home the highest tally on the scroll. But the Yihai edict denied provincial captains parity with palace hosts and field armies—even deeds stamped "first class" won no patent of nobility. Only Yang Xin's Jincheng column, pinning Jiangyou by the throat, saw thirty men raised to rank. West of Jincheng, outside Yang Xin's roster, not one soldier tasted title. Serve by central-army rule and modest stripes still bought a marquis's seal; hold a provincial posting and towering deeds meant silence—hardly the old pledge that reward need not pile up near the throne while distant sweat is still remembered.
16
臣聞魚懸由於甘餌，勇夫死於重報。 故荊軻慕燕丹之義，專諸感闔閭之愛，匕首振于秦庭，吳刀耀於魚腹，視死如歸，豈不有由也哉！ 夫功名重賞，士之所競，不平致怨，由來久矣。 《詩》云：「屍鳩在桑，其子七兮。 淑人君子，其儀一兮。 」臣以為此等宜蒙爵封。
Sweet bait lifts fish from the stream; heavy coin buys the brave man's last breath. So Jing Ke rode Yan Dan's honor and Zhuan Zhu answered Helü's favor—steel shocked the Qin hall, a blade leapt from the banquet fish—and both stepped toward death as toward their own hearth. Of course they did. Glory and gold are what fighting spirits chase; grudges born of unfair scales are older than memory. The Songs record: "On the mulberry sits the shijiu—seven chicks beneath her wing. The worthy man's measure never divides." " Men of that stamp deserve patents and fields—I would see them clothed in both.

Ziying ruled only as king, not emperor; isolated and without allies, he fell within forty days. Wicked ministers seized authority and inverted truth—calling a deer a horse—which hastened Qin's ruin. When Qin lost control, contenders strove for mastery; Xiang Yu gained the upper hand then squandered it by executing Han Sheng and rejecting Fan Zeng's counsel. Had he dismissed Xiang Bo's bad advice, killed Liu Bang at Hongmen, and ruled from Xianyang over the lords, he would have been unbeatable. He spurned Han Sheng's frank advice and Fan Zeng's strategy, assumed the hegemon's task finished, fixed his capital at Pengcheng, went home in triumph, and flaunted silk by day—petty vanity he mistook for glory. Within five years Han took him prisoner; still unrepentant, he blamed Heaven rather than his generalship—a grievous blindness. Men drawn to humane rule behave like water seeking low ground; hence the saying that tyrants drive the people to founders worthy as Tang and Wu—as predators scatter smaller creatures. Liu Bang rose from common ranks with a sword, conquered the realm with the former states' wealth, and seized power without the gracious abdications of antiquity. His success rested not only on Zhang Liang and Chen Ping but also on how the Xiang clan alienated the populace. Han heirs held the throne for more than two centuries until Emperor Cheng handed authority to his maternal kin and influence drained from the court. Zhang Yu of Anchang ranked among the Three Dukes and tutored Emperor Cheng; the sovereign visited him at home, bowed before his seat, and questioned him closely about omens and policy. Had Zhang Yu acted as a chief minister should—counseling frankly on dangers to the dynasty—the Wangs might not have monopolized power, nor Wang Mang climbed the throne on forged portents and ended Han's line. Zhang Yu flattered and schemed, shuttling among the Five Marquises to save his own skin. Zhu Yun demanded the imperial executioner's blade to strike Zhang Yu down and warn others—a courageous loyalty. Chengdi remained obtuse, treating the protest as insult to his tutor and a capital offense; he ordered guards to seize Zhu Yun and would have executed him summarily. Zhu Yun clung to the palace balustrade until it broke; Xin Qingji saved him by battering his brow in desperate appeal. Otherwise Zhu Yun would have been destroyed on the spot. Leaving the railing unrepaired honored blunt counsel for posterity, yet it did nothing to halt the dynasty's decline. Commentators call Wang Mang history's worst traitor—yet that verdict exaggerates evil much as lore exaggerates King Zhou. Accounts describe Wang Mang beginning as a maternal relative who tempered himself for reputation: kin praised his filial piety, friends his humane conduct. As chief minister under Emperors Cheng and Ai, he labored for the state and earned widespread commendation. Men petitioned endlessly at the palace gates to praise him; officials everywhere credited him with merit and virtue. Han weakened mid-reign, the succession failed three times, and the aged Grand Empress Dowager headed the clan—so Mang invested the child emperor and took the throne. Tang and Wu had likewise seized power first, then ruled legitimately. Had Wang Mang studied how Shang and Zhou took and kept power—ruling with virtue, faith, and generosity for eighteen years until people and heroes rallied—Han shrines might still stand; even a talented Guangwu could hardly have founded his cause. Once enthroned, Wang Mang claimed Heaven's blessing, fancied himself greater than the sage-kings, grew arrogant, ruled by terror and forged omens, and outdid cruelty until omens alarmed him—winter thunder, summer quakes. He still did not reform: harsh laws, collective punishment, favor for sycophants, and execution of honest critics. The empire erupted in rage, realms fragmented, defenses collapsed, and he fell to a common assassin—a spectacle of folly. The fault was not seizing power but ruling without moral methods. After Wang Mang's ruin the realm convulsed: Liu Xuan ruled without clarity, Fan Penzi followed and failed, and Gongsun Shu claimed the throne in Shu. They were no true heirs of Heaven's mandate; they only swept the road for Guangwu. The empire belongs to the world, not to a single ruler. "Shang's army gathered thick as a forest at Muye and pledged: it is we, the border lords, who rise." "And: the royal domains bow to Zhou, for Heaven's mandate shifts." Thus the sovereign is no common person: virtue draws allegiance; its absence breeds revolt. Wise kings of old worried and planned ahead as though standing before a river without crossing. They took Heaven and Earth as models, matched the seasons, cultivated kindness, honored senior ministers, favored the loyal, and shunned flatterers. Filial devotion showed inside the palace; humane rule reached the common people. Their ways were straight as a level line; sincerity moved both men and spirits. Despite palace favorites and in-laws, they refused twisted pleas. Despite intimate servants and favored eunuchs, they ignored indulgent words. The four gates stood open and solemn, welcoming critics without restriction. They trembled with vigilance to preserve Heaven's favor and avoid becoming stepping-stones for later sages. I have heard that those who fear danger tend to stay safe. Those who dread ruin tend to endure. If a sovereign rests secure yet remembers peril, the dynasty lasts, honor holds, and standing matches Heaven and Earth—why dread paving the way for successors! The classic says: "Even a madman's words deserve the wise ruler's scrutiny."

The verdict runs: Moved by duty he gathered in Zhong Hui; keeping faith to the end he settled Deng Ai. Their paths were alike; their reputations rose together in glory. Xubo alone, low in station, bore his bier and set forth his counsel. Crushed by those debauched favorites, sound designs came to nothing. They sob their tears away; too late then for vain lament!

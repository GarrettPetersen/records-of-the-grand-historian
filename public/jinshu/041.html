1
魏舒，字陽元，任城樊人也。 少孤，為外家寧氏所養。 寧氏起宅，相宅者云：「當出貴甥。 」外祖母以魏氏甥小而慧，意謂應之。 舒曰：「當為外氏成此宅相。 」久乃別居。 身長八尺二寸，姿望秀偉，飲酒石餘，而遲鈍質樸，不為鄉親所重。 從叔父吏部郎衡，有名當世，亦不之知，使守水碓，每歎曰：「舒堪數百戶長，我願畢矣！ 」舒亦不以介意。 不修常人之節，不為皎曆之事，每欲容才長物，終不顯人之短。 性好騎射，著韋衣。 入山澤，以漁獵為事。 唯太原王乂謂舒曰：「卿終當為台輔，然今未能令妻子免饑寒，吾當助卿營之。 」常振其匱乏，舒受而不辭。 舒嘗詣野王，主人妻夜產，俄而聞車馬之聲，相問曰：「男也，女也？ 」曰：「男，書之，十五以兵死。 」復問：「寢者為誰？ 」曰：「魏公舒。 」後十五載，詣主人，問所生兒何在，曰：「因條桑為斧傷而死。 」舒自知當為公矣。
Wei Shu, whose courtesy name was Yangyuan, came from Fan in Rencheng commandery. He lost his parents while still young and was brought up by his mother’s people, the Nings. While the Nings were erecting a new residence, a geomancer declared, “This site will produce a distinguished nephew.” His grandmother, noting that the Wei boy was young but bright, assumed the prophecy pointed to him. Shu replied, “It falls to me to vindicate this reading of the house for my mother’s family.” Only much later did he move out and establish a separate household. He stood eight chi two cun tall, carried himself with striking presence, and could put away more than a shi of wine at a sitting, yet he seemed slow and unpolished, so neighbors and relatives thought little of him. His father’s cousin Heng, a celebrated Director in the Ministry of Personnel, likewise failed to see his worth and set him to mind the water-powered mill, sighing, “Shu is good enough to head a few hundred households—that would satisfy me!” Shu himself was untroubled by such slighting treatment. He spurned petty conventional propriety and flashy self-advertisement; he preferred to make room for others’ abilities and strengths and never paraded their faults. He loved horseback archery and habitually dressed in tough leather hunting clothes. He roamed hills and wetlands and lived by fishing and the chase. Only Wang Yi of Taiyuan told him, “You are destined for the highest councils of state, but for now you cannot spare your family want; let me help you get on your feet.” Wang regularly supplied him when he was short, and Shu accepted without false modesty. On a visit to Yewang, Shu was there when the host’s wife delivered at night; soon voices outside—carriage and horses—called out, “Boy or girl?” The answer came: “A son. Mark it down—he will perish by arms at fifteen.” They asked further, “Who is the guest asleep inside?” “Wei Shu of Wei,” came the reply. Fifteen years later he looked up the host and inquired after the child; he was told the boy had died from an axe cut while trimming mulberry—weapon enough to fulfill the omen. Shu understood then that he was fated to rise to ducal rank.
2
年四十餘，郡上計掾察孝廉。 宗党以舒無學業，勸令不就，可以為高耳。 舒曰：「若試而不中，其負在我，安可虛竊不就之高以為己榮乎！ 」於是自課。 百日習一經，因而對策升第。 除澠池長，遷浚儀令，入為尚書郎。 時欲沙汰郎官。 非其才者罷之。 舒曰：「吾即其人也。 」襆被而出。 同僚素無清論者咸有愧色，談者稱之。
Past forty, he was put forward as “filial and incorrupt” by the commandery’s accounts officer. Kinsmen, knowing he had no classical training, urged him to stay home and preserve a reputation for high-minded refusal. Shu objected: “If I sit the examination and fail, the blame is mine; I will not borrow the empty prestige of someone who never tried.” He thereupon imposed a rigorous study regimen on himself. He mastered one classic every hundred days and passed the policy examination with a high placement. He was named magistrate of Mianchi, then of Junyi, and finally entered the capital as a secretary in the Masters of Writing. A purge of the Masters of Writing staff was underway. Anyone judged incompetent was to be removed. Shu said simply, “I am exactly the sort you mean to weed out.” He rolled up his bedding and walked out on the spot. Coworkers who had never enjoyed a good name for integrity looked mortified, while observers praised his honesty.
3
累遷後將軍鐘毓長史，毓每與參佐射，舒常為畫籌而已。 後遇朋人不足，以舒滿數。 毓初不知其善射。 舒容范閒雅，發無不中，舉坐愕然。 莫有敵者。 毓歎而謝曰：「吾之不足以盡卿才，有如此射矣，豈一事哉！ 」轉相國參軍，封劇陽子。 府朝碎務，未嘗見是非； 至於廢興大事，眾人莫能斷者，舒徐為籌之，多出眾議之表。 文帝深器重之，每朝會坐罷，目送之曰：「魏舒堂堂，人之領袖也。 」遷宜陽、滎陽二郡太守，甚有聲稱。 徵拜散騎常侍。 出為冀州刺史，在州三年，以簡惠稱。 入為侍中。 武帝以舒清素，特賜絹百匹。 遷尚書，以公事當免官，詔以贖論。 舒三娶妻皆亡，是歲自表乞假還本郡葬妻，詔賜葬地一頃，錢五十萬。
He rose to serve as chief clerk to Rear General Zhong Yu, who often held archery contests with his staff; Shu was usually relegated to keeping score. Once they were short a participant for the paired shooting, they drafted Shu to round out the party. Zhong Yu had no idea Shu could shoot. Shu stepped up with unhurried grace and hit the mark every time, leaving the assembly dumbfounded. No one there could rival him. Zhong Yu sighed and said, “If I have failed to use your abilities fully, this archery alone proves it— and that is hardly the only thing you can do!” He was reassigned as an adviser on the minister of state’s staff and enfeoffed as Marquis of Juyang. On routine office business he never aired petty disputes; but on weighty questions of state that stumped everyone, he would think them through quietly and usually see farther than the rest. Emperor Wen thought the world of him; after each audience, as Shu withdrew, the emperor would watch him go and murmur, “Wei Shu has true stature—he is born to lead men.” He was appointed governor of both Yiyang and Xingyang and earned wide acclaim. He was recalled to court as a Cavalier Attendant-in-Ordinary. He left the capital to serve as Inspector of Ji, where for three years he was known for a light touch and benevolent rule. He returned to the capital as a Palace Attendant. Emperor Wu, honoring his austerity, presented him with a special grant of a hundred bolts of silk. He rose to Master of Writing; a bureaucratic infraction would normally have cost him his post, but the throne ruled the matter could be settled by a fine in lieu. All three of Shu’s wives had predeceased him; that year he asked leave to go home and bury his latest spouse, and the court granted him a hectare of burial ground and half a million cash toward the funeral.
4
太康初，拜右僕射。 舒與衛瓘、山濤、張華等以六合混一，宜用古典封禪東嶽，前後累陳其事，帝謙讓不許。 以舒為左僕射，領吏部。 舒上言：「今選六宮，聘以玉帛，而舊使禦府丞奉聘，宣成嘉禮，贄重使輕。 以為拜三夫人宜使卿，九嬪使五官中郎將，美人、良人使謁者，於典制為弘。 」有詔詳之，眾議異同，遂寢。 加右光祿大夫、儀同三司。
Early in the Taikang era he was named Right Vice Director of the Masters of Writing. Shu joined Wei Guan, Shan Tao, Zhang Hua, and others in arguing that, with the realm reunited, the time had come for the classical Eastern Peak sacrifices of feng and shan; they pressed the case repeatedly, but the emperor demurred and refused. Shu was made Left Vice Director of the Masters of Writing while concurrently directing appointments. Shu memorialized: “Selection for the harem is sealed with jade and silk, yet we still send a mere treasury aide as matchmaker; the gifts proclaim a grand rite, but the emissary’s rank belittles it.” He proposed that investitures for the Three Ladies be conducted by a minister, for the Nine Imperial Concubines by a Leader of Court Gentlemen for All Purposes, and for lower-ranking consorts by heralds—more in keeping with statutory dignity.” The court ordered a review, but opinion split and the proposal died in committee.” He was further honored as Right Grand Master of Splendid Happiness with ceremonial parity to the Three Dukes.
5
及山濤薨，以舒領司徒，有頃即真。 舒有威重德望，祿賜散之九族，家無餘財。 陳留周震累為諸府所辟，辟書既下，公輒喪亡，僉號震為殺公掾，莫有辟者。 舒乃命之，而竟無患，識者以此稱其達命。 以年老，每稱疾遜位。 中復暫起，署兗州中正，尋又稱疾。 尚書左丞郤詵與舒書曰：「公久疾小差，視事是也，唯上所念。 何竟起訖還臥，曲身回法，甚失具瞻之望。 公少立巍巍，一旦棄之，可不惜哉！ 」舒稱疾如初。 後以災異遜位，帝不聽。 後因正旦朝罷還第，表送章綬。 帝手詔敦勉。 而舒執意彌固，乃下詔曰：「司徒、劇陽子舒，體道弘粹，思量經遠，忠肅居正，在公盡規。 入管銓衡，官人允敘； 出贊袞職，敷弘五教。 惠訓播流，德聲茂著，可謂朝之俊乂者也。 而屢執沖讓，辭旨懇誠，申覽反覆，省用憮然。 蓋成人之美，先典所與，難違至情。 今聽其所執，以劇陽子就第，位同三司，祿賜如前。 几杖不朝，賜錢百萬，床帳簟褥自副。 以舍人四人為劇陽子舍人，置官騎十人。 使光祿勳奉策，主者詳案典禮，令皆如舊制。 」於是賜安車駟馬，門施行馬。 舒為事必先行而後言，遜位之際，莫有知者。 時論以為晉興以來，三公能辭榮善終者，未之有也。 司空衛瓘與舒書曰：「每與足下共論此事，日日未果，可謂瞻之在前，忽焉在後矣。 」太熙元年薨，時年八十二。 帝甚傷悼，賵賻優厚，諡曰康。
On Shan Tao’s death Shu was placed in charge of the Ministry of Education and soon confirmed in full tenure. Shu combined moral authority with high prestige; he shared stipends and gifts across his extended family and kept nothing for himself. Zhou Zhen of Chenliu had been recruited several times, but each time the appointment was issued the sponsoring minister died; people dubbed him the “death-bringing clerk,” and no one would hire him after that. Shu nonetheless offered him a post, and no misfortune followed; observers cited this as proof of Shu’s grasp of destiny. As he aged he repeatedly pleaded illness to resign his offices. He briefly returned to serve as provincial impartial judge for Yan, then once more retired on grounds of ill health. Xi Shen, Left Aide of the Masters of Writing, wrote: “You have been unwell a long time but seem a little better; resuming duty would be fitting—that is what the throne expects.” Why appear at court only to go straight back to bed, dodging your responsibilities? It deeply disappoints everyone who looks to you.” You built a towering reputation in your prime—would you throw it away now? What a waste!” Shu continued to plead ill health as before. He later cited ominous portents to resign, but the emperor would not hear of it. After the New Year audience he went home and formally returned his seals of office. The emperor answered with a personal edict urging him to stay. Shu would not budge, so the throne issued an edict: “Minister of Education and Marquis of Juyang Wei Shu embodies the Way in its purest breadth, plans with long vision, serves with loyal dignity, and gives the state his utmost counsel.” Within the ministry he set the standard for appointments and saw men placed in proper order; in the outer administration he supported the ducal councils and spread the five moral teachings.” His gentle guidance has reached everywhere, and his virtuous name shines brightly—truly he is one of the court’s foremost men.” Yet he has repeatedly pressed his modest wish to retire; his petitions are heartfelt, and each time We read them through We are moved with regret.” The classics teach Us to honor another’s noble purpose, and We cannot deny so sincere a plea.” We therefore grant his wish: he may retire to private life as Marquis of Juyang with rank equal to the Three Dukes and keep his former stipends and perquisites.” He need not attend court; We grant a million cash and supply bed hangings, mats, and bedding for his household.” Four Gentlemen-in-Attendance shall attend the marquis, and ten mounted guards shall be assigned to him.” The Minister of the Imperial Clan shall convey the patent; responsible offices shall follow precedent in every ceremonial detail.” He was also given a comfortable carriage with a four-horse team and the right to mount crossbars at his gate.” Shu always acted before he spoke; when he stepped down, no one had seen it coming. Opinion held that since the Jin dynasty began, no one among the Three Dukes had relinquished power so gracefully and died in honor—until Shu. Minister of Works Wei Guan wrote: “We spoke of this again and again, yet never pinned it down—like something right in front of you that slips away the moment you grasp for it.” He died in the first year of Taixi, aged eighty-two. The emperor grieved deeply, sent lavish funeral goods, and gave him the posthumous title “Kang” (“Peaceful”).
6
子混，字延廣，清惠有才行，為太子舍人。 年二十七，先舒卒，朝野咸為舒悲惜。 舒每哀慟，退而歎曰：「吾不及莊生遠矣，豈以無益自損乎！ 」於是終服不復哭。 詔曰：「舒惟一子，薄命短折。 舒告老之年，處窮獨之苦，每念怛然，為之嗟悼。 思所以散愁養氣，可更增滋味品物。 仍給賜陽燧四望繐窗戶皁輪車牛一乘，庶出入觀望，或足散憂也。 」以庶孫融嗣。 又早卒，從孫晃嗣。
His son Hun, courtesy name Yanguang, was clear-minded, generous, and able, and served as an attendant to the heir apparent. He died at twenty-seven, predeceasing his father; officials and commoners alike mourned for Shu. Shu grieved bitterly, then sighed, “I am no Zhuangzi; why harm myself with useless sorrow?” For the rest of the mourning period he did not weep again. An edict read: “Shu had but one son, and fate cut him off in youth.” In the years when Shu has begged to retire he endures the loneliness of a bereaved old man; the thought wrings Our heart.” We would ease his sorrow and restore his spirits—let richer fare and comforts be added to his table.” Grant him besides a sun-canopied four-window carriage with black wheels and a team of oxen, so drives about the city may lift his gloom.” His grandson Rong, born of a concubine’s line, was named heir. Rong also died young, and the succession passed to a great-grandson, Huang.
7
李憙，字季和，上黨銅鞮人也。 父牷，漢大鴻臚。 憙少有高行，博學研精，與北海管甯以賢良征，不行。 累辟三府，不就。 宣帝復辟憙為太傅屬，固辭疾，郡縣扶輿上道，時憙母疾篤，乃竊逾泫氏城而徒還，遂遭母喪，論者嘉其志節。 後為并州別駕，時驍騎將軍秦朗過并州，州將畢軌敬焉。 令乘車至閣。 憙固諫以為不可，軌不得已從之。
Li Xi, whose courtesy name was Jihe, came from Tongdi in Shangdang commandery. His father Quan had been Grand Herald under the Han. From youth Xi showed sterling character and wide, meticulous scholarship; he and Guan Ning of Beihai were summoned as “worthy and good” candidates, but he declined to report. The Three Ducal Offices summoned him repeatedly; each time he refused. Emperor Xuan appointed him to the Grand Tutor’s staff; he pleaded illness, but local officials forced him into a litter for the journey. His mother lay dying, so he slipped away, slipped past Xuanshi, and walked home; she died soon after, and commentators honored his devotion. He later served as senior aide in Bing Province when Valiant Cavalry General Qin Lang passed through; the provincial commander Bi Gui treated him with marked respect. He insisted that Xi ride in a carriage right up to the gate pavilion. Xi protested firmly that this was improper, and Bi Gui had no choice but yield.
8
景帝輔政，命憙為大將軍從事中郎，憙到，引見，謂憙曰：「昔先公辟君而君不應，今孤命君而君至，何也？ 」對曰：「先君以禮見待，憙得以禮進退。 明公以法見繩，憙畏法而至。 」帝甚重之。 轉司馬，尋拜右長史。 從討毌丘儉還，遷御史中丞。 當官正色，不憚強禦，百僚震肅焉。 薦樂安孫璞，亦以道德顯，時人稱為知人。 尋遷大司馬，以公事免。
While Emperor Jing was regent, he named Xi a staff colonel on the grand general’s command. When Xi reported for duty and was received in audience, the regent asked, “My father once called you to office and you stayed away; I summon you and you come—how is that?” Xi answered, “Your father received me according to propriety, so I could accept or decline in kind.” Your lordship rules by law; I obeyed the law and came.” The regent thought highly of him. He was reassigned as marshal, then soon named Right Chief Clerk. After the expedition against Guanqiu Jian he was promoted to Palace Assistant Secretary. In office he was fearless before the mighty, and the whole bureaucracy stood in awe. He recommended Sun Pu of Le’an, another man of moral stature, and contemporaries praised his eye for talent. He was soon raised to Grand Marshal, then removed over an administrative matter.
9
司馬伷為甯北將軍，鎮鄴，以憙為軍司。 頃之，除涼州刺史，加揚威將軍、假節，領護羌校尉，綏禦華夷，甚有聲績。 羌虜犯塞，憙因其隙會，不及啟聞，輒以便宜出軍深入，遂大克獲，以功重免譴，時人比之漢朝馮、甘焉。 於是請還，許之。 居家月餘，拜冀州刺史，累遷司隸校尉。 及魏帝告禪于晉，憙以本官行司徒事，副太尉鄭沖奉策。 泰始初，封祁侯。
When Sima Zhou held Ye as General Who Pacifies the North, he named Xi director of his headquarters. Before long he became Inspector of Liang with the added titles General Who Displays Might and acting Colonel Protecting the Qiang, staff of authority in hand; he kept peace among Han and non-Han peoples and earned a strong record. When Qiang raiders struck the frontier, Xi seized the opening before he could seek court approval, ordered troops forward on his own authority, and won a major victory; his success outweighed any charge of overstepping, and observers likened him to Feng Fengshi and Gan Yanshou of the Han. He asked to come home, and the court agreed. After a month at home he was named Inspector of Ji and eventually rose to Metropolitan Commandant. When the Wei emperor abdicated in favor of Jin, Xi served provisionally as Minister of Education while assisting Grand Commandant Zheng Chong in presenting the patent of succession. Early in Taishi he was enfeoffed as Marquis of Qi.
10
憙上言：「故立進令劉友、前尚書山濤、中山王睦、故尚書僕射武陔各占官三更稻田，請免濤、睦等官。 陔已亡，請貶諡。 」詔曰：「法者，天下取正，不避親貴，然後行耳，吾豈將枉縱其間哉！ 然案此事皆是友所作，侵剝百姓，以繆惑朝士。 奸吏乃敢作此，其考竟友以懲邪佞。 濤等不貳其過者，皆勿有所問。 《易》稱'王臣蹇蹇，匪躬之故'。 今憙亢志在公，當官而行，可謂'邦之司直'者矣。 光武有云：'貴戚且斂手以避二鮑'。 豈其然乎！ 其申敕群僚，各慎所司，寬宥之恩，不可數遇也。 」憙為二代司隸，朝野稱之。 以公事免。
Xi memorialized that the former magistrate of Lijin Liu You, the former Master of Writing Shan Tao, Prince Mu of Zhongshan, and the late Vice Director Wu Gai had each seized government rice land across three commanderies, and he asked that Shan Tao, the prince, and their ilk be stripped of office.” Wu Gai was already dead; Xi asked that his posthumous title be reduced.” The throne replied: “The law must be the standard for all, kin and grandees included; I will not twist it for anyone’s convenience.” The inquiry shows Liu You alone engineered the seizures, fleecing the people and misleading officials at court. Corrupt underlings dared this; try Liu You to the full extent of the law as a warning to sycophants.” Shan Tao and the rest, having no second offense, shall face no further inquiry.” As the Book of Changes puts it, ‘The king’s minister suffers hardship—not for private gain.’” Li Xi serves the public good without flinching; he is truly what the ode calls ‘the straightener of the state.’” Emperor Guangwu said that even imperial in-laws kept their hands folded to stay clear of Bao Yong and Bao Xian.” Should it not be so today?” Let every official mind his charge—imperial mercy is not something you can count on twice.” Xi served two reigns as Metropolitan Commandant and won praise in court and countryside alike. He was removed from office over an administrative matter.
11
其年，皇太子立，以憙為太子太傅。 自魏明帝以後，久曠東宮，制度廢闕，官司不具，詹事、左右率、庶子、中舍人諸官並未置，唯置衛率令典兵，二傅並攝眾事。 憙在位累年，訓道盡規。 遷尚書僕射，拜特進、光祿大夫，以年老遜位。 詔曰：「光祿大夫、特進李憙，杖德居義，當升台司。 毗亮朕躬，而以年尊致仕。 雖優遊無為，可以頤神，而虛心之望，能不憮然！ 其因光祿之號，改假金紫，置官騎十人，賜錢五十萬，祿賜班禮，一如三司，門施行馬。」
The same year the heir apparent was invested, Xi became his Grand Tutor. Since Emperor Ming of Wei the heir’s quarters had stood empty; institutions had lapsed, posts were unfilled, and offices such as the Household Superintendent, the Left and Right Guard Leaders, the Mentors, and the Gentlemen Attendants did not exist—only a Guard commander held troops, while both tutors handled every other duty. Xi held the post for years, teaching the heir and offering unstinting counsel. He rose to Vice Director of the Masters of Writing and received the titles Specially Advanced and Grand Master of Splendid Happiness, then retired on account of age. An edict read: “Grand Master of Splendid Happiness and Specially Advanced Li Xi, a pillar of virtue and integrity, was fit for the highest councils.” He aided and enlightened Us, yet chose to retire on grounds of age.” Leisure may nourish the spirit, yet how can We not feel the ache of an unfulfilled hope!” Retain his Splendid Happiness title but grant gold ribbon and purple sash, assign ten mounted attendants, and award half a million cash; his stipends and court precedence shall match the Three Dukes, with crossbars mounted at his gate.”
12
初，憙為僕射時，涼州虜寇邊，憙唱義遣軍討之。 朝士謂出兵不易，虜未足為患，竟不從之。 後虜果大縱逸，涼州覆沒，朝廷深悔焉。 以憙清素貧儉，賜絹百匹。 及齊王攸出鎮，憙上疏諫爭，辭甚懇切。 憙自曆仕，雖清非異眾，而家無儲積，親舊故人乃至分衣共食，未嘗私以王官。 及卒，追贈太保，諡曰成。 子贊嗣。
Earlier, while Xi was Vice Director, tribes on the Liang frontier raided the border; he was the first to urge a punitive expedition. Courtiers argued that mobilizing troops was too costly and the raiders no real threat, and in the end they ignored him. The tribes then ran wild, Liang fell, and the court bitterly regretted its refusal. Because he lived in austere poverty, the court granted him a hundred bolts of silk. When Prince You of Qi was sent to his princely fief, Xi submitted a passionate memorial of protest. Through a long career he remained no richer than most honest men—there was nothing laid by, and he shared clothes and food with friends and kin—yet he never abused public office for private gain. After his death he was posthumously honored as Grand Guardian with the title “Cheng” (“Accomplished”). His son Zan inherited the title.

Liu Shi, courtesy name Zizhen, was a native of Gaotang in Pingyuan commandery. He descended from Prince Hui of Jibei, Liu Shou, of the Han; his father Guang had been magistrate of Chiqiu. He grew up in grinding poverty and sold straw cattle-mats to get by. Yet he loved books, knotted cords on his wrist as prompts, recited texts aloud, and mastered the classical tradition from antiquity to his own day. He kept himself scrupulously clean and his conduct without blemish. The commandery nominated him as filial and incorrupt and the province as a “flourishing talent”; he declined both. He went to Luoyang as a commandery accounts officer, was named aide to the Governor of Henan, and rose to secretary in the Masters of Writing and senior judge in the court of justice. He later served in the Ministry of Personnel, joined the staff of Sima Zhao as minister of state, and was enfeoffed as Marquis of Xunyang.

以世多進趣，廉遜道闕，乃著《崇讓論》以矯之。 其辭曰：
Seeing his contemporaries scramble for advancement while modesty vanished, he wrote “On Exalting Yielding” to set matters right. It begins:
17
古之聖王之化天下，所以貴讓者，欲以出賢才，息爭競也。 夫人情莫不欲已之賢也，故勸令讓賢以自明賢也，豈假讓不賢哉！ 故讓道興，賢能之人不求而自出矣，至公之舉自立矣，百官之副亦豫具矣。 一官缺，擇眾官所讓最多者而用之，審之道也。 在朝之士相讓於上，草廬之人咸皆化之，推賢讓能之風從此生矣。 為一國所讓，則一國士也； 天下所共推，則天下士也。 推讓之風行，則賢與不肖灼然殊矣。 此道之行，在上者無所用其心，因成清議，隨之而已。 故曰，蕩蕩乎堯之為君，莫之能名。 言天下自安矣，不見堯所以化之，故不能名也。 又曰，舜禹之有天下而不與焉，無為而化者其舜也歟。 賢人相讓于朝，大才之人恆在大官，小人不爭於野，天下無事矣。 以賢才化無事，至道興矣。 已仰其成，復何與焉！ 故可以歌《南風》之詩，彈五弦之琴也。 成此功者非有他，崇讓之所致耳。 孔子曰，能以禮讓為國，則不難也。
The sage-kings of old prized yielding because it brought worthy men forward and stilled destructive rivalry. Everyone wishes to be thought worthy, so we urge them to yield place to the better qualified and thus prove their own quality—this is no pretense of yielding to the unworthy!” When yielding prevails, able men step forward unbidden, the most impartial choices make themselves, and deputy posts are filled in advance. When a post opens, appoint the man most often yielded to by his peers—that is sound policy. If ministers defer to one another before the throne, commoners in their huts will follow suit, and a culture of recommending the able will take root. The man an entire kingdom would yield to is that kingdom’s foremost gentleman; the man the whole realm would advance is the foremost gentleman of the age. Once yielding is the fashion, worthy and unworthy stand clearly apart. Under such a system rulers need not micromanage; pure public opinion does the sorting for them. Hence the saying: “How vast was Yao as ruler—no one could put a name to his art.” The realm was at peace of itself; people could not point to how Yao had engineered it, and so could not describe his method. It is also said that Shun and Yu possessed the realm as if it were not theirs—who ruled by nonaction if not Shun?” Let worthies defer in court, place great talent in high office, and keep petty strivers from the countryside, and the realm will know no turmoil. Governing through worthy men until nothing remains to do is the highest Way. The ruler has only to trust the outcome—what need is there to meddle?” Then he may sing the “South Wind” and strum the five-string lute in peace. No other art produced such order—only the exaltation of yielding. Confucius said that ruling a state through courteous yielding presents no difficulty.
18
在朝之人不務相讓久矣，天下化之。 自魏代以來，登進辟命之士，及在職之吏，臨見受敘，雖自辭不能，終莫肯讓有勝己者。 夫推讓之風息，爭競之心生。 孔子曰，上興讓則下不爭，明讓不興下必爭也。 推讓之道興，則賢能之人日見推舉； 爭競之心生，則賢能之人日見謗毀。 夫爭者之欲自先，甚惡能者之先，不能無毀也。 故孔墨不能免世之謗己，況不及孔墨者乎！ 議者僉然言，世少高名之才，朝廷不有大才之人可以為大官者。 山澤人小官吏亦復雲，朝廷之士雖有大官名德，皆不及往時人也。 餘以為此二言皆失之矣。 非時獨乏賢也，時不貴讓。 一人有先眾之譽，毀必隨之，名不得成使之然也。 雖令稷契復存，亦不復能全其名矣。 能否混雜，優劣不分，士無素定之價，官職有缺，主選之吏不知所用，但案官次而舉之。 同才之人先用者，非勢家之子，則必為有勢者之所念也。 非能獨賢，因其先用之資，而復遷之無已。 遷之無已，不勝其任之病發矣。 觀在官之人，政績無聞，自非勢家之子，率多因資次而進也。
Courtiers have long ceased to defer to one another, and the whole realm has taken its cue from them. Since the Wei dynasty, appointees and serving officials alike, even when they protest that they are unfit, never yield to someone more capable. When yielding dies, the scramble for place begins. Confucius said that when superiors encourage yielding, inferiors do not fight; when yielding is not honored below, strife is inevitable. Where yielding flourishes, able men are advanced every day; where rivalry rules, they are slandered every day. Schemers want to be first themselves and loathe anyone ahead of them; slander follows inevitably. Even Confucius and Mozi could not escape calumny; what hope have lesser men?” Critics all insist the age lacks men of towering reputation and that the court has no one fit for the highest posts. Rustic clerks echo the charge that today’s grandees, however titled, cannot match men of former days. Both verdicts are wrong. The problem is not a shortage of worthies but a culture that scorns yielding. Whoever outshines the pack is torn down; reputations cannot mature because of it. Even Ji and Xie reborn could not keep their names intact in such a climate. Able and inept are jumbled together, merit goes unrecognized, and gentlemen have no settled standing; when posts open, appointing officials have no guide but seniority. Among equals, the first promoted are either sons of great houses or men favored by the powerful. They are not uniquely virtuous; their head start feeds endless promotion. Endless advancement soon exposes men who cannot bear their responsibilities. Look at men in office: few have real achievements; unless they are wellborn, most have climbed by seniority alone.
19
向令天下貴讓，士必由於見讓而後名成，名成而官乃得用之。 諸名行不立之人，在官無政績之稱，讓之者必矣，官無因得而用之也。 所以見用不息者，由讓道廢，因資用人之有失久矣。 故自漢魏以來，時開大舉，令眾官各舉所知，唯才所任，不限階次，如此者甚數矣。 其所舉必有當者，不聞時有擢用，不知何誰最賢故也。 所舉必有不當，而罪不加，不知何誰最不肖也。 所以不可得知，由當時之人莫肯相推，賢愚之名不別，令其如此。 舉者知在上者察不能審，故敢漫舉而進之。 或舉所賢，因及所念，一頓而至，人數猥多，各言所舉者賢，加之高狀，相似如一，難得而分矣。 參錯相亂，真偽同貫，更復由此而甚。 雖舉者不能盡忠之罪，亦由上開聽察之路濫，令其爾也。 昔齊王好聽竽聲，必令三百人合吹而後聽之，廩以數人之俸。 南郭先生不知吹竽者也，以三百人合吹可以容其不知，因請為王吹竽，虛食數人之俸。 嗣王覺而改之，難彰先王之過。 乃下令曰：「吾之好聞竽聲有甚于先王，欲一一列而聽之。 」先生于此逃矣。 推賢之風不立，濫舉之法不改，則南郭先生之徒盈於朝矣。 才高守道之士日退，馳走有勢之門日多矣。 雖國有典刑，弗能禁矣。
If yielding were honored across the realm, a man would first have to be publicly deferred to before his name was made; only then would office rightly take him up. Men of no established reputation who achieve nothing in office would surely be passed over in favor of others, and the state would have no excuse to employ them. Mediocre men stay in office because yielding has died out and appointment by seniority alone has misgoverned us for ages. Since Han and Wei, the court has often ordered a general call for recommendations—each official to name men he knew, talent the only criterion, rank no barrier—and such measures have been tried again and again. Some nominees must have been fit, yet we never hear who was actually promoted, because no one can tell who was worthiest. Some nominees must have been unfit, yet no one is punished—again because no one can pick out the worst. The confusion comes from everyone refusing to yield place, so worthy and worthless look the same. Recommenders know superiors cannot vet every name, so they nominate recklessly. Some push their favorites in a single mass nomination; the lists swell, every sponsor swears his picks are paragons and pads their files with identical praise, so the truth cannot be sorted out. Truth and falsehood are tangled together, and the mess only deepens. Recommenders share the blame, but the throne invited the abuse by opening indiscriminate channels of review. The king of Qi loved massed mouth-organs and would hear no fewer than three hundred players at once, paying each band the stipend of several ordinary musicians. Mr. Southwall could not play at all, but hid inside the three-hundred-man ensemble, volunteered for the royal band, and drew several musicians’ pay for nothing. The new king saw the fraud but hesitated to shame his father’s memory. He therefore proclaimed: “I love the mouth-organ even more than my father did—I mean to hear each player perform solo.” At that Mr. Southwall fled. Until we revive real recommendation and fix promiscuous nomination, the court will swarm with Southwalls. Able men of principle withdraw while sycophants rush the doors of the mighty in ever greater numbers. Statute law alone cannot stop it.
20
夫讓道不興之弊，非徒賢人在下位，不得時進也，國之良臣荷重任者，亦將以漸受罪退矣。 何以知其然也？ 孔子以為顏氏之子不貳過耳，明非聖人皆有過。 寵貴之地欲之者多矣，惡賢能者塞其路，其過而毀之者亦多矣。 夫謗毀之生，非徒空設，必因人之微過而甚之者也。 譭謗之言數聞，在上者雖欲弗納，不能不杖所聞，因事之來而微察之也，無以，其驗至矣。 得其驗，安得不理其罪。 若知而縱之，王之威日衰，令之不行自此始矣。 知而皆理之，受罪退者稍多，大臣有不自固之心。 夫賢才不進，貴臣日疏，此有國者之深憂也。 《詩》曰：「受祿不讓，至於已斯亡。 」不讓之人憂亡不暇，而望其益國朝，不亦難乎！
The evil of suppressing yielding is not merely that talent languishes below; even good ministers in heavy posts will in time be hounded from office. How do we know? Confucius said only Yan Hui never repeated a mistake—meaning every man short of sagehood errs. Coveted posts attract many suitors; the envious bar the able and seize on the slightest slip to destroy them. Slander is never baseless—it magnifies a man’s smallest lapses. Rumors pile up until even a reluctant ruler feels bound to heed them, probes each incident, and finds seeming proof. Once “proof” appears, punishment must follow. To ignore verified guilt would drain royal authority and mark the start of disobedience. Punish every case and dismissed ministers multiply, until senior officials no longer feel secure. Barred talent and alienated ministers are a ruler’s gravest concern. The Book of Odes says, ‘They took emoluments without yielding—thus they brought ruin on themselves.’ Men who never yield busy themselves dodging disaster—can we expect them to enrich the realm?”
21
竊以為改此俗甚易耳。 何以知之？ 夫一時在官之人，雖雜有凡猥之才，其中賢明者亦多矣，豈可謂皆不知讓賢為貴邪！ 直以其時皆不讓，習以成俗，故遂不為耳。 人臣初除，皆通表上聞，名之謝章，所由來尚矣。 原謝章之本意，欲進賢能以謝國恩也。 昔舜以禹為司空，禹拜稽首，讓於稷契及咎繇。 使益為虞官，讓於硃虎、熊、羆。 使伯夷典三禮，讓于夔龍。 唐虞之時，眾官初除，莫不皆讓也。 謝章之義，蓋取於此。 《書》記之者，欲以永世作則。 季世所用，不賢不能讓賢，虛謝見用之恩而已。 相承不變，習俗之失也。
I believe this habit could be changed with ease. Why? Officialdom always mixes mediocrities with many who do know that yielding to the better man is right. They simply follow a custom of never yielding—it is habit, not ignorance. New appointees have always memorialized their thanks to the throne—the practice is ancient. The memorial of thanks was meant to recommend better men in return for imperial favor. When Shun made Yu Minister of Works, Yu kowtowed and ceded the honor to Ji, Xie, and Gao Yao. When Yi was made forest warden, he yielded to Zhu Hu, Xiong, and Pi. Boyi was put in charge of the three classes of rites but named Kui and Long instead. Under Yao and Shun every new minister deferred to rivals. The thanks memorial harks back to that ideal. The Book of Documents preserves the stories to be an eternal model. Later ages hollowed the rite into empty gratitude from men neither able nor willing to yield. Unthinking continuity turned it into a vulgar habit.
22
夫敘用之官得通章表者，其讓賢推能乃通，其不能有所讓徒費簡紙者，皆絕不通。 人臣初除，各思推賢能而讓之矣，讓之文付主者掌之。 三司有缺，擇三司所讓最多者而用之。 此為一公缺，三公已豫選之矣。 且主選之吏，不必任公而選三公，不如令三公自共選一公為詳也。 四征缺，擇四征所讓最多而用之，此為一征缺，四征已豫選之矣，必詳於停缺而令主者選四征也。 尚書缺，擇尚書所讓最多者而用之，此為八尚書共選一尚書，詳于臨缺令主者選八尚書也。 郡守缺，擇眾郡所讓最多者而用之，詳于任主者令選百郡守也。
Let every appointing officer accept only memorials that name worthier men; bar empty thanks that waste paper. Each new minister would propose a worthier candidate; those nominations go on file with the registrar. Fill a vacancy among the Three Dukes with the man most often yielded to by sitting dukes. Thus one post is filled from a pool the three dukes have already ranked. Better the Three Dukes choose one of their own than leave it to a personnel clerk who need not be a duke himself. Do the same for the four expeditionary commands: when one opens, pick the candidate most deferred to by all four—a far sounder method than freezing the post and letting a clerk pick. For a vacancy among the Masters of Writing, let the eight incumbents jointly nominate the man most yielded to—wiser than a hurried personnel shuffle. Governorships should go to the men most widely recommended by other governors, not to whoever a central clerk favors.
23
夫以眾官百郡之讓，與主者共相比，不可同歲而論也。 雖復令三府參舉官，本不委以舉選之任，各不能以根其心也。 其所用心者裁之不二三，但令主者案官次而舉之，不用精也。 賢愚皆讓，百姓耳目盡為國耳目。 夫人情爭則欲毀己所不知，讓則競推於勝己。 故世爭則毀譽交錯，優劣不分，難得而讓也。 時讓則賢智顯出，能否之美歷歷相次，不可得而亂也。 當此時也，能退身修已者，讓之者多矣。 雖欲守貧賤，不可得也。 馳騖進趣而欲人見讓，猶卻行而求前也。 夫如此，愚智咸知進身求通，非修之於己則無由矣。 游外求者，於此相隨而歸矣。 浮聲虛論，不禁而自息矣。 人人無所用其心，任眾人之議，而天下自化矣。 不言之化行，巍巍之美於此著矣。 讓可以致此，豈可不務之哉！
Peer nomination across hundreds of posts bears no comparison to a clerk ticking names in one sitting. Even when the Three Offices “assist” in selection, they are not truly charged with it and never invest their hearts in the outcome. They skim two or three names and tell the clerk to go by seniority—no care at all. If every man must yield, the people become the state’s eyes and ears. Rivalry breeds slander of the unknown; yielding makes men lift up their betters. In a contentious age praise and blame tangle until merit is invisible and yielding impossible. When yielding is the fashion, talent stands out in clear order and cannot be confounded. Then men who retire to perfect themselves win many sponsors. They cannot stay obscure even if they wish to. To scramble for place yet expect others to yield is to walk backward and hope to advance. Then fool and sage alike see that advancement lies in self-cultivation or nowhere. Adventurers who sought shortcuts would give them up and come home. Empty gossip would die without being banned. Rulers need not scheme; trust public consensus and the realm orders itself. Silent transformation unfolds, and the majesty of true rule appears. Yielding can achieve this—how can we neglect it?”
24
《春秋傳》曰：「范宣子之讓，其下皆讓。 欒黶雖汰，弗敢違也。 晉國以平，數世賴之。 」上世之化也，君子尚能而讓其下，小人力農以事其上，上下有禮，讒慝遠黜，由不爭也。 及其亂也，國家之弊，恆必由之。 篤論了了如此。 在朝君子典選大官，能不以人廢言，舉而行之，各以讓賢舉能為先務，則群才猥出，能否殊別，蓋世之功，莫大於此。
The Zuo Commentary says, ‘When Fan Xuanzi yielded, his subordinates yielded in turn.’ Even the reckless Luan Yan dared not defy the example. Jin knew peace for generations because of it. Such was the teaching of high antiquity: gentlemen honored ability and stepped aside for subordinates, commoners farmed to support their betters, rank observed ritual, and slander vanished because nothing was contested.” When that order collapses, national ruin always follows. The moral is plain. If ministers in charge of appointments heed this advice despite its author and put yielding first, talent will pour forth, competence will sort itself, and no reform would do more good.

泰始初，進爵為伯，累遷少府。 咸甯中為太常。 轉尚書。 杜預之伐吳也，寔以本官行鎮南軍司。
Early in Taishi his fief was raised to earl’s rank and he rose to Privy Treasurer. During Xianning he served as Minister of Ceremonies. He was transferred to the Masters of Writing. During Du Yu’s conquest of Wu, Liu Shi served as army director on the southern command staff in his regular capacity.

初，寔妻盧氏生子躋而卒，華氏將以女妻之。 寔弟智諫曰：「華家類貪，必破門戶。 」辭之不得，竟婚華氏而生子夏。 寔竟坐夏受賂，免官。 頃之為大司農，又以夏罪免。
Shi’s first wife, Lady Lu, bore a son, Ji, and died; the Hua family then proposed a daughter as his next wife. His brother Zhi warned him, “The Huas are a grasping clan—they will ruin our house.” He could not refuse; he married into the Huas and fathered a son, Xia. Shi was stripped of office when Xia was convicted of taking bribes. Soon named Minister of Agriculture, he lost that post too over Xia’s guilt.
27
寔每還州裏，鄉人載酒肉以候之。 寔難逆其意，輒共啖而返其餘。 或謂寔曰：「君行高一世，而諸子不能遵。 何不旦夕切磋，使知過而自改邪！ 」寔曰：「吾之所行，是所聞見，不相祖習，豈復教誨之所得乎！ 」世以寔言為當。
Whenever Shi visited his home commandery, villagers brought wine and meat to greet him. Unwilling to spurn their kindness, he shared the meal and sent the leftovers back. Someone said to him, “Your conduct towers above the age, yet your sons do not follow it.” Why not coach them daily until they mend their ways?” Shi replied, “My conduct comes from what I saw and heard; it is not something sons pick up by family habit—how can lectures instill it?” Contemporaries judged him right.
28
後起為國子祭酒、散騎常侍。 湣懷太子初封廣陵王，高選師友，以寔為師。 元康初，進爵為侯，累遷太子太保，加侍中、特進、右光祿大夫、開府儀同三司，領冀州都督。 九年，策拜司空，遷太保，轉太傅。 太安初，寔以老病遜位，賜安車駟馬、錢百萬，以侯就第。 及長沙成都之相攻也，寔為軍人所掠，潛歸鄉里。
He was later recalled as Libationer of the Imperial Academy and Cavalier Attendant-in-Ordinary. When the heir Minhuai was first made Prince of Guangling, the court chose eminent tutors and named Shi his principal teacher. Early in Yuankang he was promoted to marquis, rose to Junior Mentor to the heir, and accumulated the titles Palace Attendant, Specially Advanced, Right Grand Master of Splendid Happiness, and commander with ceremonial parity to the Three Dukes while directing military affairs in Ji Province. In the ninth year he was appointed Minister of Works by patent, then Grand Guardian, then Grand Tutor. Early in Tai’an he resigned on grounds of age and ill health, receiving a comfortable carriage, a four-horse team, and a million cash to retire home as a marquis. During the war between the princes of Changsha and Chengdu, soldiers plundered him and he slipped away to his native place.
29
三年，詔曰：「昔虞任五臣，致垂拱之化，漢相蕭何，興寧一之譽，故能光隆於當時，垂裕於百代。 朕紹天明命，臨御萬邦，所以崇顯政道者，亦賴之於元臣庶尹，畢力股肱，以副至望。 而君年耆告老，確然難違。 今聽君以侯就第，位居三司之上，秩祿准舊，賜几杖不朝及宅一區。 國之大政，將就諮於君，副朕意焉。 」歲余薨，時年九十一，諡曰元。
In the third year an edict declared: “Yu employed five ministers and ruled with folded hands; Chancellor Xiao He of Han won fame for unifying the realm—thus they glorified their own age and left an example for all time.” We have inherited Heaven’s charge and rule the myriad lands; to exalt good government We depend on chief ministers and high officials to give their utmost as Our arms and legs and meet Our deepest expectations.” Yet you, venerable in years, have begged to retire, and your resolve cannot be denied.” We grant you leave to retire home as a marquis with rank above the Three Dukes, stipends as before, a stool and staff excusing you from court, and a residence.” On weighty affairs of state We shall still seek your counsel—such is Our wish.” A little over a year later he died at ninety-one and received the posthumous title “Yuan” (“Primordial”).
30
寔少貧窶，杖策徒行，每所憩止，不累主人，薪水之事，皆自營給。 及位望通顯，每崇儉素，不尚華麗。 嘗詣石崇家，如廁，見有絳紋帳，裀褥甚麗，兩婢持香囊。 寔便退，笑謂崇曰：「誤入卿內。 」崇曰：「是廁耳。 」寔曰：「貧士未嘗得此。 」乃更如他廁。 雖處榮寵，居無第宅，所得俸祿，贍恤親故。 雖禮教陵遲，而行己以正。 喪妻為廬杖之制，終喪不禦內。 輕薄者笑之，寔不以介意。 自少及老，篤學不倦，雖居職務，卷弗離手。 尤精《三傳》，辨正《公羊》，以為衛輒不應辭以王父命，祭仲失為臣之節，舉此二端以明臣子之體，遂行於世。 又撰《春秋條例》二十卷。
In youth he was destitute, walked with staff in hand, and wherever he lodged imposed nothing on his host—he fetched his own fuel and water. Even after he rose to prominence he clung to plain austerity and shunned display. On a visit to Shi Chong he stepped into what he thought was a lavatory and found crimson brocade hangings, sumptuous bedding, and two maids bearing sachets of perfume. Shi backed out and said with a smile, “I blundered into your private rooms.” Chong said, “That was the privy.” Shi replied, “A poor scholar like me has never seen a privy like that.” He used another lavatory instead. Though honored at court, he owned no grand house and spent his salary supporting kinsmen and old friends. Even as public morals slipped, he held himself to the straight path. When his wife died he observed the full mourning hut and staff rites and took no concubine until they ended. Wits mocked him; he paid them no mind. From youth to age he studied tirelessly and never set a book aside, even in office. He excelled in the Three Commentaries and revised readings of the Gongyang, arguing that Prince Zhe of Wei was wrong to refuse the throne citing his grandfather’s command and that Ji Zhong had betrayed a minister’s duty; these two cases became his model of how subjects and sons should behave, and his reading won wide acceptance. He also compiled twenty fascicles of Spring and Autumn precedents.
31
有二子，躋、夏。 躋字景雲，官至散騎常侍。 夏以貪污棄放於世。
His progeny included Ji and the later-disgraced Xia. Ji, courtesy name Jingyun, rose to Cavalier Attendant-in-Ordinary. Xia was disgraced for graft and cast out of public life.
32
弟智，字子房，貞素有兄風。 少貧窶，每負薪自給，讀誦不輟，竟以儒行稱。 曆中書黃門吏部郎，出為潁川太守。 平原管輅嘗謂人曰：「吾與劉潁川兄弟語，使人神思清發，昏不假寐。 自此之外，殆白日欲寢矣。 」入為秘書監，領南陽王師，加散騎常侍，遷侍中、尚書、太常。 著《喪服釋疑論》，多所辨明。 太康末卒，諡曰成。
His brother Zhi, courtesy name Zifang, was as upright and austere as Liu Shi himself. He grew up poor, hauled his own firewood, never stopped reading, and won renown as a Confucian gentleman. He served in the Palace Secretariat, the Yellow Gates, and the Ministry of Personnel, then left the capital as governor of Yingchuan. Guan Lu of Pingyuan once said, “Conversation with the Liu brothers of Yingchuan clears the mind so that evening needs no nap.” With anyone else I could nap in broad daylight.” Zhi was recalled as Director of the Palace Library and tutor to the Prince of Nanyang, given the added title Cavalier Attendant-in-Ordinary, and rose to Palace Attendant, Master of Writing, and Minister of Ceremonies. He wrote “Resolving Doubts on Mourning Dress,” clarifying many disputed points. He died toward the end of Taikang with the posthumous title “Cheng” (“Accomplished”).
33
高光，字宣茂，陳留圉城人，魏太尉柔之子也。 光少習家業，明練刑理。 初以太子舍人累遷尚書郎，出為幽州刺史、潁州太守。 是時武帝置黃沙獄，以典詔囚。 以光曆世明法，用為黃沙御史，秩與中丞同，遷廷尉。 元康中，拜尚書，典三公曹。 時趙王倫篡逆，光於其際，守道全貞。 及倫賜死，齊王冏輔政，復以光為廷尉，遷尚書，加奉車都尉。 後從駕討成都王穎有勳，封延陵縣公，邑千八百戶。 于時朝廷咸推光明于用法，故頻典理官。 惠帝為張方所逼，幸長安，朝臣奔散，莫有從者，光獨侍帝而西。 遷尚書左僕射，加散騎常侍。 光兄誕為上官巳等所用，曆徐、雍二州刺史。 誕性任放無倫次，而決烈過人，與光異操。 常謂光小節，恆輕侮之，光事誕愈謹。 帝既還洛陽，時太弟新立，重選傅訓，以光為少傅，加光祿大夫，常侍如故。 及懷帝即位，加光祿大夫金章紫綬，與傅祗並見推崇。 尋為尚書令，本官如故。 以疾卒，贈司空、侍中。 屬京洛傾覆，竟未加諡。
Gao Guang, courtesy name Xuanmao, came from Yucheng in Chenliu and was the son of the Wei Grand Commandant Gao Rou. From youth he studied his family’s legal tradition and mastered penal law. He began as a crown prince’s attendant, rose to secretary in the Masters of Writing, then served as Inspector of You and governor of Ying commandery. Emperor Wu had just founded the Yellow Sand prison for inmates sentenced by imperial edict. Given the family’s legal expertise, Guang was named censor of the Yellow Sand prison with rank equal to the Palace Assistant Secretary, then promoted Director of Justice. During Yuankang he became a Master of Writing in charge of the Three Dukes section. When Prince of Zhao Sima Lun seized power, Guang held to principle and kept his honor intact. After Lun was forced to kill himself, Prince of Qi Sima Jiong became regent and reappointed Guang Director of Justice, then Master of Writing with the added title Commandant of Court Carriages. He later accompanied the emperor against Prince of Chengdu Sima Ying and earned a dukedom of Yanling county with eighteen hundred households. The court acknowledged his mastery of the law and repeatedly put him over the judiciary. When Emperor Hui was driven by Zhang Fang to take refuge in Chang’an and the court scattered, Guang alone followed him west. He was made Left Vice Director of the Masters of Writing and Cavalier Attendant-in-Ordinary. Guang’s elder brother Dan served Shangguan Si’s faction and held successive terms as Inspector of Xu and Yong. Dan was reckless and disorderly yet bolder than most—a temper utterly unlike his brother’s. He sneered at Guang as fussy and treated him with contempt, yet Guang treated him with growing deference. After the emperor’s return to Luoyang, with a new heir designate named, Guang was chosen Junior Tutor with the added title Grand Master of Splendid Happiness while keeping his position as attendant-in-ordinary. Emperor Huai added the gold seal and purple ribbon to his Splendid Happiness title and ranked him with Fu Zhi among the most honored ministers. He soon became Director of the Masters of Writing while retaining his other posts. He died of illness and was posthumously named Minister of Works and Palace Attendant. Luoyang’s fall followed so quickly that no posthumous title was ever conferred.
34
子韜字子遠，放佚無檢。 光為廷尉時，韜受貨賕，有司奏案之，而光不知。 時人雖非光不能防閑其子，以其用心有素，不以為累。 初，光詣長安留台，以韜兼右衛將軍。 韜與殿省小人交通，及光卒，仍於喪中往來不絕。 時東海王越輔政，不朝觀。 韜知人心有望，密與太傅參軍薑賾、京兆杜概等謀討越，事泄伏誅。
His son Tao, courtesy name Ziyuan, was dissolute and undisciplined. While Guang headed the court of justice, Tao took bribes; the authorities impeached him, yet Guang knew nothing of it. Critics faulted him for failing to rein in his son, yet his lifelong integrity spared him lasting blame. When Guang went to the provisional administration at Chang’an, he had Tao named concurrent general of the Right Guard. Tao consorted with low palace cliques and, even during mourning for his father, kept up those visits without pause. Sima Yue of Donghai was regent then and rarely appeared at formal court. Tao sensed public hatred of Yue and secretly joined Grand Tutor staffer Jiang Ze, Du Gai of Jingzhao, and others in a plot against him; when it leaked, Tao was put to death.
35
史臣曰：下士競而文，中庸靜而質，不若進不足而退有餘也。 魏舒、劉寔發慮精華，結綬登槐，覽止成務。 季和切問近對，當官正色。 詩云「貪人敗類」，豈劉夏之謂歟！
The historians write: petty men scramble for polish, the steady man keeps to plain substance—better to fall short in ambition than to overreach in holding power. Wei Shu and Liu Shi brought keen minds to the council hall beneath the locusts and saw great affairs through to completion. Li Xi answered hard questions face to face and kept a fearless mien in office. The Odes call greedy men the ruin of their kind—did they mean Liu Xia?”
36
贊曰：舒言不矜，憙對千乘。 子真、宣茂，雅志難陵。 進忠能舉，退讓攸興。 皎皎瑚器，來光玉繩。
In praise: Wei Shu spoke without swagger; Li Xi faced down the mighty. Liu Zizhen and Gao Xuanmao—noble purpose none could bend. They advanced loyalty and lifted the worthy, yielded office and let virtue flourish. Bright as ritual coral, they brought luster to the constellations of state.

皇甫謐
Huangfu Mi
2
皇甫謐，字士安，幼名靜，安定朝那人，漢太尉嵩之曾孫也。 出叔父，徙居新安。 年二十，不好學，遊蕩無度，或以為癡。 嘗得瓜果，輒進所叔母任氏。 任氏曰：「《孝經》云：『三牲之養，猶為不孝。 』汝今年餘二十，目不存教，心不入道，無以慰我。 」因歎曰：「昔孟母三徙以成仁，曾父烹豕以存教，豈我居不卜鄰，教有所闕，何爾魯鈍之甚也！ 修身篤學，自汝得之，于我何有！ 」因對之流涕。 謐乃感激，就鄉人席坦受書，勤力不怠。 居貧，躬自稼穡，帶經而農，遂博綜典籍百家之言。 沈靜寡欲，始有高尚之志，以著述為務，自號玄晏先生。 著《禮樂》、《聖真》之論。 後得風痹疾，猶手不輟卷。
Huangfu Mi, courtesy name Shi'an, childhood name Jing, came from Chaona in Anding. He was the great-grandson of Grand Commandant Huangfu Song of Han. He was given to an uncle for adoption and moved to Xin'an. At twenty he still spurned study for idle roaming—people took him for simple. Whenever he had fruit, he offered it first to his foster aunt, Lady Ren. Lady Ren told him: "The Classic of Filial Piety warns that even feeding parents the three sacrificial meats can still be unfilial." You are past twenty yet refuse instruction and ignore the Way—you bring me no comfort." She sighed: "The mothers of Mencius and Zengzi bent heaven and earth to rear their sons—have I failed to choose good neighbors or left gaps in your schooling? How can you be so thick-headed!" Master yourself and study—that achievement is yours alone, not mine!" With that she wept before him. Stirred to remorse, he studied under Xi Tan of his village without slackening. Though poor he farmed his own fields, classics tucked in his belt, until he commanded every major text and school. Withdrawn and detached, he aspired high and devoted himself to authorship, calling himself Master Xuanyan. He wrote essays on ritual and music and on sagehood and authenticity. Even after crippling wind-numbness struck, he never put down his books.

遂不仕。 耽玩典籍，忘寢與食，時人謂之「書淫」。 或有箴其過篤，將損耗精神。 謐曰：「朝聞道，夕死可矣，況命之修短分定懸天乎！」
He therefore refused office. He buried himself in texts until he forgot meals and sleep—contemporaries dubbed him a "book debauchee." Well-wishers warned that such zeal would ruin his health. He answered: "If I grasp the Way at dawn I may die at dusk content—why fret when Heaven fixes life's span?"
5
叔父有子既冠，謐年四十喪所生後母，遂還本宗。
Once his cousin came of age and Mi turned forty, he buried the mother who bore him and then rejoined his birth clan.
6
城陽太守梁柳，謐從姑子也，當之官，人勸謐餞之。 謐曰：「柳為布衣時過吾，吾送迎不出門，食不過鹽菜，貧者不以酒肉為禮。 今作郡而送之，是貴城陽太守而賤梁柳，豈中古人之道，是非吾心所安也。」
Liang Liu of Chengyang—his aunt's son—was leaving for his post; friends urged Huangfu Mi to give him a send-off banquet. Huangfu Mi refused: "When Liang Liu was a commoner I never stepped beyond my gate to greet him and meals never rose above pickles—poverty forbids wine-laden etiquette." To banquet him now as governor would honor the office and insult the man—hardly the ancient code, and not what my conscience allows."
7
時魏郡召上計掾，舉孝廉； 景元初，相國辟，皆不行。 其後鄉親勸令應命，謐為《釋勸論》以通志焉。 其辭曰：其後武帝頻下詔敦逼不已，謐上疏自稱草莽臣曰：「臣以尪弊，迷於道趣，因疾抽簪，散發林阜，人綱不閑，鳥獸為群。 陛下披榛采蘭，並收蒿艾。 是以皋陶振褐，不仁者遠。 臣惟頑蒙，備食晉粟，猶識唐人擊壤之樂，宜赴京城，稱壽闕外。 而小人無良，致災速禍，久嬰篤疾，軀半不仁，右腳偏小，十有九載。 又服寒食藥，違錯節度，辛苦荼毒，於今七年。 隆冬裸袒食冰，當暑煩悶，加以咳逆，或若溫虐，或類傷寒，浮氣流腫，四肢酸重。 於今困劣，救命呼噏，父兄見出，妻息長訣。 仰迫天威，扶輿就道，所苦加焉，不任進路，委身待罪，伏枕歎息。 臣聞《韶》《衛》不並奏，《雅》《鄭》不兼禦，故郤子入周，禍延王叔； 虞丘稱賢，樊姬掩口。 君子小人，禮不同器，況臣糠䵃，糅之彫胡？ 庸夫錦衣，不稱其服也。 竊聞同命之士，咸以畢到，唯臣疾疢，抱釁床蓐，雖貪明時，懼斃命路隅。 設臣不疾，已遭堯、舜之世，執志箕山，猶當容之。 臣聞上有明聖之主，下有輸實之臣； 上有在寬之政，下有委情之人。 唯陛下留神垂恕，更旌瑰俊，索隱于傅岩，收釣於渭濱，無令泥滓久濁清流。 」謐辭切言至，遂見聽許。
Wei commandery nominated him accounting clerk and Filial and Incorrupt; Early in Jingyuan the minister of state summoned him—he declined each. When kin pressed him to answer summons he wrote the "Discourse Releasing Persuasion" to clarify his resolve. The scrap begins "Thus he spoke," yet the chapter immediately jumps to Emperor Wu's repeated summons; Huangfu Mi memorialized as a recluse: "I am frail and obsessed with private aims; illness drove me to cast aside office and herd with beasts in the hills, ignorant of courtly ties." Yet Your Majesty winnows fine orchids from the scrub—and still gathers mugwort with them." Thus Gao Yao left his hut and the cruel fled." Though dull I eat Jin grain and still recall the Tang villagers' contentment—I should kneel at the capital and shout long life." But I am none too good; sickness struck fast—nine years half-paralyzed, my right foot shrunken." Cold Food Powder misused has poisoned me seven years." Winters I shiver naked gnawing ice; summers I suffocate with coughs—now malaria-like, now chill-like—edema and aching limbs." I am too weak to breathe; kin escort me while I bid wife and children forever." Heaven's command forces me into a litter though each league worsens my pain—I lie waiting judgment, sighing on my pillow." I know Shao and Wei music never mix; when Xi Ke entered Zhou the king suffered." Yu Qiu's praise made Lady Fan of Chu hide a smile." Nobles and commoners do not share vessels—how dare chaff like me mix with polished rice?" Silk on a peasant never fits." Others summoned have arrived; only I lie sick in bed—though I crave this enlightened reign I might die on the road." Even hale in Yao-Shun days I would hide on Mount Ji—surely allowed." When the throne is wise ministers prove earnest;" when policy is generous subjects give their hearts." Please look kindly elsewhere—seek Fu Yue and Jiang Taigong—and keep mud from fouling clear water." Huangfu Mi spoke so earnestly that the emperor let him be.
8
歲餘，又舉賢良方正，並不起。 自表就帝借書，帝送一車書與之。 謐雖羸疾，而披閱不怠。 初服寒食散，而性與之忤，每委頓不倫，嘗悲恚，叩刃欲自殺，叔母諫之而止。
A year later he was nominated Worthy, Good, and Upright—again he stayed home. He asked to borrow imperial books; the emperor sent a whole cart. Gaunt as he was, he never stopped reading. Cold Food Powder disagreed with him—fits left him wrecked; once he seized a knife until his aunt stayed his hand.
9
濟陰太守蜀人文立，表以命士有贄為煩，請絕其禮幣，詔從之。 謐聞而歎曰：「亡國之大夫不可與圖存，而以革歷代之制，其可乎！ 夫『束帛戔戔』，《易》之明義，玄纁之贄，自古之舊也。 故孔子稱夙夜強學以待問，席上之珍以待聘。 士於是乎三揖乃進，明致之難也； 一讓而退，明去之易也。 若殷湯之于伊尹，文王之于太公，或身即莘野，或就載以歸，唯恐禮之不重，豈吝其煩費哉！ 且一禮不備，貞女恥之，況命士乎！ 孔子曰：『賜也，爾愛其羊，我愛其禮。 』棄之如何？ 政之失賢，於此乎在矣。」
Wen Li of Shu, governor of Jiyin, asked to end congratulatory gifts for nominees—the court agreed. Huangfu Mi groaned: "Can ministers of a fallen state remake eternal rites?" "Bolts of silk" mark clear meaning in the Changes—dark silk gifts are ancient usage." Confucius prized scholars who refine themselves for audience and keep treasures for patrons." Three bows show how hard it is to advance;" one yielding shows how easy it is to withdraw." Tang sought Yi Yin and Wen sought Taigong in the weeds—never minding cost—lest courtesy fall short." One lapse shames a maiden—how much more a sworn scholar!" Confucius told Zigong: you spare the sheep—I defend the rite." Drop it—what then?" When government loses worthy men this is where it begins."
10
咸甯初，又詔曰：「男子皇甫謐沈靜履素，守學好古，與流俗異趣，其以謐為太子中庶子。 」謐固辭篤疾。 帝初雖不奪其志，尋復發詔徵為議郎，又召補著作郎。 司隸校尉劉毅請為功曹，並不應。 著論為葬送之制，名曰《篤終》，曰：而竟不仕。 卒，時年六十八。 子童靈、方回等遵其遺命。
Early in Xianning an edict praised Huangfu Mi's purity and love of antiquity and named him attendant to the heir. He pleaded grave illness and refused. Though the emperor first indulged him he soon summoned him counselor and compiler. Metropolitan governor Liu Yi sought him as merit clerk—still no answer. He drafted burial regulations titled "Dying with Resolve"; nevertheless he never took office. He died at sixty-eight. His sons Tongling and Fanghui honored his last wishes.
11
謐所著詩賦誄頌論難甚多，又撰《帝王世紀》、《年曆》、《高士》、《逸士》、《列女》等傳、《玄晏春秋》，並重於世。 門人摯虞、張軌、牛綜、席純，皆為晉名臣。
He left vast poetry and prose plus Records of August Ages, chronologies, collections of recluses and exemplary women, and the Xuanyan Annals—all esteemed. Students Zhi Yu, Zhang Gui, Niu Zong, and Xi Chun rose to Jin ministerial rank.

His son Huangfu Fanghui

=方回少遵父操，兼有文才。 永嘉初，博士征，不起。 避亂荊州，閉戶閒居，未嘗入城府。 蠶而後衣，耕而後食，先人後己，尊賢愛物，南土人士咸崇敬之。 刺史陶侃禮之甚厚。 侃每造之，著素士服，望門輒下而進。 王敦遣從弟暠代侃，遷侃為廣州。 侃將詣敦，方回諫曰：「吾聞敵國滅，功臣亡。 足下新破杜弢，功莫與二，欲無危，其可得乎！ 」侃不從而行。 敦果欲殺侃，賴周訪獲免。 暠既至荊州，大失物情，百姓叛暠迎杜弢。 暠大行誅戮以立威，以方回為侃所敬，責其不來詣己，乃收而斬之。 荊土華夷，莫不流涕。
Huangfu Fanghui kept his father's discipline and showed literary gifts. When Yongjia opened he ignored summons to the erudite academy. He fled turmoil to Jingzhou, shut his gate, and never entered the yamen. He wore silk only after raising silkworms and ate after harvest—others before himself—and southland scholars revered him. Governor Tao Kan honored him richly. Tao Kan always wore plain clothes and dismounted before Fanghui's gate. Wang Dun replaced Tao Kan with cousin Wang Hao and moved Kan to Guangzhou. When Tao Kan planned to visit Wang Dun, Fanghui warned: conquerors soon kill their champions." You just crushed Du Tao unmatched—how can you stay safe?" Tao Kan went anyway. Wang Dun nearly executed him—Zhou Fang saved him. Wang Hao alienated Jingzhou until commoners welcomed Du Tao back. Wang Hao slaughtered widely for terror; resenting Fanghui's prestige with Tao Kan, he arrested and executed him. Chinese and natives of Jing wept together.

摯虞
Zhi Yu
16
摯虞，字仲洽，京兆長安人也。 父模，魏太僕卿。 虞少事皇甫謐，才學通博，著述不倦。 郡檄主簿。 虞嘗以死生有命，富貴在天。 天之所祐者義也，人之所助者信也。 履信思順，所以延福，違此而行，所以速禍。 然道長世短，禍福舛錯，怵迫之徒，不知所守，蕩而積憤，或迷或放。 故借之以身，假之以事，先陳處世不遇之難，遂棄彝倫，輕舉遠遊，以極常人罔惑之情，而後引之以正，反之以義，推神明之應於視聽之表，崇否泰之運于智力之外，以明天任命之不可違，故作《思遊賦》。 其辭曰：舉賢良，與夏侯湛等十七人策為下第，拜中郎。 武帝詔曰：「省諸賢良答策，雖所言殊塗，皆明于王義，有益政道。 欲詳覽其對，究觀賢士大夫用心。 」因詔諸賢良方正直言，會東堂策問，曰：「頃日食正陽，水旱為災，將何所修，以變大眚？ 及法令有不宜於今，為公私所患苦者，皆何事？ 凡平世在於得才，得才者亦借耳目以聽察。 若有文武器能有益於時務而未見申敘者，各舉其人。 及有負俗謗議，宜先洗濯者，亦各言之。 」虞對曰：「臣聞古之聖明，原始以要終，體本以正末。 故憂法度之不當，而不憂人物之失所； 憂人物之失所，而不憂災害之流行。 誠以法得於此，則物理於彼； 人和於下，則災消於上。 其有日月之眚，水旱之災，則反聽內視，求其所由，遠觀諸物，近驗諸身。 耳目聽察，豈或有蔽其聰明者乎？ 動心出令，豈或有傾其常正者乎？ 大官大職，豈或有授非其人者乎？ 賞罰黜陟，豈或有不得其所者乎？ 河濱山岩，豈或有懷道釣築而未感於夢兆者乎？ 方外遐裔，豈或有命世傑出而未蒙膏澤者乎？ 推此類也，以求其故，詢事考言，以盡其實，則天人之情可得而見，咎征之至可得而救也。 若推之於物則無忤，求之於身則無尤，萬物理順，內外咸宜，祝史正辭，言不負誠，而日月錯行，夭癘不戒，此則陰陽之事，非吉凶所在也。 期運度數，自然之分，固非人事所能供禦，其亦振廩散滯，貶食省用而已矣。 是故誠遇期運，則雖陶唐、殷湯有所不變； 苟非期運，則宋、衛之君，諸侯之相，猶能有感。 唯陛下審其所由，以盡其理，則天下幸甚。 臣生長蓽門，不逮異物，雖有賢才，所未接識，不敢瞽言妄舉，無以疇答聖問。 」擢為太子舍人，除聞喜令。
Zhi Yu, courtesy name Zhongqia, came from Chang'an in the capital district. His father Zhi Mo served Wei as Minister Coachman. Youth study under Huangfu Mi gave him encyclopedic learning and tireless authorship. The commandery drafted him chief clerk. He held that life and death are fated and wealth heaven-sent. Heaven favors the dutiful; men help the trustworthy." Faith and docility prolong fortune; defiance hastens ruin." Yet life is brief and fate tangled—anxious souls lose anchor and drift or rage." So he framed the "Rhapsody on Pondering Roaming": feign exile and wandering to mirror mortal confusion, then guide readers back to duty, proving Heaven's decree beyond wit. The opening "Thus he spoke" collides with office history: nominated Worthy and Good with Xia Hou Zhan and sixteen others he scored lowest yet became gentleman cadet. Emperor Wu replied: "Though answers differed, each clarified royal doctrine for governance." I wish to study each answer and weigh scholars' intent. He therefore summoned all candidates rated worthy, upright, and candid to gather in the Eastern Hall for the examination and asked: "Of late the sun was darkened at midday, and flood and drought have struck. What should we reform to turn aside this great scourge?" What statutes ill suit our age and vex both the state and the people?" Ordering the realm rests on winning good men, and winning good men depends on opening one's ears and eyes to what is heard and seen." If anyone possesses civil or military gifts that would serve the times but has not yet been properly recommended, each of you should put that person forward." And if there are men weighed down by popular scandal who deserve to be vindicated first, speak for them as well." Yu answered: "I have heard that the sage kings of old searched origins to understand outcomes and held fast to the root to set the branches straight." They therefore fretted first when institutions went awry, not merely when individuals were misplaced; they cared when talent was misassigned before they blamed Heaven for calamities." For when right rule holds here, order follows there; when the folk live in harmony below, calamities cease above." Whenever eclipses appeared or floods and droughts struck, they listened inwardly, examined themselves, traced origins, observed things afar, and tested truth at hand." Could anything be clouding clear perception in what we hear and see?" Could anything be upsetting steadiness and justice in our intentions and edicts?" Could any high office have gone to the wrong person?" Could rewards and penalties, promotions and demotions, ever miss their mark?" Along rivers and in mountain retreats, might gifted men still hidden—like those who fished or hewed stone—have failed to appear even in portents?" In the farthest corners of the realm, might brilliant men who could define their generation still lack the dew of royal favor?" Press such inquiries to learn why things happen; weigh deeds and words until the truth is plain—then the bond between Heaven and humanity becomes visible, and threatening signs can be answered." If the realm answers scrutiny without contradiction and the ruler's conscience is clear—if all runs smoothly within and without and ritual language stays honest—yet the luminaries wander and plague strikes without warning, that belongs to cosmic imbalance, not to moral reward or blame." The turning of fate and the count of nature lie beyond human power; we can only open the granaries, ease shortages, tighten rations, and trim expenses." When destiny truly runs its course, not even rulers like Yao and Tang could alter it; if it is not fixed fate, even lesser lords and regional ministers can still sway Heaven's response." If Your Majesty weighs those causes and follows them through, the world will be profoundly blessed." I was raised in humble quarters and have met few extraordinary men; though talent exists, I know none of it at first hand, and I dare not babble names at random—I cannot adequately answer Your Majesty." He was advanced to gentleman attendant to the heir apparent and given the magistracy of Wenxi.

The emperor was then devoted to sound rule; Wu had just been subdued and the empire was calm. Yu submitted the "Hymn of Great Concord" to celebrate Jin's virtue. The text begins:

O glorious primeval age, cradle of human life." Every quarter had its home; myriad kingdoms rolled on one axle." Han lost its strength; ruin reigned without rule." The heartland's ties frayed beyond the capital; the feudal bulwarks crumbled within." Heaven's scourge had fallen; the age knew deepest woe." Hosts clashed like dragons and beasts; the realm shattered into rival states." Liu Bei seized the throne in Shu; Gongsun Du defied the court beyond the eastern sea." Sun Quan seized the opening and held the lands between the Three Rivers." Radiant God on high, whose awe-filled gaze rests on the world below." Then royal majesty blazed forth, executing Heaven's judgments." War flared at Liaosui and the culprits were seized." Korea was brought to peace; Han and Mo were swept into submission." 且夫進無險懼，而惟寂之務者，率其性也。 兩可俱是，而舍彼趣此者，從其志也。 蓋無為可以解天下之紛，澹泊可以救國家之急，當位者事有所窮，陳策者言有不入，翟璜不能回西鄰之寇，平、勃不能正如意之立，幹木臥而秦師退，四皓起而戚姬泣。 夫如是何舍何執，何去何就？ 謂山岑之林為芳，穀底之莽為臭。 守分任性，唯天所授，鳥不假甲於龜，魚不借足於獸，何必笑孤竹之貧而羨齊景之富！ 恥布衣以肆志，甯文裘而拖繡。 且能約其躬，則儋石之畜以豐； 苟肆其欲，則海陵之積不足； 存道德者，則匹夫之身可榮； 忘大倫者，則萬乘之主猶辱。 將研六籍以訓世，守寂泊以鎮俗，偶鄭老於海隅，匹嚴叟於僻蜀。 且世乙太虛為輿，玄爐為肆，神游莫競之林，心存無營之室，榮利不擾其覺，殷憂不幹其寐，捐誇者之所貪，收躁務之所棄，雉聖籍之荒蕪，總群言之一至。 全素履於丘園，背纓緌而長逸，請子課吾業於千載，無聽吾言於今日也。」
Besides, promotion carries no mortal dread; choosing quietism follows innate disposition." When either path may be justified, yet someone forsakes one for the other, individual intent decides." Stillness untangles worldly knots; detachment eases dynastic peril—those enthroned reach the end of their means; memorials sometimes fail to persuade; Zhai Huang could not stop western invasions; Chen Ping and Zhou Bo could not secure Prince Ruyi; Duan Ganmu's mere posture routed Qin troops; the Four Elders rose and Consort Qi wept." Under such conditions what is worth grasping or releasing, fleeing or embracing?" You label upland groves sweet and lowland thickets stench." Hold your station and obey innate bent—Heaven grants each nature; birds never steal tortoise shells for feathers nor fish hooves from beasts—so why ridicule poor Guzhu yet crave Qi Jing's riches!" They blush at hemp robes yet chase ambition with silken pride." Master restraint and even modest savings feel lavish; indulge appetite and even oceanic granaries cannot satisfy. Long ago, Bu Zhun of Ji Commandery looted the burial mound of King Xiang of Wei—or, some claimed, of King An of Li—and hauled out bamboo slips by the cartload. The Bamboo Annals, thirteen scrolls, runs from the Xia age to King You’s fall to the Quanrong; it picks up the thread through the three houses’ partition of Jin and carries Wei’s story to the twentieth year of King An’s reign. Broadly, Wei’s chronicle and the Spring and Autumn Annals align on many points. Where text and gloss part company sharply, it asserts Xia’s calendar ran longer than Shang’s; Yi wrested the throne from Qi; Qi slew him; Tai Jia put Yi Yin to death; King Wen Ding executed Ji Li; From Zhou’s receipt of Heaven’s charge to King Mu spans a century—not that Mu himself lived a hundred years; When King You fell, Elder He of Gong held the royal interim—not the dual stewardship miscalled ‘Joint Harmony.’ Its two-scroll Changes parallels the Zhouyi’s upper and lower wings. The pair titled Yi Yaoyin Yang Hexagrams roughly tracks the Zhouyi while its line texts diverge. The piece called Hexagram-subordinate Changes looks like the Shuogua yet departs from it. Two scrolls of Gong Sun Duan record his debate on the Changes with Shao Zhi. Three scrolls of Discourses of the States treat Chu and Jin. Three scrolls titled Names echo the Record of Rites and recall the Erya and Analects. Shi Chun, one scroll, collects Zuo-style oracle readings; the title likely memorializes its author. Eleven scrolls of Suoyu gather omens, dreams, and fate lore from many lands. Liangqiu Zang opens with Wei’s generations, then turns to treasure buried in the mound. Two scrolls of Jiaoshu expound the trailing-line hunt. Sheng Feng catalogs imperial enfeoffments. Two scrolls of Great Calendar belong to the astronomizing school of Zou Yan. Five scrolls of Mu Tianzi Zhuan follow King Mu across the seas to the Emperor’s terrace and Xiwangmu’s realm. Tu Shi is of the picture-caption kind. Nineteen miscellanies follow—field law, Zhou documents, Chu affairs, and the death of King Mu’s favorite Sheng Ji. Seventy-five titles in all; seven slip-bundles snapped beyond reading, names lost. They also pulled a bronze blade two feet five inches from the mound. Every character was lacquered tadpole script. Looters torched bamboo for light; when the state salvaged the haul, char and breaks left the text unsalvageable for collation. The emperor entrusted the bundle to the secretariat for restoration, sense-finding, and conversion into current graphs. As editor, Xi studied the slips firsthand, resolving doubts with argued readings. He rose to Gentleman of the Masters of Writing.

The emperor quizzed Zhi Yu on ‘winding water’ on day three. Yu’s tale: during Han Zhangdi, Xu Zhao of Pingyuan’s triple birth on the double-third ended in triple death; villagers drew kin to the bank for ablution, then cups rode the current—that, he said, was the origin. The throne answered: If your story is true, the custom began in grief, not joy. Xi interjected: The lad lacks breadth—I will explain. When the Duke of Zhou finished Luoyi he let cups ride the current—hence the stray verse, ‘feathered cups on the wave.’ King Zhao of Qin feasted at the river bend on day three and saw a golden figure offer a sword from midstream: ‘You shall master Western Xia.’ Thus he rose above the lords—and founded the winding-water gathering. Both Han courts continued it as grand festivals. The sovereign delighted and weighed out fifty jin of gold for Xi.

Wang Jie
47
王接，字祖遊，河東猗氏人，漢京兆尹尊十世孫也。 父蔚，世修儒史之學。 魏中領軍曹羲作《至公論》，蔚善之，而著《至機論》，辭義甚美。 官至夏陽侯相。 接幼喪父，哀毀過禮，鄉親皆歎曰：「王氏有子哉！ 」渤海劉原為河東太守，好奇，以旌才為務。 同郡馮收試經為郎，七十餘，薦接于原曰：「夫驊騮不總轡，則非造父之肆； 明月不流光，則非隋侯之掌。 伏惟明府苞黃中之德，耀重離之明，求賢與能，小無遺錯，是以鄙老思獻所知。 竊見處士王接，岐嶷俊異，十三而孤，居喪盡禮，學過目而知，義觸類而長，斯玉鉉之妙味，經世之徽猷也。 不患玄黎之不啟，竊樂春英之及時。 」原即禮命，接不受。 原乃呼見曰：「君欲慕肥遁之高邪？ 」對曰：「接薄祜，少孤而無兄弟，母老疾篤，故無心為吏。 」及母終，柴毀骨立，居墓次積年，備覽眾書，多出異義。 性簡率，不修俗操，鄉里大族多不能善之，唯裴頠雅知焉。 平陽太守柳澹、散騎侍郎裴遐、尚書僕射鄧攸皆與接友善。 後為郡主簿，迎太守溫宇，宇奇之，轉功曹史。 州辟部平陽從事。 時泰山羊亮為平陽太守，薦之于司隸校尉王堪，出補都官從事。
Wang Jie, styled Zuyou, from Yishi in Hedong—the tenth generation down from Wang Zun, Han’s capital intendant. His father Wei sustained generations of classical and historical learning. Palace Army commander Cao Xi’s Utmost Impartiality impressed Wei, who answered with Utmost Pivot—language of rare polish. He rose to magistrate for the Marquis of Xiayang. Orphaned young, Jie mourned past the rites; neighbors murmured, The Wang line has an heir. Liu Yuan of Bohai, as Hedong governor, courted wonder and hunted talent. Co-provincial Feng Shou, a classics-tested gentleman past seventy, urged Yuan: ‘Unbitted bays never reach Zaofu’s yard; Moonlight unfelt never rests in Sui Hou’s hand.’ He trusts Your Honor’s centered virtue and luminous clarity; you miss no gifted soul—so this old man offers a name. The witness: young Jie, brilliant, bereaved at thirteen yet ritually perfect—reads once and grasps, infers wide—the savor of the sacred tripods, policy for an era. I do not fret that night grain fails to sprout—I rejoice spring blooms on time. Yuan dispatched formal invitation; Jie declined. Yuan summoned him: Do you chase Fei’s lofty withdrawal? Jie answered: Little blessing left me fatherless and brotherless with a dying mother—office holds no lure. When she passed he withered beside her mound for years, reading widely and arguing fresh glosses. Plain-spoken, indifferent to fashion, grand families spurned him—only Pei Yi saw his worth. Governor Liu Dan of Pingyang, attendant Pei Xia, and minister Deng You counted him friend. He served as county registrar, impressed incoming Governor Wen Yu, and became merit clerk. The province appointed him Pingyang staff officer. Taishan’s Yang Liang, then Pingyang governor, pushed Jie to Wang Kan, who gave him a capital-affairs post.

The editors: Huangfu Mi kept plain virtue in quiet withdrawal—ink for illness, joy in tombs—unswayed by rank or poverty: a Jin-era recluse among recluses. His ‘Earnest Endings’ essay favors humble graves—scorning Ji excess yet avoiding Yang’s nihilism—seeing clear through mortality’s door. Zhi Yu and Shu Xi among others mastered the archives and classical precedent; their counsel reads well and their prose glows—they rank among the truly learned. Some staffed the imperial library on provisional assignment and shaped policy memorials. Others oversaw the ministry of rites and helped fix suburban offerings. Yu suffered for holding fast to duty; Xi died before his talents ripened—how strangely Heaven rewards men. Wang Jie's gifts dazzled connoisseurs, yet heaven clipped him before his stride—what waste.

Encomium: Huangfu Mi sought quiet and dwelt among humble lanes. He lavished care on literature and ignored titles. His burial essays deserve praise; Cold Food elixirs betrayed his health. Zhi Yu ranged wide; Shu Guangwei stood apart. They shaped ritual law and edited stray manuscripts. Archives from Wei gained sequence; Han slips found their place. Wang Zuyou rose afterward—and still perfumes later ages.

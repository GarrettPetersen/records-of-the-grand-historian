皇甫謐
Huangfu Mi
2
皇甫謐，字士安，幼名靜，安定朝那人，漢太尉嵩之曾孫也。 出叔父，徙居新安。 年二十，不好學，遊蕩無度，或以為癡。 嘗得瓜果，輒進所叔母任氏。 任氏曰：「《孝經》云：『三牲之養，猶為不孝。 』汝今年餘二十，目不存教，心不入道，無以慰我。 」因歎曰：「昔孟母三徙以成仁，曾父烹豕以存教，豈我居不卜鄰，教有所闕，何爾魯鈍之甚也！ 修身篤學，自汝得之，于我何有！ 」因對之流涕。 謐乃感激，就鄉人席坦受書，勤力不怠。 居貧，躬自稼穡，帶經而農，遂博綜典籍百家之言。 沈靜寡欲，始有高尚之志，以著述為務，自號玄晏先生。 著《禮樂》、《聖真》之論。 後得風痹疾，猶手不輟卷。
Huangfu Mi, style Shi’an, childhood name Jing, came from Chaona in Anding commandery. He was a great-grandson of the Han grand commandant Huangfu Song. He was given in adoption to an uncle and moved with that branch to Xin’an. At twenty he still shunned study, idled without restraint, and people took him for a simpleton. Whenever he had fruit, he brought it first to his aunt by adoption, Lady Ren. Lady Ren said, “The *Classic of Filial Piety* warns that even offering parents the three sacrificial meats can still be unfilial.” You are past twenty yet pay no mind to instruction and give no thought to the Way—you cannot comfort me so.” She sighed, “Mencius’ mother moved three times to find a good neighborhood; Zengzi’s father kept a promise to butcher a pig for the boy’s lesson. Have I failed to choose neighbors wisely, or left some gap in your upbringing, that you remain so thick-witted?” Moral self-culture and real learning are yours to win or lose—they are not mine to give!” With that she wept before him. Stricken with remorse, he went to the local scholar Xi Tan to study and worked without slackening. Poor, he worked his own fields with a copy of the classics tucked in his belt until he had mastered the canon and the philosophers. Calm and detached, he resolved on a scholar’s life, devoted himself to authorship, and took the sobriquet Master Xuanyan. He wrote essays titled *Ritual and Music* and *Sagely Truth*. Later he fell ill with wind paralysis, yet never set his scrolls aside.

叔父有子既冠，謐年四十喪所生後母，遂還本宗。
When his uncle’s son came of age, Mi at forty buried the stepmother who had reared him and rejoined his birth father’s line.
6
城陽太守梁柳，謐從姑子也，當之官，人勸謐餞之。 謐曰：「柳為布衣時過吾，吾送迎不出門，食不過鹽菜，貧者不以酒肉為禮。 今作郡而送之，是貴城陽太守而賤梁柳，豈中古人之道，是非吾心所安也。」
Liang Liu of Chengyang, his cousin on the distaff side, was leaving for a post; friends pressed Mi to host a parting banquet. Mi said, "When Liang Liu was still a commoner I never stepped past my gate to escort him, and we dined on pickled greens alone—the poor do not toast with meat and wine." To fete him now that he wears a governor’s seal would honor the office and slight the man—hardly the ancient way, and not what my conscience allows."
7
時魏郡召上計掾，舉孝廉； 景元初，相國辟，皆不行。 其後鄉親勸令應命，謐為《釋勸論》以通志焉。 其辭曰：其後武帝頻下詔敦逼不已，謐上疏自稱草莽臣曰：「臣以尪弊，迷於道趣，因疾抽簪，散發林阜，人綱不閑，鳥獸為群。 陛下披榛采蘭，並收蒿艾。 是以皋陶振褐，不仁者遠。 臣惟頑蒙，備食晉粟，猶識唐人擊壤之樂，宜赴京城，稱壽闕外。 而小人無良，致災速禍，久嬰篤疾，軀半不仁，右腳偏小，十有九載。 又服寒食藥，違錯節度，辛苦荼毒，於今七年。 隆冬裸袒食冰，當暑煩悶，加以咳逆，或若溫虐，或類傷寒，浮氣流腫，四肢酸重。 於今困劣，救命呼噏，父兄見出，妻息長訣。 仰迫天威，扶輿就道，所苦加焉，不任進路，委身待罪，伏枕歎息。 臣聞《韶》《衛》不並奏，《雅》《鄭》不兼禦，故郤子入周，禍延王叔； 虞丘稱賢，樊姬掩口。 君子小人，禮不同器，況臣糠䵃，糅之彫胡？ 庸夫錦衣，不稱其服也。 竊聞同命之士，咸以畢到，唯臣疾疢，抱釁床蓐，雖貪明時，懼斃命路隅。 設臣不疾，已遭堯、舜之世，執志箕山，猶當容之。 臣聞上有明聖之主，下有輸實之臣； 上有在寬之政，下有委情之人。 唯陛下留神垂恕，更旌瑰俊，索隱于傅岩，收釣於渭濱，無令泥滓久濁清流。 」謐辭切言至，遂見聽許。
Wei commandery summoned him as chief clerk for the capital report; Early in the Jingyuan era the minister of state summoned him again—he still refused. When neighbors pressed him to serve, he set out his resolve in *Releasing Persuasion*. The piece opens with a flourish, then turns to the emperor’s repeated summons: Mi styled himself a "weed-country subject," pleading that frailty had driven him to the hills among beasts, far from human ties. "Your Majesty," he wrote, "sifts the wilds for talent and gathers sage and commoner alike." "So Gao Yao once shook out his rustic cloak and the wicked fled." "Though a dullard fed at the Jin court, I still remember the simple joy of the ancients—I should rush to the capital to cheer your reign." "Yet vice has brought calamity: I have lain gravely ill for years, half my body numb, my right foot shrunken, for the better part of two decades." "Cold-food elixirs, wrongly dosed, have tormented me seven years." "In winter I shivered naked on ice; in summer I gasped with fever, cough, chills like malaria or typhoid, limbs swollen and leaden." "Now I can only cry for breath while kin stand by for my last breath and my wife and children take their leave." "Forced by imperial majesty onto a litter, I grow worse by the mile; I lay down my body and await judgment, sighing on my pillow." "As fine music cannot mix with vulgar strains, so when a flawed man entered Zhou the king suffered—" "Virtue and vice do not share one vessel, as when Fan Ji silenced her lord’s praise of a pretty face." "Noble and base cannot share one bowl—how much less this chaff with your fine grain?" A peasant in silk never fits the cloth. "Others summoned with me have reached the capital; I alone lie sick in bed, fearing I would die on the road if I tried." "Even in a Yao-Shun age a man set on Mount Ji’s reclusion would be indulged—how much more I?" "Where the throne is wise, ministers speak plain truth;" "where policy is gentle, subjects may bare their hearts." "I beg you to forgive me, seek worthies at Fu Yan and the Wei River, and keep the muddy from fouling the clear current." The throne granted his plea when his memorial struck home.
8
歲餘，又舉賢良方正，並不起。 自表就帝借書，帝送一車書與之。 謐雖羸疾，而披閱不怠。 初服寒食散，而性與之忤，每委頓不倫，嘗悲恚，叩刃欲自殺，叔母諫之而止。
A year later the court nominated him as worthy and upright; again he stayed home. He asked the emperor for books and received a whole cartload from the palace. Wasted by sickness, he never stopped reading. Cold-food powders clashed with his constitution and left him in spasms; once, in despair, he drew a knife to end his life until his aunt stayed his hand.
9
濟陰太守蜀人文立，表以命士有贄為煩，請絕其禮幣，詔從之。 謐聞而歎曰：「亡國之大夫不可與圖存，而以革歷代之制，其可乎！ 夫『束帛戔戔』，《易》之明義，玄纁之贄，自古之舊也。 故孔子稱夙夜強學以待問，席上之珍以待聘。 士於是乎三揖乃進，明致之難也； 一讓而退，明去之易也。 若殷湯之于伊尹，文王之于太公，或身即莘野，或就載以歸，唯恐禮之不重，豈吝其煩費哉！ 且一禮不備，貞女恥之，況命士乎！ 孔子曰：『賜也，爾愛其羊，我愛其禮。 』棄之如何？ 政之失賢，於此乎在矣。」
Wen Li, governor of Jiyin, asked to end the silk gifts required of summoned scholars, and the throne agreed. Huangfu Mi groaned, "A minister of a fallen state should not redraw the rites of every age!" "The *Book of Changes* sanctifies modest gifts of silk; dark and light silks have always opened the way to office." Confucius praised scholars who study nightly for the questions a lord will ask and keep their talents ready like treasures on the mat. Hence the triple bow before entering—accepting office is meant to be hard. One courteous refusal and he may withdraw—leaving should be easy. Tang met Yi Yin in the fields; Wen carried Lü Shang home in his cart—they worried only that the rites were not lavish enough. A chaste girl blushes if one rite is missing—how much more a gentleman summoned to court! Confucius told Zigong, "You would save the sheep; I would save the ritual." To abolish such gifts—what then? That is how a state loses its best men."
10
咸甯初，又詔曰：「男子皇甫謐沈靜履素，守學好古，與流俗異趣，其以謐為太子中庶子。 」謐固辭篤疾。 帝初雖不奪其志，尋復發詔徵為議郎，又召補著作郎。 司隸校尉劉毅請為功曹，並不應。 著論為葬送之制，名曰《篤終》，曰：而竟不仕。 卒，時年六十八。 子童靈、方回等遵其遺命。
Early in Xianning the court named him junior mentor to the crown prince, praising his learning and distance from vulgar fashion. He pleaded grave illness and refused. The emperor yielded at first, then summoned him as gentleman consultant and later as palace compiler. Liu Yi, metropolitan commandant, wanted him as merit clerk—again he stayed silent. He wrote *Steadfast to the End* on simple burial, yet never took office. He died at sixty-eight. His sons Tongling and Fanghui carried out his last wishes.
11
謐所著詩賦誄頌論難甚多，又撰《帝王世紀》、《年曆》、《高士》、《逸士》、《列女》等傳、《玄晏春秋》，並重於世。 門人摯虞、張軌、牛綜、席純，皆為晉名臣。
He left a large body of poetry and prose plus *Records of Emperors*, chronologies, accounts of recluses and heroines, and *Master Xuanyan’s Annals*, all esteemed in his time. His pupils Zhi Yu, Zhang Gui, Niu Zong, and Xi Chun rose to high office under Jin.

=方回少遵父操，兼有文才。 永嘉初，博士征，不起。 避亂荊州，閉戶閒居，未嘗入城府。 蠶而後衣，耕而後食，先人後己，尊賢愛物，南土人士咸崇敬之。 刺史陶侃禮之甚厚。 侃每造之，著素士服，望門輒下而進。 王敦遣從弟暠代侃，遷侃為廣州。 侃將詣敦，方回諫曰：「吾聞敵國滅，功臣亡。 足下新破杜弢，功莫與二，欲無危，其可得乎！ 」侃不從而行。 敦果欲殺侃，賴周訪獲免。 暠既至荊州，大失物情，百姓叛暠迎杜弢。 暠大行誅戮以立威，以方回為侃所敬，責其不來詣己，乃收而斬之。 荊土華夷，莫不流涕。
Huangfu Fanghui, in youth, followed his father’s way and showed literary gifts. When Yongjia opened he was summoned as erudit but would not go. He fled the wars to Jingzhou, kept his door shut, and never entered the yamen. He clothed himself after the silkworms, ate after the harvest, put others first, honored the worthy, and won the south’s respect. Governor Tao Kan honored him lavishly. Tao Kan always donned plain robes and dismounted at his gate out of respect. Wang Dun sent his cousin Wang Hao to replace Tao Kan and moved Kan to Guangzhou. When Tao Kan prepared to answer Wang Dun’s summons, Fanghui warned, "They say that once the foe is gone the strong general is next." "You have just crushed Du Tao and outshine every rival—do you imagine you can stay safe?" Tao Kan went anyway. Wang Dun did try to kill him; Zhou Fang saved him. Wang Hao so mishandled Jingzhou that the people rose for Du Tao against him. Wang Hao butchered people to assert authority; because Fanghui was honored by Tao Kan, Hao executed him for refusing a personal call. Chinese and non-Chinese alike in Jingzhou wept for him.

摯虞
Zhi Yu
16
摯虞，字仲洽，京兆長安人也。 父模，魏太僕卿。 虞少事皇甫謐，才學通博，著述不倦。 郡檄主簿。 虞嘗以死生有命，富貴在天。 天之所祐者義也，人之所助者信也。 履信思順，所以延福，違此而行，所以速禍。 然道長世短，禍福舛錯，怵迫之徒，不知所守，蕩而積憤，或迷或放。 故借之以身，假之以事，先陳處世不遇之難，遂棄彝倫，輕舉遠遊，以極常人罔惑之情，而後引之以正，反之以義，推神明之應於視聽之表，崇否泰之運于智力之外，以明天任命之不可違，故作《思遊賦》。 其辭曰：舉賢良，與夏侯湛等十七人策為下第，拜中郎。 武帝詔曰：「省諸賢良答策，雖所言殊塗，皆明于王義，有益政道。 欲詳覽其對，究觀賢士大夫用心。 」因詔諸賢良方正直言，會東堂策問，曰：「頃日食正陽，水旱為災，將何所修，以變大眚？ 及法令有不宜於今，為公私所患苦者，皆何事？ 凡平世在於得才，得才者亦借耳目以聽察。 若有文武器能有益於時務而未見申敘者，各舉其人。 及有負俗謗議，宜先洗濯者，亦各言之。 」虞對曰：「臣聞古之聖明，原始以要終，體本以正末。 故憂法度之不當，而不憂人物之失所； 憂人物之失所，而不憂災害之流行。 誠以法得於此，則物理於彼； 人和於下，則災消於上。 其有日月之眚，水旱之災，則反聽內視，求其所由，遠觀諸物，近驗諸身。 耳目聽察，豈或有蔽其聰明者乎？ 動心出令，豈或有傾其常正者乎？ 大官大職，豈或有授非其人者乎？ 賞罰黜陟，豈或有不得其所者乎？ 河濱山岩，豈或有懷道釣築而未感於夢兆者乎？ 方外遐裔，豈或有命世傑出而未蒙膏澤者乎？ 推此類也，以求其故，詢事考言，以盡其實，則天人之情可得而見，咎征之至可得而救也。 若推之於物則無忤，求之於身則無尤，萬物理順，內外咸宜，祝史正辭，言不負誠，而日月錯行，夭癘不戒，此則陰陽之事，非吉凶所在也。 期運度數，自然之分，固非人事所能供禦，其亦振廩散滯，貶食省用而已矣。 是故誠遇期運，則雖陶唐、殷湯有所不變； 苟非期運，則宋、衛之君，諸侯之相，猶能有感。 唯陛下審其所由，以盡其理，則天下幸甚。 臣生長蓽門，不逮異物，雖有賢才，所未接識，不敢瞽言妄舉，無以疇答聖問。 」擢為太子舍人，除聞喜令。
Zhi Yu, style Zhongqia, came from Chang’an in Jingzhao commandery. His father Zhi Mo had been Wei’s Grand Coachman. He studied under Huangfu Mi, mastered wide learning, and wrote constantly. The commandery appointed him chief clerk. He held that life and death are fated, wealth and rank lie with Heaven. Heaven favors the righteous; men aid the trustworthy. Faith and humility prolong good fortune; their opposite hastens disaster. Yet life is short and the Way long; fortune twists; frightened men lose their bearings and swing between despair and recklessness. So in *Rhapsody on Pondering Roaming* he first paints worldly frustration and escapist fantasy, then turns the reader back to moral duty and Heaven’s decree. The preface leads into his examination: nominated with Xiahou Zhan and seventeen others, he scored lowest on the policy test yet was made a gentleman of the palace. Emperor Wu observed that every candidate’s policy essay, though different, clarified royal principle and helped government. He meant to study each answer closely to see how these men thought. He then summoned every candidate honored as worthy, upright, and outspoken, brought them together in the Eastern Hall for a policy debate, and asked: "A solar eclipse has lately struck at high noon, and floods and droughts afflict the realm. What must we set right in ourselves and in government to turn aside this grave reproof from Heaven?" Which laws and regulations no longer fit the times, and which policies now torment the state and the people alike? Name them plainly. In any well-ordered age, the decisive thing is to secure the right men; and the ruler who would secure them must lend his ears and eyes wide to what is heard and seen below. If you know men of civil or military gifts who could serve the needs of the hour but have never been brought forward or given a fair hearing, each of you should name them now. If there are men smeared by rumor and held in public contempt who deserve to be cleared and restored, speak for them as well. Yu answered: "The sage-kings of old read the beginning in the end and took their stand on the root so that every twig might grow straight." They fretted first whether the laws were sound, not whether the wrong faces sat in the wrong chairs; they worried whether the right men held the right posts, not whether flood or drought had already broken loose. For when institutions are set right here, the natural order of affairs falls into line there; when those below live in concord, Heaven’s omens of wrath melt away above. Should eclipses darken the luminaries or the seasons bring flood and drought, the wise ruler bends his ear inward, searches his own heart for the cause, scans the world at large, and tests every suspicion against his own conduct. Might something be muffling what he hears or dimming what he sees? Might his passions or his edicts be tilting him off the steady middle course? Have high posts gone to men unfit to bear them? Do rewards lift the deserving, and do punishments fall on the guilty, or has justice wandered from its mark? Along rivers and among crags, are there hermits of true talent, like the old worthies who fished the Wei or built in the wilderness, still waiting for the dream that calls a king to seek them? Beyond the pale, in the farthest marches, are there born leaders of the age who have never tasted the court’s favoring rain? Push the inquiry along such lines, trace each ill to its source, weigh deeds against words until the truth stands bare, and the bond between Heaven and humanity will disclose itself; even grave portents may then be turned aside. If nothing in the realm resists your rule, nothing in your breast accuses you, the world runs true within and without, and your prayers to the spirits are honest—yet the lights of heaven still wander and plague stalks the people—then you are watching the turning of cosmic breath, not the ledger of moral fortune and ruin. The span allotted by fate and the reckoning of seasons belong to nature’s own arithmetic; no government can purchase immunity. At most a ruler may open the public granaries, ease hoarded distress, tighten his table, and shave the budget—that is the whole of it. When Heaven truly fixes a season of trial, not even the perfection of Yao or the virtue of Tang could have altered its course; when the times are not locked in such a doom, even middling rulers like those of Song and Wei, or ordinary feudal ministers, could move Heaven by their conduct. If Your Majesty will trace each sign to its root and think its logic through to the end, the empire will be the richer for it. I sprang from a poor lane and never moved in circles where genius shows itself; however many able men the realm may hold, I have not shaken their hands or heard their names. I would not play the blind man naming colors, nor hazard empty nominations, and so I cannot line up answers worthy of your questions. The court raised him to gentleman attendant at the heir’s palace and gave him the magistracy of Wenxi.

Erudite as he was, Wang Jie's deepest expertise lay in the ritual canon and its exegetical traditions. He held that the Zuo narrative is self-sufficient literature rather than a mere gloss on the Spring and Autumn. Gongyang, by contrast, hews to the classic: it never invents episodes the classic omits—spare in diction yet superior for unlocking the core text. He Xiu of Rencheng is exhaustive yet demotes Zhou and elevates Lu, skewing the larger scheme; even while mastering Gongyang he often reproduces its faults. Wang Jie therefore re-annotated the Gongyang edition, offering many fresh readings. While secretariat aide Wei Heng was still collating the tomb texts, he perished in the violence. Assistant director Shu Xi finished the project, often adjudicating disputed passages. Wang Tingjian of Chenliu, governor of Donglai, challenged the reconstruction with documentation of his own. Shu Xi rebutted him, yet Wang Tingjian died before the debate closed. Pan Tao urged Wang Jie that his scholarship could settle the feud between Wei Heng's and Wang Tingjian's schools. Wang Jie then weighed each side's strengths and flaws in full detail. Zhi Yu and Xie Heng, both celebrated polymaths, judged his verdict sound. He also wrote seventy-two further exemplary women's lives and well over a hundred thousand characters of essays, verse, inscriptions, and polemics—all lost in the wars.

史臣曰：皇甫謐素履幽貞，閒居養疾，留情筆削，敦悅丘墳，軒冕未足為榮，貧賤不以為恥，確乎不拔，斯固有晉之高人者歟！ 洎乎《篤終》立論，薄葬昭儉，既戒奢于季氏，亦無取于王孫，可謂達存亡之機矣。 摯虞、束皙等並詳覽載籍，多識舊章，奏議可觀，文詞雅贍，可謂博聞之士也。 或攝官延閣，裁成言事之書； 或蒞政秩宗，參定禋郊之禮。 虞既厄於從理，皙乃年位不充，天之報施，何其爽也！ 王接才調秀出，見賞知音，惜其夭枉，未申驥足，嗟夫！
The historians praise Huangfu Mi for living plainly in seclusion, tending chronic illness while devoting himself to scholarship; rank could not tempt him and want could not shame him—an unbending Jin exemplar of the first rank. His essay Sincere to the End preached modest burial, warning against the Jis' extravagance without copying Yang Wangsun's naked interment—altogether a balanced grasp of how to meet mortality. Zhi Yu and Shu Xi, steeped in the archives, wrote lucid memorials and graceful prose—true scholars of wide learning. Some served in the imperial library and edited state papers; others directed the ministry of rites and helped shape the suburban cult. Zhi Yu was thwarted by his own integrity; Shu Xi died before his talents ripened—how uneven Heaven’s rewards can be! Wang Jie’s gifts shone and won a patron’s praise, yet he died young before his stride could open—alas!

Encomium: Master Shi’an chose ease and a thatched heart. He lived for letters and forgot ambition for office. His testament commands respect, though his quest for long life misfired. Zhi Yu was encyclopedic; Shu Xi, “Guangwei,” stood alone. They shaped ritual codes and edited lost classics. They set Wei literature in order and sorted the Han corpus. Wang Jie followed later and left a clean name as well.

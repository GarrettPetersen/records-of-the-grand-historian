傅玄
Fu Xuan is the subject of this biography.
2
傅玄，字休奕，北地泥陽人也。 祖燮，漢漢陽太守。 父幹，魏扶風太守。 玄少孤貧，博學善屬文，解鐘律。 性剛勁亮直，不能容人之短。 郡上計吏再舉孝廉，太尉辟，皆不就。 州舉秀才，除郎中，與東海繆施俱以時譽選入著作，撰集《魏書》。 後參安東、衛軍軍事，轉溫令，再遷弘農太守，領典農校尉。 所居稱職，數上書陳便宜，多所匡正。 五等建，封鶉觚男。 武帝為晉王，以玄為散騎常侍。 及受禪，進爵為子，加附馬都尉。
Fu Xuan, whose courtesy name was Xiuyi, came from Niyang in Beidi. His grandfather Fu Xie had been Han Administrator of Hanyang. His father Fu Gan served Wei as Administrator of Fufeng. Orphaned and poor in youth, Fu Xuan nevertheless mastered a wide range of learning, wrote well, and understood musical theory. He was blunt and uncompromising by temperament and had little patience for others' shortcomings. Twice the commandery recommended him as Filially Pious and Incorrupt, and the Grand Commandant offered him appointment, but he declined every summons. Recommended as a provincial "flourishing talent," he received a Gentleman's post and, alongside Mu Shi of Donghai—both men enjoying contemporary esteem—was drafted into the editorial office to help compile the 《Wei shu》. He went on to staff the headquarters of the Guard-East and Guards armies, became magistrate of Wen, rose twice to Administrator of Hongnong, and held the concurrent post of agriculture intendant. Wherever he served he earned a reputation for competence; he often memorialized with practical proposals and helped set policy right. After the five-tier noble hierarchy was instituted, he received the barony of Chungu. While still Prince of Jin, the future Emperor Wu named Fu Xuan a Regular Palace Attendant. At the Jin dynastic transition his fief was raised to viscount's rank and he was also appointed chief steward attached to the imperial son-in-law's household.
3
帝初即位，廣納直言，開不諱之路，玄及散騎常侍皇甫陶共掌諫職。 玄上疏曰：「臣聞先王之臨天下也，明其大教，長其義節。 道化隆于上，清議行於下，上下相奉，人懷義心。 亡秦蕩滅先王之制，以法術相禦，而義心亡矣。 近者魏武好法術，而天下貴刑名； 魏文慕通達，而天下賤守節。 其後綱維不攝，而虛無放誕之論盈於朝野，使天下無復清議，而亡秦之病復發於今。 陛下聖德，龍興受禪，弘堯、舜之化，開正直之路，體夏禹之至儉，綜殷周之典文，臣詠歎而已，將又奚言！ 惟未舉清遠有禮之臣，以敦風節； 未退虛鄙，以懲不恪，臣是以猶敢有言。 」詔報曰：「舉清遠有禮之臣者，此尤今之要也。 」乃使玄草詔進之。 玄復上疏曰：
Early in his reign the emperor encouraged blunt counsel and welcomed criticism without reprisal; Fu Xuan and Huangfu Tao, both Regular Palace Attendants, shared responsibility for remonstrance. Fu Xuan presented a memorial: "I have read that the ancient kings, in ruling the realm, made clear the great moral teaching and nurtured a sense of duty and restraint. The Way's transforming influence flourished at court while honest opinion held sway in society; rulers and subjects reinforced each other until righteousness shaped every heart. The doomed Qin erased the old kings' institutions, ruled through naked legalism and administrative tricks, and thereby killed the moral conscience of the age. Not long ago Cao Cao favored Legalist methods, and the empire elevated harsh law-and-punishment doctrines. Cao Pi admired clever versatility, and the world came to scorn steadfast moral principle. Later the moral framework collapsed; talk of voidness and reckless abandon flooded court and country until honest public judgment vanished—exactly the sickness that had destroyed Qin was appearing again. Your Majesty received the mandate in a dragon ascent of sacred virtue; you extend the humane governance of Yao and Shun, welcome honest counsel, emulate Yu's austerity, and draw on the classical heritage of Shang and Zhou—I can only marvel aloud; what is left for me to add! Only one thing still awaits: raising up ministers who are lucid in judgment, aloof from vulgarity, and punctilious in ritual, so as to shore up public virtue. Nor have hollow and contemptible men yet been cleared away to reprimand slack conduct—which is why I still presume to speak. The emperor replied by edict: "Elevating principled, refined ministers is precisely what matters most now." He then had Fu Xuan draft the edict for promulgation. Fu Xuan submitted another memorial:
4
書奏，帝下詔曰：「二常侍懇懇於所論，可謂乃心欲佐益時事者也。 而主者率以常制裁之，豈得不使發憤耶！ 二常侍所論，或舉其大較而未備其條目，亦可便令作之，然後主者八坐廣共研精。 凡關言於人主，人臣之所至難。 而人主若不能虛心聽納，自古忠臣直士之所慷慨，至使杜口結舌。 每念於此，未嘗不歎息也。 故前詔敢有直言，勿有所距，庶幾得以發懞補過，獲保高位。 苟言有偏善，情在忠益，雖文辭有謬誤，言語有失得，皆當曠然恕之。 古人猶不拒誹謗，況皆善意在可採錄乎！ 近者孔晁、綦毋龢皆案以輕慢之罪，所以皆原，欲使四海知區區之朝無諱言之忌也。 」俄遷侍中。
After reading the memorial, the emperor proclaimed: "These two Regular Attendants have argued their case with sincere perseverance; plainly they mean to help steady the times." Yet the responsible offices habitually tie them up in bureaucratic routine—small wonder they boil with frustration! Their proposals may state the broad principles without every procedural detail; still, let them draft concrete provisions, after which the department heads and the senior ministers can polish them together. Speaking truth to the throne is among the hardest duties a subject faces. When a ruler refuses open-minded counsel, loyal officials have always felt blocked and bitter—until they simply fall silent. Whenever I think of it, I cannot help sighing. Hence my earlier order encouraged blunt speech and forbade brushing criticism aside—so that frank words might expose faults and worthy men keep their posts. Where advice is partly sound and the motive is loyal concern, slipshod phrasing or infelicitous wording ought all to be forgiven outright. Even antiquity did not suppress bitter criticism—much less should we ignore counsel offered in good faith! Kong Chao and Qiwu He were lately charged with disrespect; I pardoned them deliberately, so the empire would see that this court does not punish plain speech. Shortly afterward Fu Xuan was promoted to Palace Attendant.
5
初，玄進皇甫陶，及入而抵，玄以事與陶爭，言喧嘩，為有司所奏，二人竟坐免官。 ，以為御史中丞。 時頗有水旱之災，玄復上疏曰：
Fu Xuan had once recommended Huangfu Tao; once Tao arrived at court they repeatedly crossed swords over policy until their shouting drew a formal impeachment, and both lost their posts. Before long the court named him Imperial Clerk, chief of the censorial bureau. Floods and droughts had become frequent; Fu Xuan again memorialized:
6
詔曰：「得所陳便宜，言農事得失及水官興廢，又安邊禦胡政事寬猛之宜，申省周備，一二具之，此誠為國大本，當今急務也。 如所論皆善，深知乃心，廣思諸宜，動靜以聞也。」
The court replied: "Your memorial covers farming policy, irrigation administration, frontier defense against the Hu, and when government should be tight or lenient—you have spelled everything out with rare thoroughness. These are precisely the state's foundations and today's pressing priorities." Your proposals are sound; I understand your intent well—think through further measures as events unfold and keep me informed.
7
五年，遷太僕。 時比年不登，羌胡擾邊，詔公卿會議。 玄應對所問，陳事切直，雖不盡施行，而常見優容。 轉司隸校尉。
In the fifth year of the reign he was promoted to Grand Coachman. Harvests failed year after year while Qiang and Hu raiders harried the frontier; the emperor ordered a joint council of high officials. Fu Xuan answered forthrightly; though not every suggestion was adopted, the throne consistently indulged him. He was reassigned as Metropolitan Governor, that is, Colonel Director of Retainers.
8
獻皇后崩于弘訓宮，設喪位。 舊制，司隸於端門外坐，在諸卿上，絕席。 其入殿，按本品秩在諸卿下，以次坐，不絕席。 而謁者以弘訓宮為殿內，制玄位在卿下。 玄恚怒，厲聲色而責謁者。 謁者妄稱尚書所處，玄對百僚而罵尚書以下。 御史中丞庾純奏玄不敬，玄又自表不以實，坐免官。 然玄天性峻急，不能有所容； 每有奏劾，或值日暮，捧白簡，整簪帶，竦踴不寐，坐而待旦。 於是貴遊懾伏，臺閣生風。 尋卒于家，時年六十二，諡曰剛。
When Empress Xian died at Hongxun Palace, mourning arrangements were established. By longstanding rule the Metropolitan Governor took a seat outside the Duan Gate above the ministers, on his own dais. Inside the hall, however, his statutory rank placed him below the ministers in the seating order, without a separate mat. The protocol officers treated Hongxun Palace as interior space and assigned Fu Xuan a seat below the ministers. Fu Xuan lost his temper and berated the herald loudly. The herald falsely blamed the Secretariat; Fu Xuan then denounced the Secretariat before the entire assembly. Yu Chun impeached him for disrespect; Fu Xuan's defense misrepresented the facts, and he was dismissed. Yet Fu Xuan was irascible by nature and tolerated no opposition. Whenever he drafted an impeachment—even at dusk—he would clutch his ivory impeachment slip, straighten his hat and sash, and sit rigidly awake until dawn. The great families quailed before him, and a chill discipline seemed to sweep the ministries. He died at home not long after, at sixty-two, with the posthumous epithet "Rigid."
9
玄少時避難於河內，專心誦學，後雖顯貴，而著述不廢。 撰論經國九流及三史故事，評斷得失，各為區例，名為《傅子》，為內、外、中篇，凡有四部、六錄，合百四十首，數十萬言，並文集百餘卷行於世。 玄初作內篇成，子咸以示司空王沈。 沈與玄書曰：「省足下所著書，言富理濟，經綸政體，存重儒教，足以塞楊、墨之流遁，齊孫、孟于往代。 每開卷，未嘗不歎息也。 『不見賈生，自以過之，乃今不及』，信矣！」
In youth he had taken refuge in Henei and immersed himself in scholarship; even after rising to high rank he never stopped writing. He wrote discourses on statecraft, the nine intellectual traditions, and institutional precedents drawn from the standard histories, weighing right and wrong in organized chapters collected as the 《Fu zi》—inner, outer, and central portions totaling four parts, six registers, one hundred forty essays, and hundreds of thousands of characters—alongside another literary corpus of well over a hundred scrolls that circulated widely. When he completed the inner books of the 《Fu zi》, his son Fu Xian showed them to Wang Shen, the Minister of Works. Wang Shen wrote: "I have read your work: the argument is ample and the doctrine serves governance; it maps the structures of government and upholds Confucian teaching—enough to cut off Yangist and Mohist evasions and set Xunzi and Mencius beside the worthies of antiquity." Every time I open it I find myself sighing in admiration. "Until I saw Jia Yi I fancied myself his better—now I know I fall short." How true!

子咸
Fu Xuan's son: Fu Xian.
13
=咸字長虞，剛簡有大節。 風格峻整，識性明悟，疾惡如仇，推賢樂善，常慕季文子、仲山甫之志。 好屬文論，雖綺麗不足，而言成規鑒。 潁川庾純常歎曰：「長虞之文近乎詩人之作矣！」
Fu Xian, courtesy name Changyu, combined austerity with high moral seriousness. Stern and disciplined in deportment, acute in judgment, he loathed wickedness as personally as an enemy while lifting worthy men and rejoicing in the good—always modeling himself on Ji Wenzi and Zhong Shanfu. He loved to write essays; they lacked ornament but served as clear standards for readers. Yu Chun of Yingchuan used to exclaim, "Changyu writes almost like a poet!"
14
咸甯初，襲父爵，拜太子洗馬，累遷尚書右丞。 出為冀州刺史，繼母杜氏不肯隨咸之官，自表解職。 三旬之間，遷司徒左長史。 時帝留心政事，詔訪朝臣政之損益。 咸上言曰：「陛下處至尊之位，而修布衣之事，親覽萬機，勞心日昃。 在昔帝王，躬自菲薄，以利天下，未有逾陛下也。 然泰始開元以暨於今，十有五年矣。 而軍國未豐，百姓不贍，一歲不登便有菜色者，誠由官眾事殷，復除猥濫，蠶食者多而親農者少也。 臣以頑疏，謬忝近職，每見聖詔以百姓饑饉為慮，無能雲補，伏用慚恧，敢不自竭，以對天問。 舊都督有四，今並監軍，乃盈於十。 夏禹敷土，分為九州，今之刺史，幾向一倍。 戶口比漢十分之一，而置郡縣更多。 空校牙門，無益宿衛，而虛立軍府，動有百數。 五等諸侯，復坐置官屬。 諸所寵給，皆生於百姓。 一夫不農，有受其饑，今之不農，不可勝計。 縱使五稼普收，僅足相接； 暫有災患，便不繼贍。 以為當今之急，先並官省事，靜事息役，上下用心，惟農是務也。」
Early in Xianning he succeeded to his father's title, entered the heir apparent's household as attendant groom, and rose through several posts to Assistant Director of the right bureau of the Masters of Writing. Posted as Inspector of Ji, he resigned because his stepmother Lady Du refused to accompany him to his province. Within a month he was appointed senior clerk on the Minister of Education's staff. The emperor was deeply engaged in government and ordered court officials to comment on what helped or harmed the administration. Fu Xian wrote: "Though seated on the throne you live like a diligent commoner, personally steering every policy until sunset. Ancient sovereigns who tightened their own belts for the realm's sake never outdid you. Yet fifteen years have passed since the Taishi founding. Yet neither treasury nor people thrives; a single bad harvest brings hollow cheeks everywhere—because offices multiply, tax exemptions are handed out indiscriminately, parasites devour resources, and too few till the soil themselves. Coarse and obscure as I am, I yet hold a place near the throne; whenever Your Majesty's edicts voice alarm over popular hunger I feel useless shame—so I dare reply with everything I can offer. We once had four regional commanders; with supervisory postings thrown in they now number over ten. Yu mapped the realm into nine provinces; we now maintain nearly twice as many provincial inspectors. Registered households are barely a tenth of Han levels, yet we maintain even more counties and commanderies. Hollow garrison posts do nothing for palace security while phantom army yamen sprawl by the hundreds. Each noble of the five ranks runs a full personal bureaucracy. Every stipend they receive comes straight from the people. Classical wisdom says when one man leaves the plough someone goes hungry—today innumerable hands never touch the soil. Even a bumper harvest across every crop would scarcely keep pace. The slightest disaster breaks the chain of supply. The pressing need is to consolidate offices, cut busywork, end needless labor service, and focus every level of government on farming alone."
15
咸在位多所執正。 豫州大中正夏侯駿上言，魯國小中正、司空司馬孔毓，四移病所，不能接賓，求以尚書郎曹馥代毓，旬日復上毓為中正。 司徒三卻，駿故據正。 咸以駿與奪惟意，乃奏免駿大中正。 司徒魏舒，駿之姻屬，屢卻不署，咸據正甚苦。 舒終不從，咸遂獨上。 舒奏咸激訕不直，詔轉咸為車騎司馬。
In office Fu Xian repeatedly insisted on proper procedure. Yu Province's chief neutral evaluator Xiahou Jun reported that Kong Yu—Lu's junior evaluator and an aide at the Ministry of Works—had relocated his sickbed four times and could no longer entertain callers, and sought to swap in Cao Fu—then within ten days tried to put Kong Yu back in office. The Minister of Education rejected the paperwork three times, yet Xiahou Jun stubbornly pressed his point. Fu Xian believed Xiahou Jun was manipulating appointments on whim and impeached him out of the chief evaluator post. Minister Wei Shu, Xiahou Jun's kinsman by marriage, repeatedly blocked the paperwork; Fu Xian fought bitterly to uphold protocol. Wei Shu never yielded, so Fu Xian filed the impeachment unilaterally. Wei Shu accused Fu Xian of malicious insinuation; the court reassigned him as carriage-and-cavalry army aide.
16
咸以世俗奢侈，又上書曰：「臣以為穀帛難生，而用之不節，無緣不匱。 故先王之化天下，食肉衣帛，皆有其制。 竊謂奢侈之費，甚於天災。 古者堯有茅茨，今之百姓競豐其屋。 古者臣無玉食，今之賈豎皆厭粱肉。 古者后妃乃有殊飾，今之婢妾被服綾羅。 古者大夫乃不徒行，今之賤隸乘輕驅肥。 古者人稠地狹而有儲蓄，由於節也； 今者土廣人稀而患不足，由於奢也。 欲時之儉，當詰其奢； 奢不見詰，轉相高尚。 昔毛玠為吏部尚書，時無敢好衣美食者。 魏武帝歎曰：『孤之法不如毛尚書。 』令使諸部用心，各如毛玠，風俗之移，在不難矣。 」又議移縣獄於郡及二社應立，朝廷從之。 遷尚書左丞。
Troubled by lavish habits at court, Fu Xian wrote again: "Grain and silk do not come easily; spend without restraint and scarcity follows inevitably. The ancient kings regulated even meat-eating and silk-wearing so consumption matched produce. I believe wasteful luxury drains the realm more cruelly than flood or drought. Emperor Yao lived beneath thatch; today ordinary families compete to build ever grander homes. Once even senior officials seldom tasted luxurious fare; now petty traders are surfeited with the best grain and meat. Finery once reserved for palace ladies now drapes slave girls and secondary wives. Men of rank once rode rather than walked; today low servants parade on sleek horses and in fast gigs. Ancient populations were dense and fields small, yet granaries stayed full because people lived frugally. Now fields are wide and people few, yet want persists—because luxury wastes what plenty could supply. If we want thrift in our times, we must discipline excess wherever it appears. Unchecked ostentation becomes a contest in conspicuous consumption. Under Mao Jie's tenure at the Ministry of Personnel, nobody flaunted sumptuous dress or banquets. Cao Cao once lamented that his own statutes fell short of Mao Jie's standards. Had each bureau worked with Mao Jie's discipline, reforming habits would have been easy. His further proposals—to relocate county jails under commandery authority and establish the two soil altars—were adopted. He rose to left Assistant Director at the Secretariat.
17
惠帝即位，楊駿輔政。 咸言於駿曰：「事與世變，禮隨時宜，諒暗之不行尚矣。 由世道彌薄，權不可假，故雖斬焉在疚，而躬覽萬機也。 逮至漢文，以天下體大，服重難久，遂制既葬而除。 世祖武皇帝雖大孝蒸蒸，亦從時釋服，制心喪三年，至於萬機之事，則有不遑。 今聖上欲委政於公，諒暗自居，此雖謙讓之心，而天下未以為善。 天下未以為善者，以億兆顒顒，戴仰宸極，聽於冢宰，懼天光有蔽。 人心既已若此，而明公處之固未為易也。 竊謂山陵之事既畢，明公當思隆替之宜。 周公聖人，猶不免謗。 以此推之，周公之任既未易而處，況聖上春秋非成王之年乎！ 得意忘言，言未易盡。 苟明公有以察其悾款，言豈在多。 」時司隸荀愷從兄喪，自表赴哀，詔聽之而未下，愷乃造駿。 咸因奏曰：「死喪之戚，兄弟孔懷。 同堂亡隕，方在信宿，聖恩矜憫，聽使臨喪。 詔未下而便以行造，急諂媚之敬，無友于之情。 宜加顯貶，以隆風教。 」帝以駿管朝政，有詔不問，駿甚憚之。 咸復與駿箋諷切之，駿意稍折，漸以不平。 由是欲出為京兆、弘農太守，駿甥李斌說駿，不宜斥出正人，乃止。 駿弟濟素與咸善，與咸書曰：「江海之流混混，故能成其深廣也。 天下大器，非可稍了，而相觀每事欲了。 生子癡，了官事，官事未易了也。 了事正作癡，復為快耳！ 左丞總司天臺，維正八坐，此未易居。 以君盡性而處未易居之任，益不易也。 想慮破頭，故具有白。 」咸答曰：「衛公-{云}-酒色之殺人，此甚於作直。 坐酒色死，人不為悔。 逆畏以直致禍，此由心不直正，欲以苟且為明哲耳！ 自古以直致禍者，當自矯枉過直，或不忠允，欲以亢厲為聲，故致忿耳。 安有空空為忠益，而當見疾乎！ 」居無何，駿誅。 咸轉為太子中庶子，遷御史中丞。
Emperor Hui's accession brought Yang Jun to power as regent. Fu Xian warned Yang Jun: "Institutions must evolve; the archaic ideal of total palatial seclusion during mourning no longer fits reality. In a decadent age power cannot be delegated, so sovereigns still handle every state paper even while wearing mourning. Emperor Wen of Han shortened full mourning because the realm was too large to pause governance indefinitely; mourning dress ended with burial. Even founding Emperor Wu, profoundly filial, adjusted outward mourning to circumstance while keeping three years of inward grief—yet still found government overwhelming. The young emperor means to leave affairs to you and withdraw in mourning; however humble that sounds, the empire does not welcome it. People look to the throne itself; hearing only a regent's voice they worry that true imperial authority is hidden. Given such sentiment, sir, your footing as regent remains precarious. Once the burial rites conclude, you must decide what truly stabilizes or endangers the dynasty. Even the Duke of Zhou faced malicious gossip. If regency taxed the Duke of Zhou, how much heavier is it when the emperor is no mere boy like King Cheng of Zhou? Insight transcends wording, yet language can never capture everything. If you recognize sincere loyalty, a few heartfelt sentences suffice. Meanwhile Xun Kai, Metropolitan Governor, sought leave to mourn a cousin; imperial approval had not yet been promulgated when he hurried to Yang Jun's door. Fu Xian seized the occasion: "Funeral grief should unite kinsmen. The cousin had barely lain two nights in death when imperial grace allowed Kai to join the mourning. Yet he scampered to your gate before the order appeared—a naked bid for favor, not familial duty. Demote him visibly to teach the capital what integrity means. The emperor shielded Jun and refused an investigation, yet Jun grew terrified of Fu Xian. Further blunt notes from Fu Xian bent Yang Jun a little yet bred quiet bitterness. Yang Jun tried to exile him to Jingzhao or Hongnong until nephew Li Bin argued that expelling honest ministers would backfire. Fu Xian argued: "The code charges the Imperial Clerk with oversight of every official. Anyone from the crown prince downward inside the palace barriers who breaks the law falls under impeachment. Even beyond those barriers, if inspectors neglect their duty, the censor may still bring charges. The statute treats offenses inside the cordon as matters of interior palace security. Outside agencies cannot police the palace interior, so the Imperial Clerk holds that jurisdiction alone. When bridges crumble and markets breed endless lawsuits, the censor rightly shifts blame to regional authorities—stretching "interior cordon" language to cover real governance failures. If the censor supervises all bureaucrats, repeating "within the cordon" makes no sense. "All officials" means capital and provincial servants together; the boundary collapses. The Metropolitan Governor omits cordon wording simply because palace security belongs to the Imperial Clerk. Both officers indict everyone below the crown prince and share jurisdiction; neither is boxed into "inner" or "outer" alone. Historically both offices have crossed palace walls when pressing charges; impeachment admits no artificial divide. My request was denied yet the throne dismisses it as a minor slip I could not help—pardoning me anyway. Either both crown prince and Secretariat sit inside the cordon or they do not—you cannot impeach one and shield the other. The logic is obvious—yet Xie Jie twists it to shame me. 宣字世弘。 年六歲喪繼母，哭泣如成人，中表異之。 及長，好學，趙王倫以為相國掾、尚書郎、太子中舍人，遷司徒西曹掾。 去職，累遷為秘書丞、驃騎從事中郎。 惠帝至自長安，以宣為左丞，不就，遷黃門郎。 懷帝即位，轉吏部郎，又為御史中丞。 卒年四十九，無子，以暢子沖為嗣。
Fu Xuan's courtesy name was Shihong. At six he mourned his stepmother like a grown man—kinsfolk were astonished. Adult studies brought posts under Prince Sima Lun—ministerial aide, Secretariat gentleman, heir's adviser—then western bureau clerk. After quitting earlier posts he rose to library deputy and champion-general aide. Emperor Hui's return from Chang'an offered Fu Xuan the left assistant directorship; he declined but accepted a Yellow Gates post. Emperor Huai shifted him into the Ministry of Personnel and restored him as Imperial Clerk. Childless at forty-nine, he adopted nephew Fu Chong, Fu Chang's son, as successor.
35
暢字世道。 年五歲，父友見而戲之，解暢衣，取其金環與侍者，暢不之惜，以此賞之。 年未弱冠，甚有重名。 以選入侍講東宮，為秘書丞。 尋沒于石勒，勒以為大將軍右司馬。 諳識朝儀，恆居機密，勒甚重之。 作《晉諸公敘贊》二十二卷，又為《公卿故事》九卷。 卒。 子詠，過江為交州刺史、太子右率。
Fu Chang used the courtesy name Shidao. When he was five a friend of his father stripped him in jest and handed his gold ring to a servant; Fu Chang scarcely cared—earning adult admiration. Before his capping ceremony he already enjoyed wide renown. Chosen to lecture in the heir's palace, he served as deputy director of the imperial library. Captured by Shi Le, he became senior aide on the latter's general staff. Fluent in court etiquette and privy to military secrets, he won Shi Le's deep respect. His literary legacy includes twenty-two scrolls of encomiastic sketches on Jin statesmen and nine scrolls of anecdotes about high ministers. His life came to an end. His son Fu Yong crossed south to serve as Jiaozhou inspector and commander of the heir's right guard.

【史評】
Historians' appraisal.
37
史臣曰：武帝覽觀四方，平章百姓，永言啟沃，任切爭臣。 傅玄體強直之姿，懷匪躬之操，抗辭正色，補闕弼違，諤諤當朝，不忝其職者矣。 及乎位居三獨，彈擊是司，遂能使台閣生風，貴戚斂手。 雖前代鮑、葛，何以加之！ 然而惟此褊心，乏弘雅之度，驟聞競爽，為物議所譏，惜哉！ 古人取戒于韋弦，良有以也。 長虞風格凝峻，弗墜家聲。 及其納諫汝南，獻書臨晉，居諒直之地，有先見之明矣。 傅祗名父之子，早樹風猷，崎嶇危亂之朝，匡救君臣之際，卒能保全祿位，可謂有道存焉。
The chroniclers observe that Emperor Wu toured the realm, settled the people, thirsted for blunt advice, and leaned heavily on remonstrating officials. Fu Xuan united moral backbone with fearless counsel, plugging policy gaps and exposing wrongdoing—he honored every charge the throne gave him. Heading an autonomous censorate he froze the bureaucracy with indictments and cowed powerful families. Not even Han exemplars such as Bao Xuan and Ge Yi clearly outshone him! Yet his cramped temper lacked gracious breadth; quick quarrels drew public sneers—a pity! The classical metaphor of soft leather versus tight bowstring warns temperaments like his—and aptly so. Fu Xian's icy dignity preserved the Fu family's reputation. His blunt counsel to Prince Liang of Runan and timely memorials at Linjin showed principled foresight. Fu Zhi, heir to a celebrated father, forged his ethos young and steadied sovereign and ministers through turmoil—emerging with honor intact proves statesmanship.
38
贊曰：鶉觚貞諒，實惟朝望。 志厲強直，性乖夷曠。 長虞剛簡，無虧風尚。 子莊才識，爰膺袞職。 忠績未申，泉途遽逼。 =
Verdict: Fu Xuan of Chungu bore unyielding integrity—the moral compass of his age. His will demanded blunt honor yet his temper refused mellow ease. Fu Xian's austerity never betrayed public virtue. Fu Zhi's abilities earned him ministerial robes and key portfolios. Loyal service unfinished—death closed in overnight. No interpretable Chinese sentence survives in this lemma.

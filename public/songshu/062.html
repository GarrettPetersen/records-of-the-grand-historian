列傳第二十二羊欣張敷王微
Biographies 22: Yang Xin, Zhang Fu, and Wang Wei
2
羊欣，字敬元，泰山南城人也。 曾祖忱，晉徐州刺史。 祖權，黃門郎。 父不疑，桂陽太守。 欣少靖默，無競於人，美言笑，善容止。 汎覽經籍，尤長隸書。 不疑初為烏程令，欣時年十二，時王獻之為吳興太守，甚知愛之。 獻之嘗夏月入縣，欣著新絹裙晝寢，獻之書裙數幅而去。 欣本工書，因此彌善。 起家輔國參軍，府解還家。 隆安中，朝廷漸亂，欣優遊私門，不復進仕。 會稽王世子元顯每使欣書，常辭不奉命，元顯怒，乃以為其後軍府舍人。 此職本用寒人，欣意貌恬然，不以高卑見色，論者稱焉。 欣嘗詣領軍將軍謝混，混拂席改服，然後見之。 時混族子靈運在坐，退告族兄瞻曰：「望蔡見羊欣，遂易衣改席。 」欣由此益知名。
Yang Xin, whose courtesy name was Jingyuan, came from Nancheng in Taishan commandery. His great-grandfather Chen had served as inspector of Xuzhou under the Jin. His grandfather Quan had been a gentleman at the Yellow Gate. His father Buyi had served as administrator of Guiyang. As a youth Xin was quiet and unassuming, never striving against others; he spoke gracefully, laughed pleasantly, and carried himself with natural elegance. He read widely in the classics and was especially accomplished in clerical script calligraphy. When his father Buyi was first appointed magistrate of Wucheng, Xin was twelve; Wang Xianzhi was then administrator of Wuxing and took a great liking to him. Once in midsummer Xianzhi came into the county seat and found Xin napping in a new silk skirt; he wrote several lines on the skirt and left. Xin was already skilled at calligraphy, and this episode made him still better. He entered office as aide to the army of the Supporting-the-State commandery and returned home when that post was abolished. During the Long'an years, as the court grew increasingly chaotic, Xin kept to his private life and sought no further advancement. Sima Yuanxian, heir apparent of Kuaiji, often asked Xin to write for him; Xin habitually refused, and Yuanxian in anger appointed him clerk in his rear-army headquarters. The post was normally given to men of humble birth, yet Xin remained serene in look and manner and showed no change of color whether treated high or low; observers praised him for it. Once when Xin called on the commanding general Xie Hun, Hun brushed the mat and changed his clothes before receiving him. Xie Lingyun, a kinsman of Hun's, was present; afterward he told his elder cousin Zhan, "When Wangcai received Yang Xin, he changed his clothes and brushed the mat." From this episode Xin became still more widely known.
3
桓玄輔政，領平西將軍，以欣為平西參軍，仍轉主簿，參預機要。 欣欲自疏，時漏密事，玄覺其此意，愈重之，以為楚臺殿中郎。 謂曰：「尚書政事之本，殿中禮樂所出。 卿昔處股肱，方此為輕也。 」欣拜職少日，稱病自免，屏居里巷，十餘年不出。
When Huan Xuan held power as regent and also served as General Pacifying the West, he appointed Xin an aide on his staff, then chief clerk, entrusting him with confidential affairs. Wishing to keep his distance, Xin sometimes leaked confidential matters; Xuan perceived his intent and valued him all the more, appointing him palace attendant of the Chu Terrace. He told him, "The Secretariat is the root of government; the palace service is where ritual and music take their form." You once stood at the ruler's right hand; this post is lighter by comparison." Xin had held the post only a few days when he pleaded illness and resigned, living secluded in his neighborhood for more than ten years without going abroad.
4
義熙中，弟徽被遇於高祖，高祖謂諮議參軍鄭鮮之曰：「羊徽一時美器，世論猶在兄後，恨不識之。 」即板欣補右將軍劉藩司馬，轉長史，中軍將軍道憐諮議參軍。 出為新安太守。 在郡四年，簡惠著稱。 除臨川王義慶輔國長史，廬陵王義真車騎諮議參軍，並不就。 太祖重之，以為新安太守，前後凡十三年，遊玩山水，甚得適性。 轉在義興，非其好也。 頃之，又稱病篤自免歸。 除中散大夫。
During the Yixi years his younger brother Hui came into favor with Emperor Wu; the emperor said to adviser Zheng Xianzhi, "Yang Hui is a rare talent of the day, yet opinion still ranks him below his elder brother—I regret never having met the elder." He at once appointed Xin marshal on the staff of the Right General Liu Fan, then chief clerk, and adviser to the Central Army general Dao Lin. He was sent out as administrator of Xin'an. During four years in the commandery he won renown for simple, benevolent rule. He was appointed chief clerk assisting the state on the staff of Prince Linchuan Liu Yiqing and adviser to the chariots-and-cavalry staff of Prince Luling Liu Yizhen, but accepted neither post. Emperor Wen valued him and kept him as administrator of Xin'an; for thirteen years in all he wandered among mountains and streams and found the life deeply congenial. When he was transferred to Yixing, it did not suit his taste. Before long he again pleaded serious illness and resigned to go home. He was appointed grandee remonstrant.
5
素好黃老，常手自書章，有病不服藥，飲符水而已。 兼善醫術，撰《藥方》十卷。 欣以不堪拜伏，辭不朝覲，高祖、太祖並恨不識之。 自非尋省近親，不妄行詣，行必由城外，未嘗入六關。 元嘉十九年，卒，時年七十三。 子俊，早卒。
He had long favored Huang-Lao teachings, often copied talisman texts himself, and when ill refused medicine, drinking only talisman water. He was also skilled in medicine and compiled a ten-juan work entitled Prescriptions. Unable to bear bowing and prostration, Xin declined court audiences; both Emperor Wu and Emperor Wen regretted never having met him in person. Except when paying condolence visits to close kin, he never called on anyone casually; when he went out he always kept to roads outside the city walls and never passed through the six inner gates. In the nineteenth year of Yuanjia he died, aged seventy-three. His son Jun died young.
6
弟徽，字敬猷，世譽多欣。 高祖鎮京口，以為記室參軍掌事。 八年，遷中書郎，直西省。 後為太祖西中郎長史、河東太守。 子瞻，元嘉末為世祖南中郎長史、尋陽太守，卒官。
His younger brother Hui, courtesy name Jingyou, enjoyed greater contemporary renown than Xin. When Emperor Wu held Jingkou, he appointed Hui recording secretary on his staff, in charge of affairs. In the eighth year he was promoted to gentleman of the secretariat and served in the western office. Later he served Emperor Wen as chief clerk on the western central-army staff and as administrator of Hedong. His son Zhan, at the end of Yuanjia, served Emperor Xiaowu as chief clerk on the southern central-army staff and as administrator of Xunyang, and died in office.
7
張敷，字景胤，吳郡人，吳興太守邵子也。 生而母沒。 年數歲，問母所在，家人告以死生之分，敷雖童蒙，便有思慕之色。 年十許歲，求母遺物，而散施已盡，唯得一畫扇，乃緘錄之，每至感思，輒開笥流涕。 見從母，常悲感哽咽。 性整貴，風韻甚高，好讀玄書，兼屬文論，少有盛名。 高祖見而愛之，以為世子中軍參軍，數見接引。 永初初，遷秘書郎。 嘗在省直，中書令傅亮貴宿權要，聞其好學，過候之； 敷臥不即起，亮怪而去。
Zhang Fu, courtesy name Jingyin, was a native of Wu commandery and the son of Shao, administrator of Wuxing. His mother died when he was born. When he was only a few years old he asked where his mother was; the family explained that she was gone forever; though still a small child, he already wore the look of one who mourned her. At about ten he asked for his mother's belongings, but everything had already been given away; he found only a painted fan, which he sealed away; whenever grief overcame him he would open the chest and weep. Whenever he saw his aunt by marriage he would choke with grief. Upright and fastidious by nature, with a lofty bearing, he loved arcane learning and was also skilled at literary composition; he won early renown. Emperor Wu took a liking to him and appointed him aide in the crown prince's central-army staff, summoning him often for guidance. At the beginning of Yongchu he was promoted to gentleman of the secretariat. Once while on duty at court, Fu Liang, the secretariat director—a senior figure of great influence—heard of his love of learning and came to call on him; Fu remained lying down and did not rise at once; Liang took offense and left.
8
父邵為湘州，去官侍從。 太祖版為西中郎參軍。 元嘉初，為員外散騎侍郎，秘書丞。 江夏王義恭鎮江陵，以為撫軍功曹，轉記室參軍。 時義恭就太祖求一學義沙門，比沙門求見發遣，會敷赴假還江陵，太祖謂沙門曰：「張敷應西，當令相載。 」及敷辭，上謂曰：「撫軍須一意懷道人，卿可以後艑載之，道中可得言晤。 」敷不奉旨，曰：「臣性不耐雜。 」上甚不說。
When his father Shao went to Xiangzhou, he left his post to accompany him. Emperor Wen appointed him by provisional commission as aide on the western central-army staff. At the beginning of Yuanjia he served as extraordinary gentleman of the palace cavalry and secretary of the secretariat. When Prince Jiangxia Liu Yigong held Jiangling, he appointed Fu merit officer on his pacifying-army staff, then recording secretary. Yigong had asked the emperor for a monk learned in doctrine; just as the monk was seeking leave to depart, Fu happened to be returning on leave to Jiangling; the emperor told the monk, "Zhang Fu is going west—you should travel with him." When Fu came to take his leave, the emperor said, "The pacifying-army command needs a devoted man of the Way; you may take him in the rear boat and talk with him on the journey." Fu declined the order, saying, "Your servant by nature cannot bear mixed company." The emperor was deeply displeased.
9
遷正員郎。 中書舍人狄當、周赳並管要務，以敷同省名家，欲詣之。 赳曰：「彼若不相容，便不如不往。 詎可輕往邪？ 」當曰：「吾等並已員外郎矣，何憂不得共坐。 」敷先設二床，去壁三四尺，二客就席，酬接甚歡，既而呼左右曰：「移我遠客。 」赳等失色而去。 其自摽遇如此。 善持音儀，盡詳緩之致，與人別，執手曰：「念相聞。 」餘響久之不絕。 張氏後進至今慕之，其源流起自敷也。
He was promoted to regular gentleman. The secretariat clerks Di Dang and Zhou Jiu both handled weighty affairs; seeing Fu as a famous man of the same office, they wished to call on him. Jiu said, "If he will not tolerate us, we had better not go." How can we go so lightly?" Dang said, "We are already extraordinary gentlemen—why worry that we cannot sit together?" Fu first set out two couches three or four feet from the wall; the two guests sat down and the conversation went pleasantly enough; then he called to his attendants, "Move me away from these distant guests." Jiu and the others turned pale and left. Such was the way he held himself and treated others. He was skilled in tone and bearing, carrying unhurried grace to its fullest; when he parted from someone he would take his hand and say, "Remember to let me hear from you." The lingering cadence of his voice would long not fade. Later generations of the Zhang clan still admire him; the style they follow begins with Fu.
10
遷黃門侍郎，始興王濬後軍長史，司徒左長史。 未拜，父在吳興亡，報以疾篤，敷往奔省，自發都至吳興成服，凡十餘日，始進水漿。 葬畢，不進鹽菜，遂毀瘠成疾。 世父茂度每止譬之，輒更感慟，絕而復續。 茂度曰：「我冀譬汝有益，但更甚耳。 」自是不復往。 未朞而卒，時年四十一。
He was promoted to gentleman attendant at the Yellow Gate, chief clerk on Prince Shixing Liu Jun's rear-army staff, and left chief clerk of the minister of works. Before he could take up the appointment, word came that his father was dying in Wuxing; Fu rushed home; from leaving the capital until donning mourning garb in Wuxing more than ten days passed before he took even water or broth. After the burial he ate no salted food or vegetables and wasted away into serious illness. His uncle Maodu would try each time to comfort him, but Fu only grieved the more, fainting and coming to again. Maodu said, "I hoped comforting you would help, but it only makes things worse." After that he stopped visiting. Before the mourning year was out he died, aged forty-one.
11
琅邪顏延之書弔茂度曰：「賢弟子少履貞規，長懷理要，清風素氣，得之天然。 言面以來，便申忘年之好，比雖艱隔成阻，而情問無睽。 薄莫之人，冀其方見慰說，豈謂中年，奄為長往，聞問悼心，有兼恆痛。 足下門教敦至，兼實家寶，一旦喪失，何可為懷。 」其見重如此。 世祖即位，詔曰：「司徒故左長史張敷，貞心簡立，幼樹風規。 居哀毀滅，孝道淳至，宜在追甄，於以報美。 可追贈侍中。 」於是改其所居稱為孝張里。 無子。
Yan Yanzhi of Langye wrote to condole with Maodu: "Your worthy nephew in youth upheld pure conduct; in maturity he cherished the essentials of principle; clear bearing and plain spirit were his by nature." Since we met in person we formed a friendship that ignored the difference in age; though distance and hardship have lately come between us, affection and regard have not grown cold." I am myself near evening; I had hoped soon to see him again for comfort and talk—who would have thought that in midlife he would suddenly depart forever? The news fills my heart with a grief beyond ordinary sorrow. Your household discipline is sincere and thorough, and he was moreover a true treasure of the clan; to lose him in a single day—how can grief be contained?" Such was the esteem in which he was held. When Emperor Xiaowu took the throne, an edict said, "The late left chief clerk of the minister of works, Zhang Fu, possessed a loyal heart and simple integrity and from youth established moral standards." In mourning he destroyed himself through grief; his filial devotion was pure and complete; he should be posthumously honored to repay his excellence." Let him be posthumously granted attendant within. His former residence was then renamed Filial Zhang Lane. He left no sons.
12
王微，字景玄，琅邪臨沂人，太保弘弟子也。 父孺，光祿大夫。 微少好學，無不通覽，善屬文，能書畫，兼解音律、醫方、陰陽術數。 年十六，州舉秀才，衡陽王義季右軍參軍，並不就。 起家司徒祭酒，轉主簿，始興王濬後軍功曹記室參軍，太子中舍人，始興王友。 父憂去官，服闋，除南平王鑠右軍諮議參軍。 微素無宦情，稱疾不就。 仍除中書侍郎，又擬南琅邪、義興太守，並固辭。 吏部尚書江湛舉微為吏部郎，微與湛書曰：
Wang Wei, courtesy name Jingxuan, came from Linyi in Langye and was a nephew of Grand Tutor Hong. His father Ru had been grand master for the glorious chariots. From youth Wei loved learning and read widely; he wrote well, painted skillfully, and was also versed in music, medicine, and the arts of yin-yang and numerology. At sixteen the province recommended him as cultivated talent and appointed him aide in Prince Hengyang Liu Yiji's right-army staff, but he accepted neither post. He entered office as libationer of the minister of works, then chief clerk, then merit officer and recording secretary on Prince Shixing Liu Jun's rear-army staff, gentleman attendant of the heir apparent, and friend to Prince Shixing. He left office on his father's death; when mourning ended he was appointed adviser on Prince Nanping Liu Shuo's right-army staff. Wei had never cared for office and pleaded illness without accepting. He was then appointed gentleman of the secretariat and also nominated administrator of Southern Langye and of Yixing, but firmly declined every post. 13
弟心病亂度，非但蹇蹙而已，此處朝野所共知。 騶會忽扣蓽門，閭里咸以為祥怪，君多識前世之載，天值何其易傾。 弟受海內駭笑，不過如燕石禿鶖邪，未知君何以自解於良史邪？ 今雖王道鴻鬯，或有激朗於天表，必欲探援潛寶，傾海求珠，自可卜肆巫祠之間，馬棧牛口之下，賞劇孟於博徒，拔卜式於芻牧。 亦有西戎孤臣，東都戒士，上窮範馳之御，下盡詭遇之能，兼鱗雜襲者，必不乏於世矣。 且廬於承明，署乎金馬，皆明察之官，又賢於管庫之末。 何為劫勒通家疾病人，塵穢難堪之選，將以靖國，不亦益囂乎？ 《書》云：「任官維賢才」。 而君擢士先疹廢，芃芃棫樸，似不如此。 且弟曠違兄姊，迄將十載，姊時歸來，終不任輿曳入閣，兄守金城，永不堪扶抱就路，若不憊疾，非性僻而何。 比君曰表裏，無假長目飛耳也。
Your younger brother's heart ailment has grown chaotic—not merely lameness and constraint; this is known alike at court and in the countryside. A mounted messenger suddenly knocked at my humble gate; the neighbors all took it for an omen, strange or auspicious; you are well read in the records of former ages—how easily Heaven's appointments topple. Your younger brother will become the laughingstock of the realm—no more than the Yan stone or the bald stork, perhaps? I do not know how you will explain yourself to future historians. Though the royal way is now vast and bright, there may yet be men of striking brilliance beyond the common run; if you must hunt hidden talent and drain the seas for pearls, you can find them between fortune-tellers' stalls and shaman shrines, in stables and cattle pens—reward a Ji Meng among gamblers, raise a Bu Shi from the pasture. There are also lone retainers from the western marches and seasoned men of the eastern capital—masters of charioteering to the heights of Fan Chi's art, adepts of artful gambits to the depths of crafty encounter; men of many gifts in confused profusion are scarcely rare in this world. Posts at the Chenming Gate and the Golden Horse Gate are offices of keen discernment—far worthier than a humble warehouse clerk. Why dragoon a sick neighbor from an old friend of your house—someone filthy and unbearable—as a choice to settle the realm? Would that not only make the scandal worse? The Book of Documents says, "In appointing office, look only to talent and worth." Yet you raise men while putting first the rashly ruined; "lush are the elm saplings"—surely it cannot mean this. Your younger brother has lived apart from his siblings for nearly ten years; when my sister visits, I cannot even endure being hauled in a carriage through the gate; my elder brother holds Jincheng and could never bear being helped on the road—if this is not illness and exhaustion, what is it but a contrary nature? You speak as though you know me through and through—there is no need to spy with distant eyes and floating ears.
14
常謂生遭太公，將即華士之戮； 幸遇管叔，必蒙僻儒之養。 光武以馮衍才浮其實，故棄而不齒。 諸葛孔明云：「來敏亂郡，過於孔文舉。 」況無古人之才槩，敢干周、漢之常刑。 彼二三英賢，足為曉治與否？ 恐君逄此時，或亦不免高閣，乃復假名不知己者，豈欲自比衛賜邪？ 君欲高斅山公，而以仲容見處，徒以搥提禮學，本不參選，鄙夫瞻彼，固不任下走，未知新沓何如州陵耳。 而作不師古，坐亂官政，誣飾蚯蚓，冀招神龍，如復託以真素者，又不宜居華留名，有害風俗。 君亦不至期人如此，若交以為人賜，舉未以己勞，則商販之事，又連所不忍聞也。 豈謂不肖易擢，貪者可誘，凡此數者，君必居一焉。 雖假天口於齊駢，藉鬼說於周季，公孫碎毛髮之文，莊生縱漭瀁之極，終不能舉其契，為之辭矣。 子將明魂，必靈咍於萬里，汝、潁餘彥，將拂衣而不朝。 浮華一開，風俗或從此而爽。 鬼谷以揣情為最難，何君忖度之輕謬。
I have often said that meeting Taigong in life would soon mean Hua Shi's punishment— but to meet Guan Shu would mean shelter from a reclusive scholar. Emperor Guangwu found Feng Yan's talent exceeded his substance and cast him aside, refusing to rank him among his men. Zhuge Liang said, "Lai Min threw counties into chaos, worse than Kong Rong. How much more when one lacks the caliber of the ancients and dares to invite the ordinary penalties of Zhou and Han! Are those two or three heroic worthies enough to prove whether one understands governance? I fear when you meet such a time you too may not escape the high cabinet—yet you again borrow the name of one who does not know you; do you wish to compare yourself to Wei Ci? You wish to rank yourself above Shan Tao, yet treat me like Ruan Zhongrong—merely because you hammer at ritual studies, you were never in the candidacies; a vulgar fellow like me, looking at that, surely cannot serve as a runner; I do not know how Xinta measures up to Zhouling. Yet to act without learning from antiquity, to sit and disorder official government, to dress up earthworms hoping to summon dragons—if you again rely on unadorned genuineness, you also should not dwell in splendor and leave a name, harming public morals. You would not expect this of others either; if friendship becomes a gift from others, and promotion comes from one's own labor—then the ways of merchants touch what I cannot bear to hear. Do you mean that the unworthy are easy to promote and the greedy can be tempted? Among these several, you must be one. Even if you borrowed Heaven's mouth from Qi Pian, ghost speech from Zhou Ji, Gongsun Long's hair-splitting prose, and Zhuangzi's utmost flood of words—you still could not fulfill their bargain; I have said my piece. The enlightened dead will surely wail in spirit across ten thousand li; the remaining fine men of Ru and Ying will shake out their robes and refuse court. Once vain display is unleashed, public morals may slip away from this. Guiguzi held that reading another's feelings was the hardest thing—why is your judgment so light and mistaken?
15
今有此書，非敢叨擬中散，誠不能顧影負心，純盜虛聲，所以綿絡累紙，本不營尚書虎爪板也。 成童便往來居舍，晨省復經周旋，加有諸甥，亦何得頓絕慶弔。 然生平之意，自於此都盡。 君平公云：「生我名者殺我身。 」天爵且猶滅名，安用吏部郎哉！ 其舉可陋，其事不經，非獨搢紳者不道，僕妾皆將咲之。 忽忽不樂，自知壽不得長，且使千載知弟不詐諼耳。
This letter is not written because I dare compare myself to Ji Kang; I truly cannot face my reflection and betray my heart, stealing only empty fame—hence these piled sheets. I never sought the Secretariat's tiger-claw board in the first place. Since childhood I have visited the household back and forth; morning calls and daily courtesies continue, and with all the nephews besides—how could I suddenly cut off congratulations and condolences? Yet the purpose of my life ends here entirely. Lord Ping of Jun said, "Those who give me a name will kill my body. " Even heavenly rank still destroys one's name—what use is gentleman of the ministry of personnel! This recommendation is base; the affair is irregular—not only will the gentry refuse to discuss it; even servants and concubines will laugh. Restless and unhappy, I know I cannot live long—at least let posterity know your younger brother does not sham illness.

Wei's prose was heavily archaizing and modulated; Yuan Shu, on reading it, said it sounded like a plea of wrongs suffered. Wei therefore wrote again to his cousin Sengchuo:

Though I lack the eye to judge men, I have known you long; whenever we feasted and talked, did I not always prize knowing when to stop? Holding fullness in fear and dreading excess is our family's old way—why plunge into such desolation at once? Those in power are painfully blind—will it not prove so! Could you really not have heard beforehand—or perhaps you truly did not know. There are many scions of hat and gown like me; my talent is hardly worth mentioning—only that I do not lean and overflow in deceit; gentlemen are somewhat tolerant of that. As for propriety in small matters, it is hard to reckon in detail. My rash illness grows daily; my wantonness grows worse; what human conduct esteems, I have abandoned and no longer cultivate. Fortunately the sage court tolerates me, placing me outside instruction, and old favors win me repeated indulgence. I too see that my rash illness has deeply invaded and I can hardly hold up; the pleasures of human life—my heart died to them long ago. So I steal days to survive on coarse cloth and coarse grain; half a night's sleep is my measure; I no longer speak of the ebb and flow of my vital forces; for long I have taken powdered drugs as my daily broth—does my younger brother not see this? My illness has reduced me to this; I mind only myself—above, not enough to harm public morals; below, not enough to disgrace the family; I live like a corpse, awaiting nothing. All these two or three points are facts. In writing to you I cannot deceive within the family. Zhouling's act has no cause I can see; turning it over in thought, I cannot understand it at all. Did he see my recent memorials? They are truly laughable.
18

I studied writing young, and in later years made a small advance; when the prince wished the people not to be remiss, he always added polish; in answering the great I am not weary of respect. Besides, if literary words lack grievous thought and modulation, they flow thin and tasteless. Writing that esteems antiquity values linking themes in moving grief; at first glance it seems to carry much meaning. When one sees that staying home is not seeking office, what the pure discussants reject—is that pleading grievance in general terms? That can truly be called scant in unadorned genuineness! I see guests on small guard now and then—when they come of themselves my gate is full—I do not trouble to raise auspicious signs alone. These people say it is where the tone of speech leads—not their main point. You need not dwell on this place today; at first all things do not reject one another—yet when Lu vessels are empty in Qi, it truly should be written on the sash. Now among the people of the Three Offices and Six Ministries, who shows inside and outside on this? If they suspect you have hidden influence, what does that matter to plain discussion? Then I will long be blocked undying and in the end mislead my prime of life.
19
江不過強吹拂吾，云是巖穴人。 巖穴人情所高，吾得當此，則雞鶩變作鳳皇，何為干飾廉隅，秩秩見於面目，所惜者大耳。 諸舍闔門皆蒙時私，此既未易陳道，故常因含聲不言。 至兄弟，尤為叨竊，臨海頻煩二郡，謙亦越進清階，吾高枕家巷，遂至中書郎，此足以闔棺矣。
Jiang merely blew hard at me, saying I am a cave-dweller. Cave-dwellers are what the age esteems; if I receive this label, chickens and ducks turn into phoenixes—why bother to polish clean corners and look orderly in the face? What one regrets is the larger matter. All our clan households receive private favor from the times; this is not easy to state, so I often keep silent. As for brothers, the favor is especially excessive: Linhai repeatedly held two commanderies; Qian also rose beyond his due into clear ranks; I rested at home and reached gentleman of the secretariat—this is enough to close the coffin.
20
又前年優旨，自弟所宣，雖夏后撫辜人，周宣及鰥寡，不足過也。 語皆循檢校跡，不為虛飾也。 作人不阿諛，無緣頭髮見白，稍學諂詐。 且吾何以為，足不能行，自不得出戶； 頭不耐風，故不可扶曳。 家本貧餧，至於惡衣蔬食，設使盜蹠居此，亦不能兩展其足，妄意珍藏也。 正令選官設作此舉，於吾亦無劍戟之傷，所以懃懃畏人之多言也。 管子晉賢，乃關人主之輕重，此何容易哉。 州陵亦自言視明聽聰，而返區區飾吾，何辯致而下英俊。 夫奇士必龍居深藏，與蛙蝦為伍，放勳其猶難之，林宗輩不足識也。 似不肯睠睠奉牋記，雕琢獻文章，居家近市廛，親戚滿城府，吾猶自知袁陽源輩當平此不？ 飾詐之與直獨，兩不關吾心，又何所耿介。 弟自宜以解塞群賢矣，兼悉怒此言自爾家任兄故能也。
Moreover last year's gracious edict was proclaimed by you; though Yu comforted the guilty and King Xuan reached widows and orphans, it does not surpass this. The words all follow what can be inspected and verified—not empty adornment. In conduct I do not flatter; without cause my hair has turned white—I have not slightly learned flattery and deceit. Moreover what am I fit for? My feet cannot walk, so I cannot leave the door; my head cannot bear wind, so I cannot be helped and dragged along. Our family was always poor and hungry, down to bad clothes and coarse food—even if Robber Zhi lived here, he could not stretch both feet twice and dream of hoarding treasure. Even if the selection office set up this act, it would not wound me with sword or halberd; what I earnestly fear is many people's talk. Guanzi was a worthy of Jin, yet it bore on the ruler's weight and lightness—this is no easy matter! Zhouling also says his sight is clear and hearing sharp, yet in return he fusses over adorning me—how does he argue down and yield to heroic worthies? A strange scholar must dwell hidden like a dragon, among frogs and shrimp; even Emperor Yao found it hard; men of Guo Linzong's sort are not enough to know him. As if unwilling to look back and offer memorials, carving essays to present, dwelling near the market with relatives filling the city offices—I still know whether Yuan Yangyuan's sort would level this or not? Adorning deceit and standing alone in genuineness—neither touches my heart; what is there to be stiff about? You should yourself use this to answer the crowd of worthies; I also know angry words come from your family's elder brother Ren's ability.

Day by day I hoped you would come; entrusted with illness I ultimately could not rise—my intent in writing to Jiang was roughly to lay out my heart; there was no one to write for me; only when we met face to face did I give it fully to you. The letter then felt complete; originally we were to meet half a day—I hate exertion and cannot speak much; pivots and keys are not my strength; meeting would not surpass reading this letter. If kinsmen wish to see it they may be shown; no need to hand it over in haste.

At the time some said that in Wei's recommendation He Yan of Lujiang also took part in the deliberation, feared Wei would blame him, and wrote to explain himself. Wei replied to him:

卿昔稱吾於義興，吾常謂之見知，然復自怪鄙野，不參風流，未有一介熟悉於事，何用獨識之也。 近日何見綽送卿書，雖知如戲，知卿固不能相哀。 苟相哀之未知，何相期之可論。
You once praised me at Yixing; I always called that being known—yet I also wondered at my rustic wildness, not joining the current, not having a single acquaintance versed in affairs—why single me out for special knowledge? Recently I saw Sengchuo send your letter; though I know it as if in jest, I know you truly cannot pity me. If pity is not yet known, how can expectation be discussed?
24

You young steeped in the Zhengshi style, refined and fluent—by nature a man of the Zhengshi era. I am truly a vulgar nature; my natural resolve and conduct do not double Wang Rong and Yue Guang. In childhood I was especially coarse and dull; I always followed the Erudite reading minor chapter-and-sentence texts—ultimately I gained nothing; stammering, unable to read aloud forcefully, I then gave up seeking learning. Around twenty I again drew near to reading minor tales; visitors saw several bundles of books at my bedside and said I studied—try inspecting them: what could there be? Yet again you hold this to judge people? I alone am ashamed to laugh at Yang Xiong's praise and bounty, yet also ashamed to take rhapsodies as a gentleman's work; if my seal-carving is base, it is very base indeed. You gentlemen too should especially blame me on this account. Some say my words are deep and broad, putting on a stretch of spirit, despising the human world—I at first did not dare so. Therefore whenever I see the world's literary rhapsodies, essays, and treatises, I have no yes or no; where I do not understand I ask the same day—this is my true heart.
25

As for lifelong fondness for taking superior drugs, it began when at twelve I was ill and weak. In the ingestion formulas I compiled, I have spoken of them roughly. From this I first believed regulated nourishment had effect; therefore winter asparagus and atractylodes I take in season as they come. Cold and warmth complement each other, intending to support my dangerously frail body, hoping to see white hair. The family is poor and lacks servants; even at the seasonal festivals of spring and autumn I myself take two or three disciples into the grass to gather them. I truly weary of wandering the medical departments, yet am quite versed in compounding drugs, especially trusting the Materia Medica, wishing it must be effective—therefore I personally attend, intent on taking the essence. The world then says I desire immortals and love the strange, admire and imitate unconstraint—unlike my family, quite a few revile me for it. Moreover by nature I know painting; it is also the mechanism by which the calling goose knows night; coiled and tangled in mind and eye—therefore I also love mountains and waters; going once to seek traces, all are imitations. I do not like calling on people; able to forget glory to avoid the powerful right—I should myself be secret in answering and bearing, and by curling up in shame protect myself—unable to force what is short in me. From of old there have been these several points about me; two or three worthies have again piled them up, exalted them to lofty heights, and sung them into clear ravines. Even tiles and rubble have their place; I dare not lightly rank them beside gold and silver.
26
而頃年嬰疾，沉淪無已，區區之情，愒於生存，自恐難復，而先命猥加，魂氣褰薾，常人不得作常自處疾苦，正亦臥思已熟，謂有記自論。 既仰天光，不夭庶類，兼望諸賢，共相哀體，而卿首唱誕言，布之翰墨，萬石之慎，或未然邪。 好盡之累，豈其如此。 綽大駭歎，便是闔朝見病者。 吾本儜人，加疹意惛，一旦聞此，便惶怖矣。 五六日來，復苦心痛，引喉狀如胸中悉腫，甚自憂。 力作此答，無復條貫，貴布所懷，落漠不舉。 卿既不可解，立欲便別，且當笑。
But in recent years I have been stricken with illness, sinking without end; petty feelings wearied at clinging to life, fearing I might not recover, yet fate has prematurely pressed down and my spirit droops weak—ordinary people cannot normally manage their own illness; lying abed I have thought it through and supposed there was some written self-account. Having received heaven's light and not died young among the multitude, I also hoped the worthies would together pity my condition—yet you first voiced extravagant words and spread them in ink; is the caution of ten thousand piculs perhaps not so after all? The burden of saying everything—could it really be so? Sengchuo was greatly startled and sighed; then the whole court sees the sick man. I am by nature a poor-spirited man; with rash mind confused, once I heard this I was at once terrified. For five or six days now I have again suffered heart pain; my throat constricts as if my whole chest were swollen—I am deeply worried. I forced myself to write this reply; it has no structure left. I value only laying bare what I harbor—desolate, I cannot lift myself. You are already beyond understanding and want to leave at once—for now you ought to laugh.
27

Emperor Wen, because of his skill at divination, bestowed upon him famous yarrow stalks. His younger brother Sengqian also had talent and reputation and served as Crown Prince Attendant. When he fell ill, Wei personally treated him, but Sengqian took the medicine in excess and died. Wei deeply blamed himself, fell ill and no longer treated himself, and could not cease grieving for Sengqian. He wrote to inform the spirit, saying:

My brother was fifteen when he first lodged away from home. He won no praise for keen insight, but drifted happily among books; when he heard the qin and its pieces, he remembered them at a glance. Probing literary canons and weighing biographies—before a season had passed, he was already accomplished enough to be spoken of. I have long been ill; whenever I had a slight respite, he would cite former records, no different from a trained scholar. From then he advanced day by day and month by month, famous in the district and clan, about to fulfill his early ambition and continue the beauty of former worthies—who thought that one morning he would suddenly go forever? Looking back on our lives together, it is only ten years at most. Yet apart from public business, we were never apart; a book of a single character—we had to chant and read it together; a text of a single line—we always studied and savored it together; muddy wine to forget sorrow, maps and books for consolation—the reason I could be poor yet not despair truly lay in this. Alas for cruel fate—I sit desolate and alone. I recall how in former years you would let your hair down and gaze into the distance with streaming tears; I would not leave you day or night, and always feared for my frail illness—who thought you would suddenly pass away and go first into the dark? Turning it over ten thousand times in my mind, there is no meeting again; your voice and face seem near, every memory vividly clear—where is my brother now? My grief has no end. When I served in the capital we were apart only sixty days in all; three times I mistakenly said, 'Why do you not come today?'—my heart hung on you, nothing could compare. We were just about to plan groves and marshes together to spend our remaining years—what guilt lay in this wish, to see such untimely cruelty? He perished by my hand; every memory stirs bitter regret. I have always loved the medical arts, yet did not let my younger brother be preserved whole; reflecting again that my skill was not refined and led to wrongful error—on this one point especially, the pain is cruel again. Cruel pain—what can be done! My guilt—what can be done!
30

In aspiration my brother served his parents with filial piety and his elder brother with obedience; even toward servants he never shouted or scolded—he may be called a gentleman who never lost his composure or his words before others. Gentle and penetrating, inwardly discerning right from wrong; in every movement and measure I always consulted him. He often said: 'Elder brother's writing has bone and spirit; you can extend its splendid beauty to claim for yourself. Also, as a man you are overly stiff and exacting—you should each time balance and harmonize yourself. Only ten sheets in his own hand, sealed and opened, still intact; as for longing, it cannot be contained in the breast. When he heard I was ill, his heart was torn inch by inch; he said the matter of a simple headcloth burial should be entrusted to you—who thought you would instead be the one to send me to the grave!

My brother long held that: 'Though a woman has no son, she should not enter a second household. If this custom spreads, every household can have a filial widow.' Zhongchang Cui's Discourses also states its main point. Lady Liu the new bride, by self-inflicted wound, swore herself—she must remain to provide offerings; Grand Consort Yin, moved by the integrity of the Bo Zhou ode, did not wrest away her resolve. The Director-in-chief was dutiful and obedient; Lady Fan understood ritual—they obtained the fifth son of the Left Leader; the Lu lineage seat now has its heir. What benefit is any of this to the dead in darkness—it is only for the sake of the living's feelings.
32
吾窮疾之人，平生意志，弟實知之。 端坐向窗，有何慰適，正賴弟耳。 過中未來，已自愒望，今云何得立，自省惛毒，無復人理。 比煩冤困憊，不能作刻石文，若靈響有識，不得吾文，豈不為恨。 儻意慮不遂，謝能思之如狂，不知所告訴，明書此數紙，無復詞理，略道阡陌，萬不寫一。 阿謙！ 何圖至此！ 誰復視我，誰復憂我！ 他日寶惜三光，割嗜好以祈年，今也唯速化耳。 吾豈復支，冥冥中竟復云何。 弟懷隨、和之寶，未及光諸文章，欲收作一集，不知忽忽當辦此不？ 今已成服，吾臨靈取常共飲杯，酌自釀酒，寧有仿像不？ 冤痛！ 冤痛！
I, a man worn out by illness, my lifelong intent—my brother truly knew it. Sitting upright facing the window—what comfort is there? I truly relied on my brother. Past midday you had not come—I was already desolate in hope; now how can you stand firm? Looking inward, I am confused and poisoned—no human reason remains. Lately vexing grief and weary exhaustion keep me from composing an inscribed stone text; if the spirit's echo has awareness and does not receive my writing, would that not be regret? If perchance intent and thought do not succeed, I can only brood on it like madness, not knowing whom to tell; I clearly write these several sheets, no more words or logic—roughly tracing the paths between fields, ten thousand parts, not one written down. A Qian! Who thought it would come to this! Who again will look on me? Who again will grieve for me! In other days I treasured sun, moon, and stars, cut off indulgences to pray for long life—now it is only swift transformation. How can I again support myself? In the darkness beyond, what shall there be? My brother harbored treasures like Sui and He jade, not yet brought to light in his writings; he wished to gather them into one collection—do you not know whether, in haste, this can still be done? Now the mourning garments are already donned; I approach the spirit and take the cup we always drank from together, pour self-brewed wine—is there any likeness at all? Wronged pain! Wronged pain!
33
元嘉三十年，卒，時年三十九。 僧謙卒後四旬而微終。 遺令薄葬，不設轜旐鼓挽之屬，施五尺床，為靈二宿便毀。 以嘗所彈琴置床上，何長史來，以琴與之。 何長史者，偃也。 無子。 家人遵之。 所著文集傳於世。 世祖即位，詔曰：「微棲志貞深，文行惇洽，生自華宗，身安隱素，足以賁茲丘園，惇是薄俗。 不幸蚤世，朕甚悼之。 可追贈秘書監。」
In the thirtieth year of Yuanjia he died, at the age of thirty-nine. Forty days after Sengqian's death, Wei passed away. His final instructions were for a simple burial, without funeral carriages, banners, drums, or pallbearers; provide a five-foot bed—for the spirit two nights, then destroy it. Place the qin he once played on the bed; when Chief Clerk He Yan comes, give him the qin. Director He is He Yan. He had no sons. The family followed his instructions. The collected writings he authored circulated in the world. When Emperor Xiaowu took the throne, an edict said: 'Wang Wei's lodged ambition was pure and deep, his writing and conduct sincere and harmonious; born of a splendid clan, his person secure in hidden simplicity—enough to adorn these hills and gardens and make thick this shallow custom. Unfortunate early death—We greatly mourn him. He may be posthumously enfeoffed as Director of the Secretariat.'
34
史臣曰：燕太子吐一言，田先生吞舌而死； 安邑令戒屠者，閔仲叔去而之沛。 良由內懷耿介，峻節不可輕干。 袁淑笑謔之間，而王微弔詞連牘，斯蓋好名之士，欲以身為珪璋，皦皦然使塵玷之累，不能加也。
The historian says: The Crown Prince of Yan spat one word, and Master Tian swallowed his tongue and died; the magistrate of Anyi warned the butcher, and Min Zhongshu left and went to Pei. Truly because within they harbored upright integrity—stern integrity that could not lightly be affronted. Between Yuan Shu's laughter and jest, yet Wang Wei's condolence letters ran sheet on sheet—this is probably a man who loves his name, wishing to make his person jade and regalia, bright and clear so that the burden of dust-stain cannot be added.

列傳第十六謝瞻孔琳之
Biographies 16: Xie Zhan and Kong Linzhi
2
謝瞻，字宣遠，一名簷，字通遠，陳郡陽夏人，衛將軍晦第三兄也。 年六歲，能屬文，為《紫石英贊》、《果然詩》，當時才士，莫不歎異。 初為桓偉安西參軍，楚台秘書郎。 瞻幼孤，叔母劉撫養有恩紀，兄弟事之，同於至親。 劉弟柳為吳郡，將姊俱行，瞻不能違，解職隨從，為柳建威長史。 尋為高祖鎮軍、琅邪王大司馬參軍，轉主簿，安成相，中書侍郎，宋國中書、黃門侍郎，相國從事中郎。
Xie Zhan, courtesy name Xuanyuan (also called Yan, with the courtesy name Tongyuan), was a native of Yangxia in Chen Commandery and the third elder brother of the General of the Guard, Xie Hui. At the age of six he could already write prose and poetry, producing "In Praise of Amethyst Quartz" and "On the Ginkgo Fruit"; contemporary literati all marveled at him. He began his career as a staff officer under Huan Wei in the western army, then became a secretariat clerk of the Chu court. Zhan lost his parents in childhood. His aunt-by-marriage, Lady Liu, had raised him with devoted kindness, and he and his brothers treated her exactly as they would their own parents. When Lady Liu's younger brother Liu was appointed administrator of Wu Commandery and took their elder sister with him, Zhan could not bring himself to stay behind. He resigned his post to accompany them and became chief clerk on Liu's staff as commander of the Jianwei army. He soon entered the service of the future Emperor Gaozu as a staff officer to the Grand Marshal of the Pacification Army, the Prince of Langye, then rose through a series of posts: chief clerk, chancellor of Ancheng, gentleman of the central secretariat, secretariat and palace attendant of the State of Song, and attendant of the chief minister of state.
3
弟晦時為宋台右衛，權遇已重，于彭城還都迎家，賓客輻輳，門巷填咽。 時瞻在家，驚駭謂晦曰：「汝名位未多，而人歸趣乃爾。 吾家以素退為業，不願干預時事，交遊不過親朋，而汝遂勢傾朝野，此豈門戶之福邪？ 」乃籬隔門庭，曰：「吾不忍見此。 」及還彭城，言于高祖曰：「臣本素士，父、祖位不過二千石。 弟年始三十，志用凡近，榮冠台府，位任顯密，福過災生，其應無遠。 特乞降黜，以保衰門。 」前後屢陳。 高祖以瞻為吳興郡，又自陳請，乃為豫章太守。 晦或以朝廷密事語瞻，瞻輒向親舊陳說，以為笑戲，以絕其言。 晦遂建佐命之功，任寄隆重，瞻愈憂懼。
At this time his younger brother Hui served as Right Guard of the Song regime, and his power and favor were already formidable. When he returned from Pengcheng to the capital to bring the family home, visitors swarmed in such numbers that the lanes before his gate were jammed. Zhan was at home and, alarmed, said to Hui, "Your fame and rank are not yet great, yet people are flocking to you like this. Our family has always lived by modest withdrawal from the world. We do not wish to meddle in affairs of the day, and our circle extends no farther than kin and friends—yet you have made your influence tip the entire court and countryside. Can this truly be a blessing to our house?" He then put up a fence across the gate and courtyard, saying, "I cannot bear to see this." When he returned to Pengcheng he spoke to Gaozu: "I was originally a plain scholar; my father and grandfather held posts no higher than the two-thousand-dan rank. My brother is barely thirty, his aims and abilities are unremarkable, yet glory crowns the government offices and his position is conspicuous and close to power. When fortune exceeds its measure, disaster is sure to follow—the omen cannot be far off. I beg especially that he be lowered in rank, to preserve our declining house." He made this plea again and again. Gaozu appointed Zhan administrator of Wuxing Commandery, but Zhan petitioned again on his own behalf and was then made governor of Yuzhang instead. When Hui would sometimes confide court secrets to Zhan, Zhan would immediately repeat them to relatives and friends as a joke, to put an end to such disclosures. Hui then earned merit in founding the dynasty, and the trust and authority placed in him grew weighty. Zhan became all the more anxious and afraid.
4
永初二年，在郡遇疾，不肯自治，幸於不永。 晦聞疾奔往，瞻見之，曰：「汝為國大臣，又總戎重，萬里遠出，必生疑謗。 」時果有訴告晦反者。 瞻疾篤還都，高祖以晦禁旅，不得出宿，使瞻居於晉南郡公主婿羊賁故第，在領軍府東門。 瞻曰：「吾有先人弊廬，何為於此！ 」臨終，遣晦書曰：「吾得啟體幸全，歸骨山足，亦何所多恨。 弟思自勉厲，為國為家。 」遂卒，時年三十五。
In the second year of Yongchu, while serving in his commandery he fell ill and refused to treat himself, hoping his life might not be prolonged. When Hui heard of the illness he rushed to his side. Zhan, seeing him, said, "You are a great minister of state and also hold overall military command. To travel a thousand li on such an errand is sure to breed suspicion and slander." At the time, as it turned out, there were indeed accusations lodged against Hui for rebellion. Zhan's illness grew grave and he returned to the capital. Because Hui was confined to the garrison and could not lodge elsewhere, Gaozu had Zhan stay in the former residence of Yang Ben, son-in-law of the Princess of Jin's Southern Commandery, east of the headquarters of the Headquarters Army. Zhan said, "I have my ancestors' humble old dwelling—why should I stay here!" On his deathbed he sent Hui a letter saying, "I have been able to leave my body whole and fortunate; to return my bones to the foot of the mountains—what more could I regret? Brother, strive to discipline yourself for the sake of the state and of our family." He then died, at the age of thirty-five.
5
瞻善於文章，辭采之美，與族叔混、族弟靈運相抗。 靈運父瑛，無才能。 為秘書郎，早年而亡。 靈運好臧否人物，混患之，欲加裁折，未有方也。 謂瞻曰：「非汝莫能。 」乃與晦、曜、弘微等共遊戲，使瞻與靈運共車； 靈運登車，便商較人物，瞻謂之曰：「秘書早亡，談者亦互有同異。 」靈運默然，言論自此衰止。
Zhan excelled at literary composition; the brilliance of his style rivaled that of his clan uncle Xie Hun and his cousin Xie Lingyun. Lingyun's father Ying had no ability or talent. He had served as a secretariat gentleman and died young. Lingyun liked to pass judgment on people; Hun was troubled by this and wished to rein him in but had no way to do so. He said to Zhan, "No one but you can do it." So together with Hui, Yao, Hongwei, and others they went out on pleasure outings, arranging for Zhan and Lingyun to share a carriage. When Lingyun boarded the carriage he at once began debating people's merits and faults. Zhan said to him, "The Secretariat gentleman died young; those who discuss him also disagree among themselves." Lingyun fell silent, and from then on such talk gradually ceased.
6
弟㬭，字宣鏡，幼有殊行。 年數歲，所生母郭氏，久嬰痼疾，晨昏溫清，嘗藥捧膳，不闕一時，勤容戚顏，未嘗暫改。 恐僕役營疾懈倦，躬自執勞。 母為病畏驚，微踐過甚，一家尊卑，感㬭至性，鹹納屢而行，屏氣而語，如此者十餘年。 初為州主簿，中軍行參軍，太子舍人，俄遷秘書丞。 自以兄居權貴，己蒙超擢，固辭不就。 徐羨之請為司空長史，黃門郎。 元嘉三年，從坐伏誅，時年三十一。 有詔宥其子世平，又早卒，無後。
His younger brother Can, courtesy name Xuanjing, showed extraordinary conduct from childhood. When he was only a few years old, his birth mother Lady Guo suffered a chronic illness for many years. Morning and evening he attended her with warm and cool comfort, tasted her medicine and presented her meals, never missing an hour. His diligent bearing and sorrowful countenance never changed even briefly. Fearing that servants caring for the sick would grow slack and weary, he personally performed the labor himself. His mother, in her illness, feared being startled; the slightest footfall was too much. High and low throughout the household, moved by Can's utmost filial devotion, all walked in thick-soled slippers and spoke while holding their breath—thus for more than ten years. He first served as chief clerk of the province, marching staff officer of the Central Army, and attendant of the Heir Apparent, and soon was promoted to secretariat director. Considering that his elder brother held power and rank and he himself had received a leap in promotion, he firmly refused to accept the post. Xu Xianzhi requested him as chief clerk of the Minister of Works and as Gentleman of the Yellow Gate. In the third year of Yuanjia he was executed by association with his brother's crime, at the age of thirty-one. An edict pardoned his son Shiping, but he too died young and left no heirs.
7
孔琳之，字彥琳，會稽人。 祖沈，晉丞相掾。 父廞，光祿大夫。 琳之強正有志力，好文義，解音律，能彈棋，妙善草隸。 郡命主簿，不就，後辟本國常侍。 桓玄輔政為太尉，以為西閣祭酒。 桓玄時議欲廢錢用穀帛，琳之議曰：「《洪範》八政，以貨次食，豈不以交易之所資，為用之至要者乎？ 若使不以交易，百姓用力于為錢，則是妨其為生之業，禁之可也。 今農自務穀，工自務器，四民各肄其業，何嘗致勤于錢。 故聖王制無用之貨，以通有用之財，既無毀敗之費，又省運置之苦，此錢所以嗣功龜貝，歷代不廢者也。 穀帛為寶，本充衣食，今分以為貨，則致損甚多。 又勞毀于商販之手，耗棄於割截之用，此之為敝，著於自曩。 故鐘繇曰：'巧偽之民，競蘊濕穀以要利，制薄絹以充資。 '魏世制以嚴刑，弗能禁也。 是以司馬芝以為用錢非徒豐國，亦所以省刑。 錢之不用，由於兵亂積久，自至於廢，有由而然，漢末是也。 今既用而廢之，則百姓頓亡其財。 今括囊天下之谷，以周天下之食，或倉庾充衍，或糧靡鬥儲，以相資通，則貧者仰富，致之之道，實假于錢。 一朝斷之，便為棄物，是有錢無糧之民，皆坐而饑困，此斷錢之立敝也。 且據今用錢之處不為貧，用穀之處不為富。 又民習來久，革之必惑。 語曰：'利不百，不易業。 '況又錢便於穀邪？ 魏明帝時，錢廢穀用，三十年矣。 以不便於民，乃舉朝大議。 精才達治之士，莫不以為宜複用錢，民無異情，朝無異論。 彼尚舍穀帛而用錢，足以明穀帛之弊，著於已試。 世或謂魏氏不用錢久，積累巨萬，故欲行之，利公富國。 斯殆不然。 昔晉文後舅犯之謀，而先成季之信，以為雖有一時之勳，不如萬世之益。 于時名賢在列，君子盈朝，大謀天下之利害，將定經國之要術。 若谷實便錢，義不昧當時之近利，而廢永用之通業，斷可知矣。 斯實由困而思革，改而更張耳。 近孝武之末，天下無事，時和年豐，百姓樂業，便自谷帛殷阜，幾乎家給人足，驗之事實，錢又不妨民也。 頃兵革屢興，荒饉薦及，饑寒未振，實此之由。 公既援而拯之，大革視聽，弘敦本之教，明廣農之科，敬授民時，各順其業，遊蕩知反，務末自休，固以南畝競力，野無遺壤矣。 於是以往，升平必至，何衣食之足恤。 愚謂救弊之術，無取于廢錢。」
Kong Linzhi, courtesy name Yanlin, was a native of Kuaiji. His grandfather Shen served as a staff officer to the Jin chancellor of state. His father Yin served as Grand Master for Splendid Happiness. Linzhi was forceful, upright, and full of resolve. He loved literature and moral principle, understood music, could play chess, and excelled at cursive and clerical script. The commandery chief summoned him as chief clerk, but he did not accept; later he was recruited as regular attendant of his native kingdom. When Huan Xuan assisted in government as Grand Marshal, he appointed Linzhi western-pavilion libationer. At the time Huan Xuan was debating abolishing coin and using grain and cloth instead. Linzhi argued, "In the Eight Policies of the Hongfan, wealth ranks next to food—is this not because the medium of exchange is the most essential instrument of economic life? If the people were not to use it for exchange but instead expended their labor on making money, that would obstruct their livelihood—then prohibition would be fitting. Today farmers devote themselves to grain, artisans to tools; the four classes each pursue their own occupations—when have they ever been diligent about minting money? Therefore the sage kings fashioned tokens of no direct use to circulate goods of real value, avoiding spoilage and sparing the hardship of transport and storage—this is why money succeeded tortoise shells and cowries and has not been abandoned through the ages. Grain and cloth are treasures meant chiefly for food and clothing; to divide them for use as currency causes very great waste. Moreover they are worn out in merchants' hands and wasted in cutting and trimming for use as payment—the harm of this has been evident since antiquity. Therefore Zhong Yao said, "Crafty and deceitful people compete to hoard damp grain for profit and weave thin silk to serve as currency." In the Wei era they imposed severe punishments yet could not prohibit it. Therefore Sima Zhi held that using money not only enriches the state but also reduces punishments. The disuse of money came from prolonged military disorder and led in time to its abandonment—there was a reason for it, as at the end of the Han. Now that it is in use, to abolish it would make the common people suddenly lose their wealth. If one were to gather all the grain under heaven to supply all the food under heaven, some granaries would overflow while others would lack even a peck in store. To aid one another through exchange, the poor rely on the rich—and the means of achieving this in fact depends on money. To cut it off in a single day would turn it into worthless goods. Those who have money but no grain would all sit idle in hunger and distress—this is the immediate harm of abolishing money. Moreover, judging from the present, places that use money are not poor, and places that use grain are not rich. The people have been accustomed to it for a long time; to change it would surely breed confusion. A saying runs, "Unless the profit is a hundredfold, one does not change one's occupation." How much more convenient is money than grain?" In the time of Emperor Ming of Wei, money was abandoned and grain used—for thirty years. Because it was inconvenient for the people, the whole court held a great debate. Men of refined talent versed in governance all held that money ought to be used again; the people had no dissenting sentiment, the court no dissenting opinion. They still set aside grain and cloth to use money—enough to show the defects of grain and cloth, as demonstrated by what had already been tried. Some say that because the Wei had not used money for so long and had amassed vast stores, they wished to restore it to benefit the public treasury and enrich the state. This is probably not so. Formerly Duke Wen of Jin put Jiu Fan's counsel behind him and first fulfilled Ji Zi's pledge, holding that though there might be merit for a moment, it could not compare with benefit for ten thousand generations. At that time famous worthies were in the ranks and gentlemen filled the court. They deliberated on the benefits and harms under heaven and were about to fix the essential methods of governing the state. If grain had truly been more convenient than money, they would not, in principle, have obscured the near-term profit of the moment and abandoned the enduring common practice—it can be known for certain. This was in truth because hardship made them think of reform and change the established order. Near the end of Emperor Xiaowu's reign, the realm was at peace, the seasons harmonious and the years abundant, and the people were content in their occupations. Grain and cloth were plentiful of themselves, and households were nearly provided for—verified by the facts, money also did not obstruct the people. Recently arms have been raised repeatedly, famine has come in succession, and hunger and cold have not been relieved—this is truly the cause. Your Grace has already aided and rescued the people, greatly reforming what they see and hear, spreading the teaching of honoring the root, clarifying statutes to expand agriculture, respectfully granting the seasons to the people so each may follow his occupation. The idle know to return, those pursuing secondary trades rest of themselves—surely the southern fields compete in effort and no land in the wilds lies unused. From this going forward, an age of ascent and peace is sure to come—why need one worry about sufficient food and clothing? I hold that among methods to remedy harm, abolishing money should not be adopted."
8
玄又議複肉刑，琳之以為：「唐、虞象刑，夏禹立辟，蓋淳薄既異，致化實同，寬猛相濟，惟變所適。 《書》曰'刑罰世輕世重'，言隨時也。 夫三代風純而事簡，故罕蹈刑辟； 季末俗巧而務殷，故動陷憲網。 若三千行于叔世，必有踴貴之尤，此五帝不相循法，肉刑不可悉複者也。 漢文發仁惻之意，傷自新之路莫由，革古創制，號稱刑厝，然名輕而實重，反更傷民。 故孝景嗣位，輕之以緩。 緩而民慢，又不禁邪，期於刑罰之中，所以見美在昔，歷代詳論而未獲厥中者也。 兵荒後，罹法更多。 棄市之刑，本斬右趾，漢文一謬，承而弗革，所以前賢恨恨，議之而未辯。 鐘繇、陳群之意，雖小有不同，而欲右趾代棄市。 若從其言，則所活者眾矣。 降死之生，誠為輕法，然人情慎顯而輕昧，忽遠而驚近，是以盤盂有銘，韋弦作佩，況在小人，尤其所惑，或目所不睹，則忽而不戒，日陳於前，則驚心駭矚。 由此言之，重之不必不傷，輕之不必不懼，而可以全其性命，蕃其產育，仁既濟物，功亦益眾。 又今之所患，逋逃為先，屢叛不革，逃身靡所，亦以肅戒未犯，永絕惡原。 至於餘條，宜依舊制。 豈曰允中，貴獻管穴。」
Xuan again debated restoring corporal punishments. Linzhi held, "Tang and Yu used symbolic punishments; Yu of Xia established statutes—probably because simplicity and sophistication had already differed, yet the achievement of transformation was in fact the same. Leniency and severity complement each other; only change according to what is fitting. The Documents says, "Punishments in one age are light, in another heavy"—meaning they follow the times. In the Three Dynasties customs were pure and affairs simple, so people rarely fell into criminal statutes. At the end of an age customs grow crafty and affairs numerous, so people constantly fall into the net of law. If the three thousand statutes were applied in a declining age, there would surely be the excess of Yong and Gui—this is why the Five Emperors did not follow one another's laws, and corporal punishments cannot all be restored. Emperor Wen of Han expressed humane compassion, grieving that the path of self-renewal had no outlet. He reformed the ancient and created new institutions, claiming punishments were laid aside—yet the name was light while the reality was heavy, and in the end the people were harmed all the more. Therefore when Emperor Jing succeeded to the throne, he lightened and softened them. When softened the people grew lax, yet evil was not restrained. Aiming at the mean in punishments—this is why it was praised in the past, yet through the ages detailed debate has not attained the true center. After the turmoil of war, those who fall under the law are more numerous. The punishment of abandonment in the market originally meant cutting off the right foot. Emperor Wen made one error and later ages followed without reform—this is why former worthies grieved and debated it without resolving it. The intent of Zhong Yao and Chen Qun, though slightly differing, was to have cutting off the right foot replace abandonment in the market. If their words had been followed, many more would have lived. Reducing death to life is indeed a lightening of the law, yet human nature is cautious of what is visible and careless of what is obscure, neglects what is distant and is startled by what is near—therefore bowls and dishes bear inscriptions and bowstrings are worn as pendants. How much more with petty men, whom this especially deludes: what the eye does not see they neglect and do not guard against; what is set before them day after day startles the heart and fixes the gaze. From this it follows that making punishments heavy does not necessarily prevent harm, nor making them light necessarily prevent fear—yet one can preserve their lives and increase their offspring. Benevolence has already aided things, and the merit would also benefit the multitude. Moreover what is feared today puts flight and evasion first: repeated rebellion without reform, the fugitive with nowhere to turn—this too is to make severity a warning before offense and cut off the source of evil forever. As for the remaining articles, the old system should be followed. How can one say this hits the exact center? I value offering the view from a narrow tube."
9
玄好人附悅，而琳之不能順旨，是以不見知。 遷楚台員外散騎侍郎。 遭母憂，去職。 服闋，除司徒左西掾，以父致仕自解。 時司馬休之為會稽內史、後將軍，仍以琳之為長史。 父憂，去官。 服闋，補太尉主簿，尚書左丞，揚州治中從事史，所居著績。
Xuan liked men who attached themselves to please him, but Linzhi could not follow his intent—therefore he was not regarded with favor. He was transferred to extraordinary attendant of the scattered cavalry of the Chu regime. When his mother died he left his post. When mourning ended he was appointed western assistant of the left branch of the Secretariat, but resigned because his father had retired from office. At the time Sima Xiuzhi served as interior administrator of Kuaiji and Rear General, and Linzhi was again made his chief clerk. When his father died he left office. When mourning ended he was appointed chief clerk to the Grand Marshal, left assistant in the Masters of Writing, and supervising clerk of Yang Province. Wherever he served he achieved notable results.
10
時責眾官獻便宜，議者以為宜修庠序，恤典刑，審官方，明黜陟，舉逸拔才，務農簡調。 琳之於眾議之外，別建言曰：「夫璽印者，所以辯章官爵，立契符信。 官莫大于皇帝，爵莫尊于公侯。 而傳國之璽，歷代迭用，襲封之印，奕世相傳，貴在仍舊，無取改作。 今世唯尉一職，獨用一印，至於內外群官，每遷悉改，討尋其義，私所未達。 若謂官各異姓，與傳襲不同，則未若異代之為殊也。 若論其名器，雖有公卿之貴，不若帝王之重； 若以或有誅夷之臣，忌其凶穢，則漢用秦璽； 延祚四百，未聞以子嬰身戮國亡，而棄之不佩。 帝王公侯之尊，不疑於傳璽，人臣眾僚之卑，何嫌于即印。 載籍未聞其說，推例自乖其准。 而終年刻鑄，喪功肖實，金銀銅炭之費，不可稱言，非所以因循舊貫易簡之道。 愚謂眾官即用一印，無煩改作。 若有新置官，又官多印少，文或零失，然後乃鑄，則仰裨天府，非唯小益。」
At the time all officials were required to submit practical proposals. The debaters held that schools should be restored, proper punishments upheld, official posts examined, demotions and promotions clarified, recluses recommended and talent elevated, agriculture promoted and corvée lightened. Apart from the common proposals, Linzhi separately advised, "Seals and stamps are means to distinguish offices and ranks and to establish contracts and tokens of trust. No office is greater than that of the emperor, no rank more honored than duke or marquis. Yet the seal that transmits the state is used in succession through the ages, and the seal of hereditary enfeoffment is handed down generation after generation—the value lies in continuing the old, not in making new ones. In the present age only the post of commandant uses a single seal throughout. As for all other officials within and without, at every transfer the seal is changed entirely—searching for the meaning of this, I have not grasped it. If one says that offices each have different surnames and differ from hereditary transmission, that is still not as great a difference as between different dynasties. If one discusses titles and regalia, though there is the honor of dukes and ministers, it does not compare with the weight of emperors and kings. If one fears the ill omen of ministers who were executed and exterminated, then Han used the Qin seal. Its fortune extended four hundred years, and one never heard that because Ziying was put to death and the state perished, the seal was cast aside and not worn. The honor of emperors, kings, dukes, and marquis is not doubted in transmitting the seal—why should the lowliness of ministers and the multitude of officials be troubled by using the seal of the moment? Records have not heard this doctrine; pushing the analogy, it itself violates the standard. Yet year-round carving and casting wastes labor to no real gain. The expense in gold, silver, copper, and charcoal cannot be reckoned—this is not the way to follow old practice and simplify. I hold that all officials should use one seal without the trouble of remaking it. If there is a newly established office, or many offices and few seals, or documents lost or damaged, only then should casting be done—then it would aid the imperial treasury, not merely bring small benefit."
11
又曰：「凶門柏裝，不出禮典，起自末代，積習生常，遂成舊俗。 爰自天子，達于庶人，誠行之有由，卒革必駭。 然苟無關於情，而有愆禮度，存之未有所明，去之未有所失，固當式遵先典，厘革後謬，況複兼以游費，實為民患者乎！ 凡人士喪儀，多出閭裏，每有此須，動十數萬，損民財力，而義無所取。 至於寒庶，則人思自竭，雖複室如懸磬，莫不傾產殫財，所謂葬之以禮，其若此乎。 謂宜謹遵先典，一罷凶門之式，表以素扇，足以示凶。」
He also said, "The cypress fittings of the Gate of Mourning do not appear in the ritual canon. They arose in the late age, accumulated habit became custom, and thus formed an old practice. From the Son of Heaven down to the common man, it is true that there is reason for practicing it; to abolish it suddenly would surely cause alarm. Yet if it has no relation to true feeling but violates ritual measure, to keep it clarifies nothing, to remove it loses nothing—one should surely follow the former canon and reform later errors. How much more when combined with wasteful expenditure—it truly harms the people! As for funeral rites among gentlemen, most come from village custom. Whenever this is required, the cost often runs to tens of thousands, damaging the people's wealth and strength while gaining nothing in principle. As for the poor and humble, each person thinks to exhaust himself. Though his house be empty as hanging bells, none fail to pour out property and spend all wealth—is this what is meant by burying with ritual? I hold that one should strictly follow the former canon, abolish entirely the form of the Gate of Mourning, and display plain white fans—enough to show mourning."
12
又曰：「昔事故饑荒，米穀綿絹皆貴，其後米價登複，而絹於今一倍。 綿絹既貴，蠶業者滋，雖勤厲兼倍，而貴猶不息。 愚謂致此，良有其由。 昔事故之前，軍器正用鎧而已，至於袍襖裲襠，必俟戰陣，實在庫藏，永無損毀。 今儀從直衛及邀羅使命，或有防衛送迎，悉用袍襖之屬，非唯一府，眾軍皆然。 綿帛易敗，勢不支久。 又晝以禦寒，夜以寢臥，曾未周年，便自敗裂。 每絲綿新登，易折租以市，又諸府競收，動有千萬，積貴不已，實由於斯，私服為脂艱貴，官庫為之空盡。 愚謂若侍衛所須，固不可廢，其餘則依舊用鎧。 小小使命送迎之屬，止宜給仗，不煩鎧襖。 用之既簡，則其價自降」
He also said, "Formerly because of events there was famine; grain and silk were all dear. Afterward grain prices rose and returned, but silk is now double. Since silk is dear, those in sericulture multiply. Though diligence is doubled, the dearness still does not cease. I hold that bringing this about truly has its cause. Before the former troubles, military equipment properly used only armor. As for robes, padded coats, and vests, they awaited battle and were stored in the arsenal, never damaged. Today the ceremonial guard, direct guards, and envoys on patrol missions, or those escorting and receiving guests, all use robes and padded coats—not only one office; all armies are the same. Silk cloth is easily ruined and cannot last long by its nature. Moreover they are worn by day against cold and by night for sleep. Before a full year they are already torn apart. Whenever fresh silk comes to market, rents are exchanged to buy it. Moreover the various offices compete to collect it, often in tens of millions—the accumulated dearness never stops, truly because of this. Private dress becomes grievously dear and the official storehouses are emptied for it. I hold that what the guards require certainly cannot be abolished. For the rest, follow the old use of armor. For minor missions of escort and reception, it is enough to supply weapons; there is no need for armor and padded coats. If use is simplified, the price will naturally fall."
13
又曰：「夫不恥惡食，唯君子能之。 肴饌尚奢，為日久矣。 今雖改張是弘，而此風未革。 所甘不過一味，而陳必方丈，適口之外，皆為悅目之費，富者以之示誇，貧者為之殫產，眾所同鄙，而莫能獨異。 愚謂宜粗為其品，使奢儉有中； 若有不改，加以貶黜，則德儉之化，不日而流。」
He also said, "Not being ashamed of coarse food—only the gentleman can do this. Delicacies have honored extravagance for a long time. Today though this reform is proclaimed, this custom has not been changed. What pleases the palate is no more than one flavor, yet what is set out must fill a square zhang. Beyond what suits the mouth, all is expense to please the eye. The rich use it to show off; the poor exhaust their estates for it. All alike despise it, yet none can stand apart. I hold that one should roughly set grades for it, so that extravagance and frugality have a mean. If some do not reform, add demotion and censure—then the transformation of virtuous frugality will flow abroad within days."
14
遷尚書吏部郎。 義熙六年，高祖領平西將軍，以為長史，大司馬琅邪王從事中郎。 又除高祖平北、征西長史，遷侍中。 宋台初建，除宋國侍中。 出為吳興太守，公事免。
He was transferred to gentleman of the Ministry of Personnel in the Masters of Writing. In the sixth year of Yixi, Gaozu held the post of Pacifying-West General and made him chief clerk and attendant of the Grand Marshal, the Prince of Langye. He was also appointed chief clerk to Gaozu's Pacifying-North and Campaigning-West armies and was promoted to palace attendant. When the Song regime was first established, he was appointed palace attendant of the State of Song. He went out as governor of Wuxing and was dismissed for public business.
15
永初二年，為禦史中丞。 明憲直法，無所屈橈。 奏劾尚書令徐羨之曰：「臣聞事上以奉憲為恭，臨下以威嚴為整。 然後朝典惟明，蒞眾必肅。 斯道或替，則憲綱其頹。 臣以今月七日，預皇太子正會。 會畢車去，並猥臣停門待闕。 有何人乘馬，當臣車前，收捕驅遣命去。 何人罵詈收捕，咨審欲錄。 每有公事，臣常慮有紛紜，語令勿問，而何人獨罵不止，臣乃使錄。 何人不肯下馬，連叫大喚，有兩威儀走來，擊臣收捕。 尚書令省事倪宗又牽威儀手力，擊臣下人。 宗雲：'中丞何得行兇，敢錄令公人。 凡是中丞收捕，威儀悉皆縛取。 '臣敕下人一不得鬥，凶勢輈張，有頃乃散。 又有群人就臣車側，錄收捕樊馬子，互行築馬子頓伏，不能還台。 臣自錄非，本無對校，而宗敢乘勢凶恣，篡奪罪身。 尚書令臣羨之，與臣列車，紛紜若此，或雲羨之不禁，或雲羨之禁而不止。 縱而不禁，既乖國憲； 禁而不止，又不經通。 陵犯監司，凶聲彰赫，容縱宗等，曾無糾問，虧損國威，無大臣之體，不有準繩，風裁何寄。 羨之內居朝右，外司輦轂，位任隆重，百辟所瞻。 而不能弘惜朝章，肅是風軌。 致使宇下縱肆，淩暴憲司，凶赫之聲，起自京邑，所謂己有短垣，而自逾之。 又宗為篡奪之主，縱不糾問，二三虧違，宜有裁貶。 請免羨之所居官，以公還第。 宗等篡奪之愆，已屬掌故禦史隨事檢處。 」詔曰：「小人難可檢禦，司空無所問，餘如奏。 」羨之任居朝端，不欲以犯憲示物。 時羨之領揚州刺史，琳之弟璩之為治中，羨之使璩之解釋琳之，停寢其事。 琳之不許。 璩之固陳，琳之謂曰：「我觸忤宰相，正當罪止一身爾，汝必不應從坐，何須勤勤邪！ 」自是百僚震肅莫敢犯禁。 高祖甚嘉之，行經蘭台，親加臨幸。 又領本州大中正，遷祠部尚書。 不治產業，家尤貧素。 景平元年，卒，時年五十五。 追贈太常。
In the second year of Yongchu he became imperial censor. He clarified statutes and applied the law straight, yielding to nothing. He memorialized to impeach the Minister of Writing, Xu Xianzhi: "I have heard that serving superiors with observance of law is respect, and facing inferiors with awe and severity is order. Only then are court statutes clear and those who preside over the multitude surely stern. If this way is neglected, the net of law collapses. Your subject on the seventh day of this month attended the Crown Prince's regular assembly. When the assembly ended the carriages departed. I and other humble officials waited at the gate for audience. Some man on horseback blocked before my carriage. I had him seized and driven away and ordered him to leave. The man cursed the arresting officers. I questioned him and intended to record him. Whenever there is public business I often fear disorder and tell them not to inquire—but this man alone kept cursing without stop, so I had him recorded. The man would not dismount and kept shouting loudly. Two guards of honor came running and struck my arresting officers. Ni Zong, clerk of the Minister of Writing, again pulled the guards' strong arms and struck my subordinates. Zong said, "How can the censor commit violence and dare to record the minister's men? Whenever the censor arrests anyone, the guards of honor are all bound and taken." I ordered my subordinates not to fight at all. The violent force spread wildly and only after a while dispersed. Moreover a crowd came to the side of my carriage and recorded the arrest of Fan Mazi. They mutually beat Fan Mazi until he collapsed prostrate and could not return to the bureau. I myself recorded the offense; originally there was no confrontation—but Zong dared to ride the momentum with violent license and seize the criminal person. The Minister of Writing, your subject Xianzhi, was in the same row of carriages. The disorder was like this—some say Xianzhi did not restrain it, some say he restrained but did not stop it. To indulge without restraining already violates the national statutes. To restrain without stopping is also not according to proper communication. Insulting the supervising office, the sound of violence resounding, indulging Zong and the rest without any inquiry or correction, damaging national dignity, lacking the bearing of a great minister—without standards and measures, where can style and discipline be placed? Xianzhi within holds the right hand of court, without administers the capital region. His position and trust are weighty; the hundred officials look to him. Yet he cannot broadly cherish the court regulations and make this standard of conduct stern. He causes those under heaven to run riot, insulting and bullying the office of law. The resounding violence arose from the capital—what is called having a low wall and yet leaping over it oneself. Moreover Zong was chief in the seizure. Even if not inquired into, the two or three breaches should receive judgment and demotion. I request that Xianzhi be removed from the office he holds and return to his mansion with only his noble rank. The offense of Zong and the rest in the seizure already belongs to the supervising censor for handling according to the matter." The edict said, "Petty men are hard to inspect and control. The Minister of Works is not to be questioned; the rest as memorialized. Xianzhi's position stood at the head of court. He did not wish to display violation of law to others. At the time Xianzhi also held the post of governor of Yang Province. Linzhi's younger brother Juanzhi was supervising clerk. Xianzhi had Juanzhi explain matters to Linzhi and halt the case. Linzhi would not permit it. Juanzhi pressed the point firmly. Linzhi said to him, "I have offended the chief minister—the punishment should stop at me alone. You certainly should not be punished by association—why need you plead so earnestly!" From this the hundred officials were awed into sternness and none dared violate the prohibitions. Gaozu greatly praised him. Passing by the Orchid Terrace he personally went to visit. He also held the post of chief assessor of his native province and was transferred to Minister of the Imperial Ancestral Temple. He did not manage estates. His household was especially poor and plain. In the first year of Jingping he died, at the age of fifty-five. He was posthumously enfeoffed as Grand Master of Splendid Happiness.
16
子邈，有父風，官至揚州治中從事史。 邈子覬，別有傅。 覬弟道存，世祖大明中，曆黃門吏部郎，臨海王子頊前軍長史、南郡太守。 晉安王子勳建偽號，為侍中，行雍州事。 事敗自殺。
His son Miao had his father's manner and reached the post of supervising clerk of Yang Province. Miao's son Ji has a separate biography. Ji's younger brother Daocun, in the Daming era of Emperor Shizu, successively held the posts of gentleman of the Yellow Gate and Ministry of Personnel, chief clerk of the Vanguard Army of the Prince of Linhai Zixu, and governor of Nan Commandery. When the Prince of Jin'an Zixun established a false reign title, he served as palace attendant and acted for Yong Province. When the affair failed he killed himself.
17
史臣曰：民生所貴，曰食與貨。 貨以通幣，食為民天。 是以九棘播于農皇，十朋興於上代。 昔醇民未離，情嗜疏寡，奉生贍己，事有易周。 一夫躬稼，則餘食委室； 匹婦務織，則兼衣被體。 雖懋遷之道，通用濟乏，龜貝之益，為功蓋輕。 而事有訛變，奸敝代起，昏作役苦，故穡人去而從商，商子事逸，末業流而浸廣，泉貨所通，非複始造之意。 於是競收罕至之珍，遠蓄未名之貨，明珠翠羽，無足而馳，絲罽文犀，飛不待翼，天下蕩蕩，鹹以棄本為事。 豐衍則同多稌之資，饑凶又減田家之蓄。 錢雖盈尺，既不療饑於堯年； 貝或如輪，信無救渴于湯世，其蠹病亦已深矣。 固宜一罷錢貨，專用穀帛，使民知役生之路，非此莫由。 夫千匹為貨，事難於懷璧； 萬斛為市，未易於越鄉，斯可使末伎自禁，遊食知反。 而年世推移，民與事習，或庫盈朽貫，而高廩未充，或家有藏鏹，而良疇罕辟。 若事改一朝，廢而莫用，交易所寄，旦夕無待，雖致乎要術，而非可卒行。 先宜削華止偽，還淳反古，抵璧幽峰，捐珠清壑。 然後驅一世之民，反耕桑之路，使縑粟羨溢，同於水火。 既而蕩滌圓法，銷鑄勿遺，立制垂統，永傳於後，比屋稱仁，豈伊唐世。 桓玄知其始而不覽其終，孔琳之睹其末而不統其本，豈慮有開塞，將一往之談可然乎。
The historian says: What the people's lives value are called food and goods. Goods circulate currency; food is the people's heaven. Therefore the nine thorns were spread by the Agricultural Sovereign, and the ten companions arose in high antiquity. Formerly when the pure people had not yet separated, feelings and desires were sparse and few. Sustaining life and supporting oneself were matters easy to complete. If one man personally plowed, surplus food filled the room. If one woman worked at weaving, then multiple garments clothed the body. Though the way of vigorous exchange universally aided those in want, the benefit of tortoise shells and cowries as achievement was mostly light. Yet affairs have errors and changes. Crafty abuses arise in succession. Dim labor and bitter corvée—therefore farmers left and followed merchants. Merchant sons found their work easy. Secondary trades spread and gradually widened. What currency circulates is no longer the intent of the first makers. Thereupon they competed to collect rarities seldom arriving, stored from afar goods not yet named. Bright pearls and kingfisher feathers sped without feet. Silk rugs and patterned rhinoceros horn flew without awaiting wings. All under heaven in turmoil—all took abandoning the root as their business. In abundance and diffusion they shared the resources of many ripe grains. In famine and disaster they again reduced the farmers' stores. Though money might pile a foot high, it still could not relieve famine in the age of Yao. Cowries might be wheel-sized, yet they truly could not slake thirst in the age of Tang. The plague of harm is already deep. It is right to abolish money entirely and use only grain and cloth, so the people know the path of laboring to live—there is no other way. A thousand bolts as goods is harder than carrying jade in one's bosom. Ten thousand bushels as a market is not easier than crossing districts. This could make secondary crafts restrain themselves and wandering idlers know to return. But as years pass, people and affairs grow accustomed. Some storehouses overflow with rotted strings while high granaries are not filled; some households hoard cash while good fields are seldom opened. If affairs change in a single morning, abolish it and cease use—what exchange depends on would have no recourse from dawn to dusk. Though this reaches essential technique, it cannot be carried out in haste. First one should cut splendor and stop falsity, return to purity and antiquity, cast disks into hidden peaks and pearls into clear ravines. Then drive the people of an age back to the path of plowing and mulberry, until silk and grain overflow in abundance like water and fire. Then wash away round-coin law, melt and cast leaving none, establish institutions and hand down systems forever to posterity. Every house would praise benevolence—is this not beyond the age of Tang? Huan Xuan knew the beginning but did not survey the end; Kong Linzhi saw the end but did not grasp the root. Did they consider opening and closing? Can a one-sided argument truly stand?

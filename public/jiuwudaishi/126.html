馮道，字可道，瀛州景城人。 其先為農為儒，不恒其業。 道少純厚，好學能文，不恥惡衣食，負米奉親之外，惟以披誦吟諷為事，雖大雪擁戶，凝塵滿席，湛如也。 天祐中，劉守光署為幽州掾。 守光引兵伐中山，訪於僚屬，道常以利害箴之，守光怒，置於獄中，尋為人所救免。 守光敗，遁歸太原，監軍使張承業辟為本院巡官。 承業重其文章履行，甚見待遇。 時有周元豹者，善人倫鑒，與道不洽，謂承業曰：「馮生無前程，公不可過用。 」時河東記室盧質聞之曰：「我曾見杜黃裳司空寫真圖，道之狀貌酷類焉，將來必副大用，元豹之言不足信也。 」承業尋薦為霸府從事，俄署太原掌書記，時莊宗並有河北，文翰甚繁，一以委之。 莊宗與梁軍夾河對壘，一日，郭崇韜以諸校伴食數多，主者不辦，請少罷減。 莊宗怒曰：「孤為效命者設食都不自由，其河北三鎮，令三軍別擇一人為帥，孤請歸太原以避賢路。 」遽命道對面草詞，將示其眾。 道執筆久之，莊宗正色促焉，道徐起對曰：「道所掌筆硯，敢不供職。 今大王屢集大功，方平南寇，崇韜所諫，未至過當，阻拒之則可，不可以向來之言，喧動群議，敵人若知，謂大王君臣之不和矣。 幸熟而思之，則天下幸甚也。 」俄而崇韜入謝，因道為之解焉，人始重其膽量。 莊宗即位鄴宮，除省郎，充翰林學士，自綠衣賜紫。 梁平，遷中書舍人、戶部侍郎。 丁父憂，持服於景城。 〈 （《談苑》：道聞父喪，即徒步見星以行，家人從後持衣囊追及之。）〉 遇歲儉，所得俸余悉賑於鄉里，道之所居惟蓬茨而已，凡牧宰饋遺，鬥粟匹帛無所受焉。 時契丹方盛，素聞道名，欲掠而取之，會邊人有備，獲免。
Feng Dao, courtesy name Kedao, came from Jingcheng in Ying Prefecture. His family had alternated between farming and scholarship, never settling on one calling. As a young man Feng Dao was sincere and steady, devoted to learning and adept at writing, and unashamed of plain food and worn clothes. Beyond carrying rice to support his parents, he spent his days reading and chanting verse; even when snow piled against the door and dust coated the floor mats, he remained perfectly at ease. In the Tianyou period Liu Shouguang appointed him a staff officer in Youzhou. When Shouguang marched against Zhongshan and asked his officials for counsel, Dao repeatedly warned him of the risks and rewards involved. Shouguang flew into a rage and threw him in jail, but others soon secured his release. After Shouguang's defeat Dao fled to Taiyuan, where Zhang Chengye, the military overseer, took him on as a headquarters inspector. Chengye prized both his writing and his character and treated him with marked favor. There was a man named Zhou Yuanbao, skilled at reading character, who disliked Dao and told Chengye, "Young Feng has no future ahead of him—you must not rely on him too much." Lu Zhi, secretary of the Hedong commandery, overheard and said, "I once saw the portrait of Du Huangchang, former minister of works—Feng Dao looks strikingly like him. He is bound for high office; Yuanbao's judgment cannot be trusted." Chengye soon recommended him as staff in the heir's headquarters, and before long he was named chief secretary at Taiyuan. With Zhuangzong holding all of Hebei, the paperwork was overwhelming, and he was given sole charge of it. Zhuangzong and the Liang forces faced each other across the river. One day Guo Chongtao pointed out that too many junior officers were dining together for the kitchen to supply and asked that the numbers be cut back. Zhuangzong burst out, "I cannot even decide how to feed the men who die for me! Let each of the three Hebei circuits pick its own commander from the ranks—I will go back to Taiyuan and make way for better men." He at once ordered Dao to draft a proclamation on the spot to read before the army. Dao held his brush for a long while. Zhuangzong, scowling, pressed him. Dao rose slowly and said, "The brush is my charge—I would never neglect it." Your Highness has won victory after victory and is about to crush the southern enemy. Chongtao's counsel was hardly excessive—you may turn it down, but you must not let your earlier outburst alarm the whole camp. If the enemy hears of it, they will think ruler and minister are divided. Please think it through carefully—the realm will be the better for it. Before long Chongtao came in to apologize, and Dao smoothed things over for him. From that day men began to admire his nerve. When Zhuangzong assumed the throne at Ye, Dao was made a secretariat officer and Hanlin academician, promoted from junior green to senior purple robes. After the Liang was subdued he was promoted to Secretariat drafter and vice minister of revenue. When his father died he went home to Jingcheng to observe the mourning period. [ (From the Tan Yuan: As soon as he heard of his father's death, Dao set out on foot, walking by starlight until his family caught up from behind with a bundle of clothes.)〉 In a famine year he gave away every spare coin of his salary to the neighborhood. He lived in nothing but a thatched hut, and refused every gift from local magistrates, whether grain or cloth. The Khitans were then at the height of their power and, having long heard of Dao's reputation, meant to carry him off—but the border communities were prepared, and he was spared.

明宗入洛，遽謂近臣安重誨曰：「先帝時馮道郎中何在？ 」重誨曰：「近除翰林學士。 」明宗曰：「此人朕素諳委，甚好宰相。 」俄拜端明殿學士，端明之號，自道始也。 未幾，遷中書侍郎、刑部尚書、平章事。 凡孤寒士子，抱才業、素知識者皆與引用； 唐末衣冠，履行浮躁者必抑而鎮之。 有工部侍郎任贊，因班退，與同列戲道於後曰：「若急行，必遺下《兔園冊》。 」道知之，召贊謂曰：「《兔園冊》皆名儒所集，道能諷之，中朝士子止看文場秀句，便為舉業，皆竊取公聊，何淺狹之甚耶！ 」贊大愧焉。 〈 （《歐陽史》云：《兔園策》者，鄉校俚儒教田夫牧子之所誦也。 《北夢瑣言》云：《兔園策》乃徐、庾文體，非鄙樸之談，但家藏一本，人多賤之。 《困學紀聞》云：《兔園策府》三十卷，唐蔣王惲令僚佐杜嗣先仿應科目策，自設問對，引經史為訓註。 惲，太宗子，故用梁王兔園名其書，馮道《兔園策》謂此也。）〉 復有梁朝宰臣李琪，每以文章自擅，曾進《賀平中山王都表》云：「復真定之逆賊」。 道讓琪曰：「昨來收復定州，非真定也。 」琪昧於地理，頓至折角。 其後百僚上明宗徽號凡三章，道自為之，其文渾然，非流俗之體，舉朝服焉。 道尤長於篇詠，秉筆則成，典麗之外，義含古道，必為遠近傳寫，故漸畏其高深，由是班行肅然，無澆漓之態。 繼改門下侍郎、戶部吏部尚書、集賢殿弘文館大學士，加尚書左僕射，封始平郡公。 一日，道因上謁既退，明宗顧謂侍臣曰：「馮道性純儉，頃在德勝寨居一茅庵，與從人同器食，臥則芻槁一束，其心晏如也。 及以父憂退歸鄉里，自耕樵采，與農夫雜處，略不以素貴介懷，真士大夫也。 」天成、長興中，天下屢稔，朝廷無事。 明宗每禦延英，留道訪以外事，道曰：「陛下以至德承天，天以有年表瑞，更在日慎一日，以答天心。 臣每記在先皇霸府日，曾奉使中山，徑井陘之險，憂馬有蹶失，不敢怠於銜轡； 及至平地，則無復持控，果為馬所顛仆，幾至於損。 臣所陳雖小，可以喻大。 陛下勿以清晏豐熟，便縱逸樂，兢兢業業，臣之望也。 」明宗深然之。 他日又問道曰：「天下雖熟，百姓得濟否？ 」道曰：「穀貴餓農，穀賤傷農，此常理也。 臣憶得近代有舉子聶夷中《傷田家詩》云：『二月賣新絲，五月糶秋穀。 醫得眼下瘡，剜卻心頭肉。 我願君王心，化作光明燭。 不照綺羅筵，遍照逃亡屋。 』」明宗曰：「此詩甚好。 」遂命侍臣錄下，每自諷之。 道之發言簡正，善於裨益，非常人所能及也。 時以諸經舛繆，與同列李愚委學官田敏等，取西京鄭覃所刊石經，雕為印版，流布天下，後進賴之。 明宗崩，唐末帝嗣位，以道為山陵使，禮畢，出鎮同州，循故事也。 道為政閑淡，獄市無撓。 一日，有上介胡饒，本出軍吏，性粗獷，因事詬道於牙門，左右數報不應。 道曰：「此必醉耳！ 」因召入，開尊設食，盡夕而起，無撓慍之色。 未幾，入為司空。
When Mingzong entered Luoyang, he turned at once to his intimate An Chonghui and asked, "Under the late emperor, where was Bureau Director Feng Dao?" " Chonghui replied, "He was just appointed Hanlin academician." " Mingzong said, "I have long known and trusted this man—he would make an excellent chief minister." " Before long he was named academician of the Duanming Hall—the very title Duanming began with him. Soon afterward he was promoted to vice minister of the Secretariat, minister of punishments, and associate chief minister. He advanced every poor scholar of talent he had long known; while those scions of late Tang society whose conduct was flighty he always held down and kept in check. Ren Zan, vice minister of works, once after court dismissed joked behind Dao's back to his colleagues: "Walk too fast and he'll drop his Hare Garden Primer." " When Dao heard of it, he summoned Zan and said, "The Hare Garden Primer was compiled by eminent scholars—I can recite it by heart. Yet court scholars read only polished examination couplets and call that their whole training, stealing every phrase from official templates—how miserably shallow!" " Zan was utterly mortified. [ (The Ouyang History says: The Hare Garden Stratagems was what rustic teachers in village schools had farmers' sons and herdsboys memorize.) The Beimeng Suoyan says: The Hare Garden Stratagems belongs to the polished style of Xu Ling and Yu Xin, not to crude village speech—yet though every home owned a copy, educated men looked down on it. The Kunxue Jiwen says: The Hare Garden Stratagems Treasury comprises thirty juan. In Tang, Prince Yun of Jiang had his staff officer Du Sixian model civil-service policy questions, framing his own Q&A and glossing them with passages from the classics and histories. Yun was a son of Taizong of Tang, hence the title drawn from the Liang prince's Hare Garden—the Hare Garden Stratagems Feng Dao cited refers to this work.)〉 There was also Li Qi, a Liang dynasty minister who fancied himself unrivaled in letters. Once he submitted a memorial congratulating the pacification of Zhongshan and Wang Du, writing that the court had "recovered the rebels of Zhending." Dao corrected him: "What was recovered recently was Dingzhou, not Zhending." " Qi knew nothing of geography and was utterly routed in debate. Later, when the bureaucracy submitted three drafts of honorific titles for Mingzong, Dao wrote them himself. The language flowed as one piece, far above the common run, and the entire court stood in awe. Dao was above all a master of poetry and prose: take up the brush and the piece was done. Beyond elegance, his meaning carried the weight of antiquity, and everything he wrote was copied and circulated far and wide—so men gradually stood in awe of his depth, and the official ranks grew grave, without a trace of frivolity. He was next made vice minister of the Chancellery, minister of revenue and of personnel, grand academician of the Jixian and Hongwen halls, elevated to left vice director of the Department of State Affairs, and enfeoffed as Duke of Shiping. One day, after Dao had paid his respects and withdrawn, Mingzong said to his attendants, "Feng Dao is utterly plain and frugal. At Desheng Stockade he once lived in a grass hut, ate from the same bowl as his men, and slept on a single bundle of straw—yet he was perfectly content." When he went home to mourn his father, he plowed his own fields and gathered his own firewood, living among farmers without a trace of former privilege—he was a true gentleman-scholar. " In the Tiancheng and Changxing years the realm enjoyed harvest after harvest, and the court had little to trouble it. Whenever Mingzong held court in the Yanying Hall, he kept Dao to discuss affairs of state. Dao said, "Your Majesty rules by supreme virtue and Heaven answers with year after year of plenty. You must grow more careful with each passing day—that is how you answer Heaven's favor." I often remember that in the late emperor's princely days, on a mission to Zhongshan, I took the perilous road through Jingxing Pass and, fearing the horse might stumble, never loosened my grip on the reins; but once I reached level ground I let go—and the horse threw me. I was nearly killed. What I tell you is a small matter, but it stands for a great one. Do not let peace and plenty lure you into idleness and pleasure. Vigilance and diligence—that is what I ask of you. " Mingzong took his meaning to heart. On another day he asked Dao again, "The realm may be at peace, but are the people truly provided for?" " Dao said, "When grain is dear the farmer starves; when grain is cheap the farmer is ruined—that is an old truth." I recall a recent examination candidate, Nie Yizhong, who wrote in his Lament for the Farming Family: "In the second month we sell the new silk; in the fifth month we pawn the autumn grain." "We cure the sore before our eyes by gouging out the flesh of the heart." "I wish the ruler's heart were a bright candle." "That did not light brocade feasts but every hovel of the destitute." '" Mingzong said, "That poem is excellent." " He had his attendants copy it out and often recited it himself. Dao spoke with brevity and integrity, and his counsel was of real benefit—beyond what most men could offer. Because the canonical texts were riddled with errors, he and his colleague Li Yu put the academic official Tian Min and others to work taking the stone classics Zheng Tan had carved in the Western Capital, cutting them into printing blocks, and distributing them across the realm—generations of students afterward owed him a debt. When Mingzong died and the last Tang emperor took the throne, Dao was appointed commissioner for the imperial tomb. When the rites were complete he was posted to govern Tongzhou, following established precedent. His rule was easy and light-handed; neither the courts nor the markets were troubled. One day his senior aide Hu Rao—a former army clerk, rough by nature—abused Dao at the headquarters gate over some matter. Attendants reported it again and again, but Dao paid no heed. Dao said, "He must be drunk!" " He had him brought in, opened wine, and set a meal before him. They rose only at evening, and Dao showed not a trace of anger. Before long he was recalled to the capital as minister of works.

及晉祖入洛，以道為首相。 二年，契丹遣使加徽號於晉祖，晉祖亦獻徽號於契丹，謂道曰：「此行非卿不可。 」道無難色。 晉祖又曰：「卿官崇德重，不可深入沙漠。 」道曰：「陛下受北朝恩，臣受陛下恩，何有不可！」 〈 （楊內翰《談苑》云：道與諸相歸中書，食訖，外廳堂吏前白道言北使事。 吏人色變手戰，道取紙一幅，署云：「道去。 」即遣寫敕進，堂吏泣下。 道遣人語妻子，不復歸家，即日舍都亭驛，不數日北行。 晉祖餞宴，語以家國之故，煩耆德遠使，自酌卮酒賜之，泣下。）〉 及行，將達西樓，契丹主欲郊迎，其臣曰：「天子無迎宰相之禮。 」因止焉，其名動殊俗也如此。 〈 （《談苑》云：契丹賜其臣牙笏及臘日賜牛頭者為殊禮，道皆得之，作詩以紀曰：「牛頭偏得賜，象笏更容持。 」契丹主甚喜，遂潛諭留意，道曰：「南朝為子，北朝為父，兩朝皆為臣，豈有分別哉！ 」道在契丹，凡得所賜，悉以市薪炭，征其意，云：「北地苦寒，老年所不堪，當為之備。 」若將久留者。 契丹感其意，乃遣歸，道三上表乞留，固遣乃去，猶更住館中月餘。 既行，所至留駐，凡兩月方出境，左右語道曰：「當北土得生還，恨無羽翼，公獨宿留，何也？ 」道曰：「縱急還，彼以筋腳馬，一夕即追及，亦何可脫，但徐緩即不能測矣。 」眾乃服。 四年二月，始至京師。）〉 及還，朝廷廢樞密使，依唐朝故事，並歸中書，其院印付道，事無巨細，悉以歸之。 尋加司徒、兼侍中，進魯國公。 晉祖曾以用兵事問道，道曰：陛下歷試諸艱，創成大業，神武睿略，為天下所知，討伐不庭，須從獨斷。 臣本自書生，為陛下在中書，守歷代成規，不敢有一毫之失也。 臣在明宗朝，曾以戎事問臣，臣亦以斯言答之。 」晉祖頗可其說。 道嘗上表求退，晉祖不之覽，先遣鄭王就省，謂曰：「卿來日不出，朕當親行請卿。 」道不得已出焉。 當時寵遇，無與為比。
When the Jin founder entered Luoyang, he made Dao his chief minister. In the second year the Khitans sent envoys to bestow an honorific title on the Jin founder, who in turn presented one to the Khitans. He told Dao, "This mission cannot go to anyone but you." " Dao did not hesitate. The Jin founder added, "Your rank is high and your virtue great—you must not go deep into the desert." Dao said, "Your Majesty owes the northern court a debt of favor; I owe Your Majesty the same—how could I refuse!" [ (Hanlin Academician Yang's Tan Yuan says: Dao and the other chief ministers had returned to the Secretariat and finished their meal when a clerk from the outer hall came forward to inform Dao of the northern envoy mission.) The clerk turned pale and his hands shook. Dao took a sheet of paper and wrote, "Dao goes." " He had an edict drafted and submitted at once, and the hall clerk burst into tears. Dao sent word to his wife and children that he would not be coming home, took lodging that same day at the Capital Pavilion relay station, and within days was marching north. The Jin founder gave a farewell banquet, spoke of the needs of state and family, and said he was troubling an elder of great virtue with a distant mission; he poured a cup of wine with his own hand, bestowed it on Dao, and wept.)〉 On the journey, as he neared the Western Tower, the Khitan ruler wished to welcome him in the suburbs, but his ministers said, "The Son of Heaven has no protocol for greeting a chief minister in the suburbs." " They dissuaded him—such was the power of his name among foreign peoples. [ (The Tan Yuan says: Among the Khitans, ivory ministerial tablets and ox-heads given on the La festival were marks of special favor—Dao received them all and wrote a poem to commemorate it: "The ox-head alone was granted me; the ivory tablet I may also hold.") The Khitan ruler was delighted and then quietly urged him to bear this in mind. Dao said, "The southern court is son to the northern, which is father—yet I am subject to both. What difference can there be!" " In Khitan territory, everything he was given he spent on fuel and charcoal, signaling his intent: "The north is bitterly cold—a man of my years cannot endure it without preparation." As though he meant to stay for good. The Khitans took his meaning and sent him home. Dao submitted three memorials begging to stay, but they insisted he leave—yet he lingered in the guesthouse more than a month longer. Once he set out, he lingered at every stop and was two months crossing the border. His attendants said to him, "In the north, a man who escapes alive wishes he had wings—yet you alone delay. Why?" " Dao said, "Even if we hurry, they have swift horses and would overtake us in a night—how could we escape? Go slowly, and they cannot read our intent." " His men were convinced. In the second month of the fourth year he at last reached the capital.)〉 On his return the court abolished the Bureau of Military Affairs and, following Tang precedent, returned its powers to the Secretariat. Its seal was handed to Dao, and matters great and small were entrusted entirely to him. Soon afterward he was made minister of education and palace attendant, and advanced to Duke of Lu. The Jin founder once asked Dao about military affairs. Dao said, "Your Majesty has been tested through every hardship and built a great enterprise. Your martial brilliance and strategic wisdom are known throughout the realm. To punish the recalcitrant, you must decide alone." I began as a mere scholar. In the Secretariat I uphold the established rules of successive dynasties and dare not permit the slightest lapse. In Mingzong's court, when he asked me about military affairs, I gave him the same answer. " The Jin founder was much pleased with his answer. Dao once submitted a memorial asking to retire. The Jin founder ignored it and first sent the Prince of Zheng to visit him at home, saying, "If you do not come to court tomorrow, I shall come in person to fetch you." Dao had no choice but to return to court. At that time no one enjoyed such imperial favor.

When the Jin minor emperor succeeded, Dao was made acting grand commandant and ennobled as Duke of Yan. Once Dao asked a court acquaintance, "What do people say about my service in the Secretariat? " Half praise him, half blame him," the guest replied. " Dao said, "Most people call agreement right and disagreement wrong—so I suspect nine out of ten think ill of me. Even Confucius, a sage, was maligned by Shusun Wushu—how much more should a man as slight as I expect it! " Yet what I uphold, I have never changed from first to last. Later someone told the minor emperor, "Dao makes a fine peacetime chancellor but is useless in a crisis—as useless as calling a Chan monk to hunt with hawks! " On this account Dao was posted out as military commissioner of Tongzhou. After a year and more he was reassigned to Nanyang and made director of the Secretariat. When the Khitan took Bian, Dao was summoned from Xiang and Deng. The Khitan ruler asked him casually, "How can the people of the empire be saved? " Dao said, "At this hour not even the Buddha reborn could save them—only Your Majesty can. " Afterward the gentry were spared slaughter and displacement—largely through the quiet protection of Dao and Zhao Yanshou. That third month he marched north with the Khitan and reached Changshan together with the Jin court's highest officials. Soon the Khitan emperor died and Prince Yongkang took command of the army. As they withdrew north, they left a clansman named Jiali to hold Changshan. Han troops, furious, then drove out Jiali and soon retook the city. Dao led his colleagues on missions in all directions to reassure the people, adapting policy to local needs until order was restored everywhere. When people praised his role, Dao said, "What can a scholar-minister accomplish? The credit belongs to the generals. " Because his moral authority commanded respect, he had the leaders choose the hardest-working veteran among the generals and made the cavalry captain Bai Zairong acting commander. Army and people then settled down—chiefly through Dao's influence. At Changshan he saw Chinese men and women held captive by the Khitan. He paid from his own funds to ransom them, housed them at reputable nunneries, and later helped each return to family. Earlier the Khitan had left Dao, Li Song, He Ning, and other officials at Changshan. On the twenty-ninth of the intercalary seventh month that year an edict recalled Song and required ten court scholars to go to Muye Shan on imperial business. The Khitan leader Moda summoned Dao and the others to his camp to give them orders. Song arrived first, guessed what was planned, and could not hide his fear. Moda meant to send them north with the court scholars the next day. Song did not wait for Dao but left early with He Ning. They met Dao outside the tent gate, said farewell, and each went home. Soon Li Yun and others set fire to the camp and battled the Khitan at close quarters. If they had all gone to the tent that day and hesitated even slightly before Moda, they would all have been captured. Later opinion held that Dao had shown supreme integrity as a commoner and carried great weight in public office—and that such covert rewards of merit were typical of his career.

Reflecting on my life from beginning to end, I rejoice that honor reaches the living and the dead—all from imperial favor, all by family rule. I received my elders' teaching and hold to the source of moral transformation: filial at home, loyal in office, no improper word on my lips, no ill-gotten goods at my gate. I wish never to deceive earth below, men in the middle, or Heaven above—these three honesties are my standard. Humble or honored, young or old, in serving parents, ruler, elders, and subjects alike, I have been spared by Heaven through repeated trials and gained many blessings; I fell among barbarians yet returned to China—not by human design but by Heaven's grace. Those blessed within the realm have a home to return to after a hundred years. Do not fill my mouth with pearls and jade; bury me in seasonal dress in a plain rush coffin on unclaimed ground, for I cannot match the ancients. Offer a single ram in sacrifice and forbid killing; use offerings that take no life. Erect no spirit-path stele, for the ancients of the Three Dynasties set no steles before their tombs. Seek no posthumous title, for I have no virtue to warrant one. I also recall that from staff officer to chief minister and as frontier commander, any small service to the state was recorded in official archives. My essays and poems, most lost in the turmoil, survive only in a family collection. How many readers understand my intent, know me, or condemn me, I cannot say. I have estates, a home, a library, and three sons to carry on my work. Even now I bathe five times and examine myself thrice daily, still mindful that each day brings loss and each month tests what skill remains. As son, brother, minister, teacher, husband, and father, with sons, nephews, and grandsons, I have done enough for myself. For the times, it is not enough—but what is lacking? I failed to bring unity to the realm and secure the eight directions for my sovereign—I am ashamed before every office I have held. How could I repay what Heaven and Earth have given me? Now and then I open a book, now and then I raise a cup—tasting flavors, hearing music, wrapped in color—growing old at peace in my own time! To grow old and be content with oneself—what greater joy could there be! So concludes the preface to the Old Man of Changle, written in the third year of Qianyou by Zhu Mingyue.

及太祖平內難，議立徐州節度使劉赟為漢嗣，遣道與秘書監趙上交、樞密直學士王度等往迎之。 道尋與赟自徐赴汴，行至宋州，會澶州軍變。 樞密使王峻遣郭崇領兵至，屯於衙門外，時道與上交等宿於衙內。 是日，赟率左右甲士闔門登樓，詰崇所自，崇言太祖已副推戴。 左右知其事變，以為道所賣，皆欲殺道等以自快。 趙上交與王度聞之，皆惶怖不知所為，惟道偃仰自適，略無懼色，尋亦獲免焉。 道微時嘗賦詩云：「終聞海嶽歸明主，未省乾坤陷吉人。 」至是其言驗矣。 〈 （《青箱雜記》載馮道詩全篇云：莫為危時便愴神，前程往往有期因，終聞海嶽歸明主，未省乾坤陷吉人。 道德幾時曾去世，舟車何處不通津，但教方寸無諸惡，狼虎叢中也立身。）〉 廣順初，復拜太師、中書令，太祖甚重之，每進對不以名呼。 及太祖崩，世宗以道為山陵使。 會河東劉崇入寇，世宗召大臣議欲親征，道諫止之，世宗因言：「唐初，天下草寇蜂起，並是太宗親平之。 」道奏曰：「陛下得如太宗否？ 」世宗怒曰：「馮道何相少也！ 」乃罷。 及世宗親征，不及扈從，留道奉太祖山陵。 時道已抱疾。 及山陵禮畢，奉神主歸舊宮，未及祔廟，一夕薨於其第，時顯德元年四月十七日也，享年七十有三。 世宗聞之，輟視朝三日，冊贈尚書令，追封瀛王，謚曰文懿。
When Taizu quelled the internal crisis, the court decided to make Liu Yun, military governor of Xuzhou, heir to the Han throne and sent Dao with Zhao Shangjiao, director of the secretariat, Wang Du, privy council academician, and others to escort him to the capital. Dao soon set out with Yun from Xuzhou toward Bianjing, but at Song Prefecture they ran into the mutiny of the Dazhou garrison. Privy council commissioner Wang Jun sent Guo Chong with troops, who camped outside the headquarters gate while Dao, Shangjiao, and the rest stayed inside. That day Yun led his armored guards to bar the gate and climb the tower, demanding Chong's purpose. Chong replied that Taizu had already been acclaimed by the army. Yun's men, seeing how events had turned, believed Dao had sold them out and wanted to kill Dao and his companions on the spot. Zhao Shangjiao and Wang Du were both terrified and at a loss, but Dao alone lay back at ease without a trace of fear, and before long he too was spared. In his youth Dao had written a poem: "In the end the seas and mountains return to a worthy sovereign; I have never known Heaven and Earth to destroy a man of fortune." Now his words had come true. [ (The Qingxiang Zaji gives Feng Dao's poem in full: Do not let hard times break your spirit—the road ahead often has its reason. In the end the seas and mountains return to a worthy sovereign; I have never known Heaven and Earth to destroy a man of fortune. When has virtue ever left the world? Where do boats and carts not find a crossing? Keep your heart free of evil, and you may stand upright even among wolves and tigers.)〉 Early in the Guangshun reign he was again made grand preceptor and director of the secretariat. Taizu held him in the highest regard and never addressed him by name in audience. When Taizu died, Shizong appointed Dao commissioner for the imperial tomb. When Liu Chong of Hedong invaded, Shizong called the chief ministers to discuss leading the campaign in person. Dao dissuaded him. Shizong then said, "In early Tang, rebels swarmed across the land, and it was Taizong who put them down himself." Dao replied, "Can Your Majesty match Taizong?" Shizong snapped, "How little Feng Dao thinks of me!" The discussion ended there. When Shizong marched in person, Dao could not accompany him and was left to oversee Taizu's tomb. By then Dao was already gravely ill. When the tomb rites were complete and the spirit tablet had been returned to the old palace but before it could be installed in the ancestral temple, he died in his home one night—the seventeenth day of the fourth month of the first year of Xiande, at the age of seventy-three. Shizong suspended court for three days, posthumously appointed him director of the secretariat, created him Prince of Ying, and gave him the posthumous title Wényì, "Refined and Excellent."
11
道歷任四朝，三入中書，在相位二十餘年，以持重鎮俗為己任，未嘗以片簡擾於諸侯，平生甚廉儉。 逮至末年，閨庭之內，稍徇奢靡，其子吉，尤恣狂蕩，道不能制，識者以其不終令譽，鹹嘆惜之。 〈 （《五代史補》：馮道之鎮同州也，有酒務吏乞以家財修夫子廟，道以狀付判官參詳其事。 判官素滑稽，因以一絕書判後云：「荊棘森森繞杏壇，儒官高貴盡偷安，若教酒務修夫子，覺我慚惶也大難。 」道覽之有愧色，因出俸重創之。 馮瀛王道之在中書也，有舉子李導投贄所業，馮相見之，戲謂曰：「老夫名道，其來久矣，加以累居相府，秀才不可謂不知，然亦名導，於禮可乎？ 」李抗聲對曰：「相公是無寸底道字，小子有寸底導字，何謂不可也！ 」公笑曰：「老夫不惟名無寸，諸事亦無寸，吾子可謂知人矣。 」了無怒色。 馮吉，瀛王道之子，能彈琵琶，以皮為弦，世宗嘗令彈於御前，深欣善之，因號其琵琶曰「繞殿雷」也。 道以其惰業，每加譴責，而吉攻之愈精，道益怒，凡與客飲，必使庭立而彈之，曲罷或賜以束帛，命背負之，然後致謝。 道自以為戒勖極矣，吉未能悛改，既而益自若。 道度無可奈何，嘆曰：「百工之司藝而身賤，理使然也。 此子不過太常少卿耳。 」其後果終於此。）〉
Dao served four dynasties, entered the secretariat three times, and held the chancellorship for more than twenty years. He made it his task to steady the realm through weight and restraint, never troubled a single regional lord with a private note, and lived his life in notable frugality. In his last years his household grew somewhat extravagant. His son Ji was especially wild and unrestrained, and Dao could not rein him in. Those who knew him sighed that his fine reputation would not endure to the end. [ (The supplement to the History of the Five Dynasties records: When Feng Dao was posted to Tongzhou, a wine-tax clerk asked to repair the Confucius temple at his own expense. Dao referred the petition to his judge for review. The judge, a born wit, appended a quatrain to his ruling: "Thorns and brambles ring the apricot altar round; lofty Confucian officers all steal their ease. If the wine-tax office must repair the Master, my shame would be more than I could bear." Dao read it and flushed with shame, then paid from his own salary to rebuild the temple properly. When Prince Feng of Ying was at the secretariat, a candidate named Li Dao presented his writings. The chancellor received him and teased, "My name is Dao and has been for a long time, and I have sat in the chief minister's office again and again—you cannot claim not to know it. Yet you are also named Dao—is that proper?" Li answered boldly, "Your character Dao has no 'inch' at the bottom; mine has an 'inch' at the bottom—what could be improper about that!" The prince laughed and said, "I have no inch not only in my name but in anything I do. Young man, you know your man." He showed not a trace of anger. Feng Ji, son of Prince Feng of Ying, played the pipa with leather strings. Shizong once had him perform before the throne and was so delighted that he named the instrument "Thunder Rolling Round the Hall." Dao rebuked him constantly for his idle pursuits, but Ji only practiced harder, which made Dao angrier still. Whenever he entertained guests he made Ji stand in the courtyard and play; when the piece ended he sometimes gave him a bundle of silk and made him carry it on his back before he was allowed to bow his thanks. Dao thought he had disciplined him as far as possible, but Ji would not change and only grew more brazen. Dao, seeing nothing more to be done, sighed and said, "Among the hundred crafts, mastery of an art still leaves a man low in rank—that is how things are." This boy will never rise above vice director of the court of imperial sacrifices. In the end that is exactly where he finished.)〉
12
史臣曰：道之履行，郁有古人之風； 道之宇量，深得大臣之禮。 然而事四朝，相六帝，可得為忠乎！ 夫一女二夫，人之不幸，況於再三者哉！ 所以飾終之典，不得謚為文貞、文忠者，蓋謂此也。
The historiographer writes: In conduct and bearing, Dao had something of the ancients about him; in breadth of spirit he deeply embodied what a great minister should be. Yet he served four dynasties and was chief minister to six emperors—can that be called loyalty! For a woman to have two husbands is misfortune enough—how much worse when it happens again and again! That is why the funeral honors could not include the posthumous titles Wénzhēn or Wénzhōng—"Refined and Upright" or "Refined and Loyal."

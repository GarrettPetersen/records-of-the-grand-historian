1
顯德六年春正月丁未朔，帝御崇元殿受朝賀，仗衛如式。 壬子，高麗國王王昭遣使貢方物。 己卯，以翰林學士、中書舍人申文炳為左散騎常侍。 辛酉，女真國遣使貢獻。 壬戌，青州奏，節度使、陳王安審琦為部曲所殺。 乙丑，賜諸將射於內鞠場。 戊辰，幸迎春苑。 甲戌，詔：「每年新及第進士及諸科聞喜宴，宜令宣徽院指揮排比。 」乙亥，詔：「禮部貢院今後及第舉人，依逐科等第定人數姓名，並所試文學奏聞，候敕下放榜」云。 是月，樞密使王樸詳定雅樂十二律旋相為宮之法，並造律準，上之。 詔尚書省集百官詳議，亦以為可，語在《樂志》。
In spring of Xiande 6, on the new-moon day dingwei of the first month, the Emperor took the throne in the Chongyuan Hall to receive the new-year congratulations, with guards and insignia in full ceremonial order. On renzi the king of Goryeo, Wang Zhao, sent envoys bearing tribute goods. On jimao Shen Wenbing, Hanlin academician and Secretariat drafter, was appointed Left Regular Attendant of the Cavalry. On xinyou the Jurchen sent envoys with tribute. On renxu Qingzhou reported that An Shenqi, military commissioner and Prince of Chen, had been killed by his own troops. On yichou the Emperor had the generals shoot at the archery range within the palace grounds. On wuchen he visited the Welcome-Spring Park. On jiaxu an edict declared that each year's Banquet of Shared Joy for newly passed jinshi and candidates in other examination categories should be arranged under the direction of the Palace Secretariat. On yihai another edict required that hereafter, for each examination category and grade at the Ministry of Rites examination yard, the number and names of successful candidates and their examination essays be reported to the throne and the results posted only after imperial approval. That month Grand Councilor Wang Pu finalized the method by which the twelve tones of court music cycle as tonal centers, constructed pitch pipes, and presented them to the throne. The throne ordered the Department of State Affairs to convene the officials for full discussion; they too approved the plan, as recorded in the Music Monograph.
2
二月庚辰，發徐、宿、宋、單等州丁夫數萬浚汴河。 甲申，發滑、亳二州丁夫浚五丈河，東流於定陶，入於濟，以通青、鄆水運之路。 又疏導蔡河，以通陳、潁水運之路。 乙酉，詔諸道應差攝官各支半俸。 丙戌，以翰林學士承旨、尚書兵部侍郎陶穀為尚書吏部侍郎充職。 詔升湖州為節鎮，以宣德軍為軍額，以湖州刺史錢偡為本州節度使，從兩浙錢俶之請也。 辛丑，幸迎春苑。 甲辰，右補闕王德成謫授右贊善大夫，坐舉官不當也。 詔賜諸道州府供用糧草有差。
In the second month, on gengchen, tens of thousands of corvée laborers from Xu, Su, Song, Shan, and other prefectures were mobilized to dredge the Bian Canal. On jiashen corvée workers from Hua and Bo were set to dredge the Five-Zhang River so that it flowed east through Dingtao into the Ji River, opening the water route to Qing and Yun. The Cai River was also dredged to open transport routes to Chen and Ying. On yiyou an edict directed that acting appointees in every circuit receive half pay. On bingxu Tao Gu, expositor-in-chief of the Hanlin Academy and vice minister of war, was appointed vice minister of personnel to fill the post. Huzhou was elevated to a military commission under the army name Xuande, and Prefect Qian Hong was made its military commissioner, at the request of Qian Chu of the Two Zhes. On xinchou he again visited the Welcome-Spring Park. On jiachen Right Remonstrator Wang Decheng was demoted to Right Mentor of the Heir Apparent for having recommended an unfit candidate. An edict granted grain and fodder to the circuits and prefectures in varying allotments.
3
三月庚申，樞密使王樸卒。 甲子，詔以北境未復，取此月內幸滄州。 以宣徽南院使吳延祚為權東京留守，判開封府事； 以宣徽北院使昝居潤為副使； 以三司使張美為大內都部署。 〈 （《東都事略·張美傳》：世宗北征，以美為大內都點檢。）〉 命諸將各領馬步諸軍及戰棹赴滄州。 己巳，濠州奏，鐘離縣饑民死者五百九十有四。 癸酉，詔廢諸州銅魚。 〈 （《五代會要》：顯德六年，敕諸道牧守，每遇除移，特降制書，何假符契，其請納銅魚，宜廢之。）〉 甲戌，車駕發京師。
In the third month, on gengshen, Grand Councilor Wang Pu died. On jiazi an edict announced that, because the northern frontier had not yet been recovered, the Emperor would visit Cangzhou within the month. Wu Yanzuo of the Palace Secretariat South Bureau was named acting regent of the Eastern Capital and assigned to administer Kaifeng; Zan Jurun of the North Bureau was named his deputy; and Zhang Mei, commissioner of the Three Departments, was made grand commander of the inner palace. Opening editorial annotation. (The 《Eastern Capital Miscellany》, Biography of Zhang Mei, states that on Shizong's northern campaign Mei was appointed grand inspector of the inner palace.) Each general was ordered to lead his cavalry, infantry, and war vessels to Cangzhou. On jisi Haozhou reported that five hundred ninety-four famine victims had died in Zhongli County. On guiyou an edict abolished the bronze tally-fish used by prefectures. Opening editorial annotation. (The 《Institutional Compendium of the Five Dynasties》 records that in Xiande 6 an edict told circuit governors that each appointment or transfer was confirmed by a special commission—tally tokens were unnecessary, and the practice of submitting bronze tally-fish should be abolished.) On jiaxu the imperial procession left the capital.
4
夏四月辛卯，車駕次滄州，以前左諫議大夫薛居正為刑部侍郎。 是日，帝率諸軍北征。 壬辰，至乾寧軍，偽寧州刺史王洪以城降。 丁酉，駕御龍舟，率舟師順流而北，首尾數十里。 辛丑，至益津關。 〈 （《通鑒》：至益津關，契丹守將終廷暉以城降。）〉 自此以西，水路漸隘，舟師難進，乃舍舟登陸。 壬寅，宿於野次。 時帝先期而至，大軍未集，隨駕之士不及一旅，賴今上率材官騎士以衛乘輿。 癸卯，今上先至瓦橋關，偽守將姚內斌以城降。 〈 （《隆平集》：姚內斌，平州人也。 世宗北征，將兵至瓦橋關，內斌為關使，開門請降，世宗以為汝州刺史。）〉 甲辰，鄚州刺史劉楚信以州來降。
In the fourth month of summer, on xinmao, the court halted at Cangzhou and former Left Remonstrating Grandee Xue Juzheng was appointed vice minister of punishments. That same day the Emperor led the armies north. On renchen the army reached Qianning; Wang Hong, rebel prefect of Ningzhou, surrendered the city. On dingyou the Emperor boarded the imperial dragon boat and led the fleet downstream, the column stretching several tens of li from van to rear. On xinchou the army reached Yijin Pass. Opening editorial annotation. (The 《Comprehensive Mirror》 records that at Yijin Pass the Khitan garrison commander Zhong Tinghui surrendered the city.) West of this point the waterways narrowed and the fleet could not proceed, so the army abandoned the boats and marched overland. On renyin the court encamped in the open field. The Emperor had arrived ahead of the main force; fewer than a regiment accompanied the imperial train, and only the present sovereign's corps of skilled cavalry guarded the imperial carriage. On guimao the present sovereign reached Waqiao Pass ahead of the main army, and the rebel garrison commander Yao Neibin surrendered the city. Opening editorial annotation. (The 《Longping Collection》 states that Yao Neibin was from Pingzhou. On Shizong's northern campaign, when the army reached Waqiao Pass, Neibin was pass commander; he opened the gates and offered surrender, and Shizong appointed him prefect of Ruzhou.) On jiachen Liu Chuxin, prefect of Mo, surrendered his prefecture.
5
五月乙巳朔，帝駐蹕於瓦橋關。 侍衛親軍都指揮使李重進及諸將相繼至行在，瀛州刺史高彥暉以本城歸順。 關南平，凡得州三、縣十七、戶一萬八千三百六十。 是役也，王師數萬，不亡一矢，邊界城邑皆望風而下。 丙午，帝與諸將議攻幽州，諸將皆以為未可，帝不聽。 是夜，帝不豫，乃止。 戊申，定州節度使孫行友奏，攻下易州，擒偽命刺史李在欽來獻，斬於軍市。 己酉，以瓦橋關為雄州， 〈 （《宋史·陳思讓傳》：得瓦橋關為雄州，命思讓為都部署，率兵戍守。）〉 以益津關為霸州。 〈 （《宋史·韓令坤傳》：為霸州都部署，率所部兵戍之。）〉 是日，先鋒都指揮使張藏英破契丹數百騎於瓦橋關北，攻下固安縣。 詔發濱、棣二州丁夫城霸州。 庚戌，遣侍衛都指揮使李重進率兵出土門，入河東界。 壬子，車駕發雄州，還京。 泉州節度使劉從效遣別駕王禹錫奉貢於行在，帝以泉州比臣江南，李景方歸奉國家，不欲奪其所屬，但錫詔褒美而已。 丁卯，西京奏，太常卿致仕司徒詡卒。 己巳，侍衛都指揮使李重進奏，破河東賊軍於百井，斬首二千級。 甲戌，上至自雄州。 〈 （《卻掃編》：周世宗既定三關，遇疾而退，至澶淵遲留不行，雖宰輔近臣問疾者皆莫得見，中外洶懼。 時張永德為澶州節度使，永德尚周太祖之女，以親故，獨得至臥內，於是群臣因永德言曰：「天下未定，根本空虛，四方諸侯惟幸京師之有變。 今澶、汴相去甚邇，不速歸以安人情，顧憚旦夕之勞而遲回於此，如有不可諱，奈宗廟何！ 」永德然之，乘間為世宗言如群臣旨，世宗問：「誰使汝為此言？ 」永德對以群臣之意皆願為此，世宗熟思久之，嘆曰：「吾固知汝必為人所教，獨不喻吾意哉！ 然觀汝之窮薄，惡足當此！ 」即日趣駕歸京師。）〉
In the fifth month, on the new-moon day yisi, the Emperor halted at Waqiao Pass. Li Chongjin, commander-in-chief of the Palace Guard, and the other generals reached the mobile court in turn, and Gao Yanhui, prefect of Ying, surrendered his city. The region south of the passes was pacified, yielding three prefectures, seventeen counties, and 18,360 households. In this campaign the imperial army of tens of thousands did not lose a single man to combat; border towns surrendered at the first approach. On bingwu the Emperor consulted his generals about attacking Youzhou; they all urged delay, but he would not heed them. That night the Emperor fell ill, and the attack was abandoned. On wushen Sun Xingyou, military commissioner of Dingzhou, reported the capture of Yizhou and the presentation of Li Zaiqin, the rebel-appointed prefect, who was beheaded in the army market. On jiyou Waqiao Pass was established as Xiong Prefecture, Opening editorial annotation. (The 《History of Song》, Biography of Chen Sirang, records that when Waqiao Pass became Xiong Prefecture, Sirang was appointed commander-in-chief and led troops to garrison it.) Yijin Pass was also established as Ba Prefecture. Opening editorial annotation. (The 《History of Song》, Biography of Han Lingkun, records that he was made commander-in-chief of Ba Prefecture and led his troops to garrison it.) That day Vanguard Commander Zhang Zangying routed several hundred Khitan horsemen north of Waqiao Pass and captured Gu'an County. An edict mobilized corvée labor from Bin and Di to build fortifications at Ba Prefecture. On gengxu Li Chongjin, commander-in-chief of the Palace Guard, was sent through Tumen into the Hedong frontier. On renzi the imperial procession left Xiong Prefecture for the capital. Liu Congxiao of Quanzhou sent Vice Prefect Wang Yuxi with tribute to the mobile court; because Quanzhou, like Jiangnan, had just submitted and Li Jing had returned to allegiance, the Emperor did not wish to seize its territory and merely issued an edict of praise. On dingmao the Western Capital reported the death of Situ Xu, retired director of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices. On jisi Li Chongjin reported defeating the Hedong rebel army at Baijing with two thousand enemy killed. On jiaxu the Emperor returned from Xiong Prefecture. Opening editorial annotation. (The 《Retreating Broom Miscellany》 records that after Shizong secured the three passes he fell ill and withdrew; at Chanyuan he lingered and would not move on; even chancellors and close ministers who came to inquire after his health were denied audience, and alarm spread through court and country. Zhang Yongde was then military commissioner of Chanyuan; as husband of a daughter of Taizu of Zhou, he alone was admitted to the sickroom, and through him the ministers urged: "The realm is unsettled and the capital exposed; regional lords on every side would welcome turmoil in the capital. Chanyuan and Bian are close; if you do not return at once to reassure the people, yet linger here for fear of a day's journey—should the worst come, what becomes of the ancestral temples?" Yongde agreed and, seizing a moment, urged Shizong as the ministers wished. Shizong asked, "Who put you up to this?" Yongde replied that all the ministers wished it. Shizong thought long and sighed: "I knew someone put you up to this—you alone fail to grasp my intent! Yet look at your paltry worth—how could you be fit for such a role!" That same day he ordered the imperial procession back to the capital.)
6
六月乙亥朔，潞州李筠奏，攻下遼州，獲偽刺史張丕旦。 丙子，以皇女薨輟朝三日。 戊寅，鳳翔奏，節度使李暉卒。 鄭州奏，河決原武，詔宣徽南院使吳延祚發近縣丁夫二萬人以塞之。 庚辰，命宣徽北院使昝居潤判開封府事。 晉州節度使楊廷璋奏，率兵入河東界，招降堡寨一十三所。 癸未，立魏王符彥卿女為皇后，仍令所司擇日備禮冊命。 以皇長子宗訓為特進左衛上將軍，封梁王； 以第二子宗讓為左驍衛上將軍，封燕國公。 賜江南進奉使李從善錢二萬貫、絹二萬匹、銀一萬兩，賜兩浙進奉使吳延福錢三千貫、絹五千匹、銀器三十兩。 丁亥，以前青州節度使李洪義為永興軍節度使，永興軍節度使王彥超移鎮鳳翔。 戊子，潞州部送所獲遼州刺史張丕旦等二百四十五人以獻，詔釋之。 己丑，宰臣範質、王溥並參知樞密院事。 以樞密使魏仁浦為中書侍郎、平章事、集賢殿大學士，依前充樞密使； 以宣徽南院使吳延祚為樞密使，行左驍衛上將軍； 以宋州節度使、侍衛都虞候韓通為侍衛親軍副都指揮使，加檢校太尉、同平章事； 澶州節度使兼殿前都點檢、駙馬都尉張永德落軍職，加檢校太尉、同平章事； 以今上為殿前都點檢，加檢校太傅，依前忠武軍節度使。 帝之北征也，凡供軍之物，皆令自京遞送行在。 一日，忽於地中得一木，長二三尺，如人之揭物者，其上卦全題云：「點檢做」，觀者莫測何物也。 至是，今上始受點檢之命，明年春，果自此職以副人望，則「點檢做」之言乃神符也。 辛卯，以宣徽北院使、判開封事昝居潤為左領軍上將軍，充宣徽南院使； 以三司使、左領衛大將軍張美為左監門衛上將軍，充宣徽北院使，判三司。 〈 （《東都事略·張美傳》：美少為三司小吏、澶州糧料使，世宗鎮澶州，每有求取，美悉力應之，及即位，連歲征討，糧饋無乏，美之力也。 然每思澶州所為，終不以公忠待之。）〉 癸巳，帝崩於萬歲殿，聖壽三十九。 甲午，宣遺制，梁王於柩前即皇帝位，服紀月日一依舊制。 是日，群臣奉梁王即位於殿東楹，中外發哀。 其年八月，翰林學士、判太常寺事竇儼上謚曰睿武孝文皇帝，廟號世宗。 十一月壬寅朔，葬於慶陵。 宰臣魏仁浦撰謚冊文，王溥撰哀冊文雲。 〈 （《五代史補》：世宗在民間，嘗與鄴中大商頡跌氏，忘其名，往江陵販賣茶貨。 至江陵，見有卜者王處士，其術如神，世宗因頡跌氏同往問焉。 方布卦，忽有一蓍躍出，卓然而立，卜者大驚曰：「吾家筮法十餘世矣，常記曾祖以來遺言，凡卜筮而蓍自躍而出者，其人貴不可言，況又卓立不倒，得非為天下之主乎！ 」遽起再拜。 世宗雖佯為詰責，而私心甚喜。 於逆旅中夜置酒，與頡跌氏半酣，戲曰：「王處士以我當為天子，若一旦到此，足下要何官，請言之。 」頡跌氏曰：「某三十年作估來，未有不由京洛者，每見稅官坐而獲利，一日所入，可以敵商賈數月，私心羨之。 若大官為天子，某願得京洛稅院足矣。 」世宗笑曰：「何望之卑耶！ 」及承郭氏之後踐祚，頡跌猶在，召見，竟如初言以與之。 世宗之征東也，駐蹕於高平，劉崇兼契丹之眾來迎戰。 時帥多持兩端，而王師不利。 親軍帥樊愛能等各退衄，世宗赫怒，躍馬入陣，引五十人直沖崇之牙帳。 崇方張樂飲酒，以示閑暇，及其奄至，莫不驚駭失次，世宗因以奮擊，遂敗之，追奔於城下。 凱旋，駐蹕潞州，且欲出其不意以誅退衄者，乃置酒高會，指樊愛能等數人責之曰：「汝輩皆累朝宿將，非不能用兵者也，然退衄者無他，誠欲將寡人作物貨賣與劉崇爾。 不然，何寡人親戰而劉崇始敗耶？ 如此則卿等雖萬死不足以謝天下，宜其曲膝引頸以待斧誅。 」言訖，命行刑壯士擒出皆斬之。 於是立功士以次行賞，自行伍拔於軍廂者甚眾，其恩威並著，皆此類也。 初，劉崇求援於契丹，得騎數千，及睹世宗兵少，侮之，曰：「吾觀周師易與爾，契丹之眾宜勿用，但以我軍攻戰，自當萬全。 如此則不惟破敵，亦足使契丹見而心服，一舉而有兩利，兵之機也。 」諸將以為然，乃使人謂契丹主將曰：「柴氏與吾，主客之勢，不煩足下余刃，敢請勒兵登高觀之可也。 」契丹不知其謀，從之。 洎世宗之陣也，三軍皆賈勇爭進，無不一當百，契丹望而畏之，故不救而崇敗。 論者曰：「世宗患諸將之難制也久矣，思欲誅之，未有其釁，高平之役，可謂天假，故其斬決而無貸焉。 自是姑息之政不行，朝廷始尊大，自非英主，其孰能為之哉！ 世宗既下江北，駐蹕於建安，以書召偽主。 偽主惶恐，命鐘謨、李德明為使，以見世宗。 德明素有詞辯，以利害說世宗使罷兵。 世宗且知之，乃盛陳兵師，排旗幟戈戟，為鹿項道以湊禦，然後引德明等入見。 世宗謂之曰：「汝江南自以為唐之後，衣冠禮樂世無比，何故與寡人隔一帶水，更不發一使奉書相問，惟泛海以通契丹，舍內事外，禮將安在？ 今又聞汝以詞說寡人罷兵，是將寡人比六國時一群癡漢，何不知人之甚也！ 汝慎勿言，當速歸報汝主，令徑來跪寡人兩拜，則無事矣。 不然，則寡人須看金陵城，借府庫以犒軍，汝等得無悔乎！ 」於是德明等戰懼，不能措一辭，即日告歸。 及見偽主，具陳世宗英烈之狀，非四方所能敵。 偽主計無所出，遂上表服罪，且乞保江南之地，以奉宗廟、修職貢，其詞甚哀。 世宗許之，因曰：「叛則征，服則懷，寡人之心也。 」於是遣使者賫書安之，然後凱還。 論者以世宗加兵於江南，不獨臨之以威，抑亦諭之以禮，可謂得大君之體矣。 陳摶，陜西人，能為詩，數舉不第，慨然有塵外之趣，隱居華山，自是其名大振。 世宗之在位也，以四方未服，思欲牢籠英傑，且以摶曾踐場屋，不得志而隱，必有奇才遠略，於是召到闕下，拜左拾遺。 摶不就，堅乞歸山，世宗許之。 未幾，賜之書：「敕陳摶，朕以汝高謝人寰，棲心物外，養太浩自然之氣，應少微處士之星，既不屈於王侯，遂甘隱於巖壑，樂我中和之化，慶乎下武之期，而能遠涉山塗，暫來城闕，浹旬延遇，宏益居多，白雲暫駐於帝鄉，好爵難縻於達士。 昔唐堯之至聖，有巢、許為外臣，朕雖寡薄，庶遵前鑒。 恐山中所闕，已令華州刺史每事供須。 乍反故山，履茲春序，緬懷高尚，當適所宜，故茲撫問，想宜知悉。 」即陶穀之詞也。 初，摶之被召，嘗為詩一章云：「草澤吾皇詔，圖南摶姓陳。 三峰十年客，四海一閑人。 世態從來薄，詩情自得真。 超然居物外，何必使為臣。 」好事者欣然謂之答詔詩。 世宗以張昭遠好古直，甚重之，因問曰：「朕欲一賢相，卿試為言朝廷誰可。 」昭遠對曰：「以臣所見，莫若李濤。 」世宗常薄濤之為人，聞昭遠之舉甚驚，曰：「李濤本非重厚，朕以為無大臣體，卿首舉此何也？ 」昭遠曰：「陛下所聞止名行，曾不問才略如何耳。 且濤事晉高祖，曾上疏論邠州節度使張彥澤蓄無君心，宜早圖之，不然則為國患。 晉祖不納，其後契丹南侵，彥澤果有中渡之變，晉社殲焉。 先帝潛龍時，亦上疏請解其兵權，以備非常之變，少主不納，未幾先帝遂有天下。 以國家安危未兆間，濤已先見，非賢而何？ 臣所以首舉之者，正為此也。 」世宗曰：「今卿言甚公，然此人終不可於中書安置。 」居無何，濤亦卒。 濤為人不拘禮法，與弟浣雖甚雍睦，然聚語之際，不典之言，往往間作。 浣娶禮部尚書竇寧固之女，年甲稍高，成婚之夕，竇女出參，濤輒望塵下拜，浣驚曰：「大哥風狂耶？ 新婦參阿伯，豈有答禮儀！ 」濤應曰：「我不風，只將謂是親家母。 」浣且漸且怒。 既坐，竇氏復拜，濤又叉手當胸，作歇後語曰：「慚無竇建，繆作梁山，喏喏喏！ 」時聞者莫不絕倒。 凡濤於閨門之內，不存禮法也如此，世宗以為無大臣體，不復任用，宜哉！ 世宗志在四方，常恐運祚速而功業不就，以王樸精究術數，一旦從容問之曰：「朕當得幾年？ 」對曰「陛下用心，以蒼生為念，天高聽卑，自當蒙福。 臣固陋，輒以所學推之，三十年後非所知也。 」世宗喜曰：「若如卿言，寡人當以十年開拓天下，十年養百姓，十年致太平足矣。 」其後自瓦橋關回戈，未到關而晏駕，計在位止及五年餘六個月，五六乃三十之成數也，蓋樸婉而言之。 世宗末年，大舉以取幽州，契丹聞其親征，君臣恐懼，沿邊城壘皆望風而下，凡蕃部之在幽州者，亦連宵遁去。 車駕至瓦橋關，探邏是實，甚喜，以為大勛必集，登高阜，因以觀六師。 頃之，有父老百餘輩持牛酒以獻，世宗問曰：「此地何名？ 」對曰：「歷世相傳，謂之病龍臺。 」默然，遽上馬馳去。 是夜，聖體不豫，翌日病亟，有詔回戈，未到關而晏駕。 先是，世宗之在民間也，常夢神人以大傘見遺，色如郁金，加《道經》一卷，其後遂有天下。 及瓦橋不豫之際，復夢向之神人來索傘與經，夢中還之而驚起，謂近侍曰：「吾夢不祥，豈非天命將去耶！ 」遂召大臣，戒以後事。 初，幽州聞車駕將至，父老或有竊議曰：「此不足憂。 且天子姓柴，幽州為燕，燕者亦煙火之謂也，此柴入火不利之兆，安得成功。 」卒如其言。）〉
In the sixth month, on the new-moon day yihai, Li Jun of Luzhou reported the capture of Liaozhou and the rebel prefect Zhang Pidan. On bingzi court was suspended for three days upon the death of an imperial daughter. On wuyin Fengxiang reported the death of Military Commissioner Li Hui. Zhengzhou reported a breach of the Yellow River at Yuanwu; Wu Yanzuo of the Palace Secretariat South Bureau was ordered to mobilize twenty thousand corvée workers from nearby counties to stem it. On gengchen Zan Jurun of the Palace Secretariat North Bureau was assigned to administer Kaifeng. Yang Tingzhang, military commissioner of Jinzhou, reported leading troops into Hedong and winning the surrender of thirteen fortified stockades. On guiwei the daughter of Fu Yanqing, Prince of Wei, was named empress, with orders to the relevant offices to choose a day for the investiture ceremony. The eldest son Zongxun was appointed special advancement Left Guard general-in-chief and enfeoffed as Prince of Liang; the second son Zongrang was appointed Left Valiant Cavalry general-in-chief and enfeoffed as Duke of Yan. Li Congshan, Jiangnan tribute envoy, received twenty thousand strings of cash, twenty thousand bolts of silk, and ten thousand taels of silver; Wu Yanfu, Two Zhes tribute envoy, received three thousand strings of cash, five thousand bolts of silk, and thirty taels of silver vessels. On dinghai former Qingzhou commissioner Li Hongyi was appointed military commissioner of Yongxing Army, and Wang Yanchao was transferred from Yongxing to Fengxiang. On wuzi Luzhou presented Zhang Pidan, captured prefect of Liaozhou, and 245 other prisoners; an edict ordered their release. On jichou chancellors Fan Zhi and Wang Pu were both appointed to participate in Bureau of Military Affairs affairs. Grand Councilor Wei Renpu was appointed vice director of the Secretariat, grand councilor, and grand academician of the Hall for Gathering Worthies, while retaining his post as grand councilor; Wu Yanzuo of the Palace Secretariat South Bureau was made grand councilor, acting as Left Valiant Cavalry general-in-chief; Han Tong, Songzhou commissioner and Palace Guard chief of staff, was named deputy commander-in-chief of the Palace Guard with the added honorary titles of grand marshal and associate grand councilor; Zhang Yongde, military commissioner of Chanyuan, commander-in-chief of the Palace Front, and imperial son-in-law commandant, was stripped of military posts and given the honorary titles of grand marshal and associate grand councilor; The present sovereign was appointed commander-in-chief of the Palace Front with the honorary title of grand mentor, while retaining his post as military commissioner of Zhongwu Army. During the northern campaign all army supplies were relayed from the capital to the mobile court. One day a piece of wood two or three feet long was unearthed, shaped like a person lifting an object; its surface bore a full hexagram inscription reading "The inspector-general is made," and none who saw it could explain its meaning. Now the present sovereign first received the post of inspector-general; the following spring he indeed rose from that office to fulfill public expectation—so the inscription "The inspector-general is made" proved a divine omen. On xinmao Zan Jurun, North Bureau commissioner and Kaifeng administrator, was appointed Left Leading Army general-in-chief and commissioner of the Palace Secretariat South Bureau; Zhang Mei, commissioner of the Three Departments and major general of the Left Leading Guard, was appointed Left Gate Guard general-in-chief, commissioner of the Palace Secretariat North Bureau, and administrator of the Three Departments. Opening editorial annotation. (The 《Eastern Capital Miscellany》, Biography of Zhang Mei, records that in youth Mei was a minor clerk of the Three Departments and Chanyuan grain commissioner; while Shizong governed Chanyuan, Mei met every requisition; after his accession, supplies never failed through years of campaigning—thanks to Mei.) Yet Shizong often recalled their Chanyuan dealings and never treated him with the loyalty due a public servant.) On guisi the Emperor died in the Hall of Ten Thousand Years at the age of thirty-nine. On jiawu the testamentary edict was proclaimed; the Prince of Liang took the throne before the bier, with mourning observances following established custom. That day the officials installed the Prince of Liang at the hall's eastern pillar, and mourning was proclaimed throughout the realm. In the eighth month of that year Dou Yan, Hanlin academician and administrator of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices, proposed the posthumous title Sagacious Martial Filial Literary Emperor and the temple name Shizong. In the eleventh month, on the new-moon day renyin, he was interred at Qing Mausoleum. Chancellor Wei Renpu composed the posthumous-title text and Wang Pu the lamentation text. Opening editorial annotation. (The 《Supplement to the History of the Five Dynasties》 records that while still a commoner, Shizong once traveled with a great merchant of Ye of the Jiedie clan—whose personal name is lost—to Jiangling to sell tea. At Jiangling they found a diviner, Wang the Recluse, whose skill seemed supernatural; Shizong went with the Jiedie merchant to consult him. As the hexagram was being cast, a yarrow stalk suddenly leaped out and stood upright. The diviner cried in alarm: "Our family's method has passed down more than ten generations. My ancestors taught that when a stalk leaps out by itself, the querent's rank is beyond telling—and when it stands upright without falling, can he be other than ruler of all under Heaven?" He sprang up and bowed twice. Shizong pretended to rebuke him but was secretly delighted. That night at an inn, half drunk with the Jiedie merchant, he jested: "Wang the Recluse says I am destined for the throne. If that day comes, what post would you like? Name it." The merchant replied: "I have traded for thirty years and always passed through the capital. Tax officers sit and collect a day's revenue equal to a merchant's for months—I have long envied them. If you become emperor, the capital tax bureau is all I ask." Shizong laughed. "How modest your ambition!" When he succeeded the Guo house and took the throne, the Jiedie merchant was still alive. Shizong summoned him and granted the post exactly as he had promised. On his eastern campaign Shizong halted at Gaoping; Liu Chong advanced with Khitan allies to meet him. Many commanders wavered, and the imperial army faltered. Palace Guard commanders including Fan Aineng fell back in defeat. Shizong flew into a rage, spurred into the line, and with fifty men charged straight at Chong's headquarters. Chong was feasting with music to show his ease; when Shizong struck suddenly, his men panicked. Shizong pressed the attack, routed them, and pursued to the city walls. After victory he halted at Luzhou, planning to surprise and execute the commanders who had retreated. At a grand feast he singled out Fan Aineng and others: "You are veteran generals of many reigns, not incompetents—yet you retreated for no reason but to sell me to Liu Chong like goods on a market. Otherwise why did Liu Chong fall only when I fought in person?" For this, ten thousand deaths would not atone to the realm. Bend your knees and stretch your necks for the axe." When he had finished, executioners seized them and beheaded them all. Meritorious soldiers were rewarded in turn, and many were promoted from the ranks—his blend of mercy and severity was always of this sort. Liu Chong had earlier sought Khitan aid and received several thousand horsemen. Seeing how few Zhou troops Shizong had, he scorned them: "The Zhou army is easy meat. Keep the Khitan out of it—my troops alone will win without fail." That way we defeat the enemy and make the Khitan see our strength and submit—two gains in one stroke, the art of war." The generals agreed and sent word to the Khitan commander: "This is a contest between the Chai and ourselves as host and guest. Spare your blades—hold your men on the heights and watch." The Khitan, unaware of the ruse, agreed. When Shizong deployed, every unit fought with such fury that one man matched a hundred. The Khitan looked on in fear and did not intervene, and Chong was defeated. Commentators say Shizong had long chafed at unruly generals and wished to execute them but lacked occasion; the Gaoping campaign was heaven's opportunity, and he showed no mercy. Indulgence toward commanders ended thereafter, and the court gained new authority—only an heroic ruler could have done it. After Shizong crossed the Yangtze he halted at Jian'an and summoned the rebel ruler by letter. The rebel ruler in alarm sent Zhong Mo and Li Deming as envoys to Shizong. Deming was a skilled speaker and urged Shizong with arguments of profit and loss to halt the campaign. Shizong already knew his intent and paraded his army, lining banners and halberds in a narrow "deer-neck" passage to the throne before admitting Deming and the others. Shizong told them: "Your Jiangnan styles itself heir to Tang, with ritual and music unmatched in the age—yet though only a river separates us, you send no envoy with letters of greeting, but sail the sea to deal with the Khitan, neglecting the inner for the outer. Where is your ritual?" Now you come with rhetoric to persuade me to halt the army—you take me for one of the fools of the Warring States. How little you know me!" Say no more. Return at once and tell your lord to come kneel before me in two bows, and all will be well. Otherwise I shall have to visit Jinling and borrow its treasury to reward my army—will you not regret it?" Deming and his party trembled with fear and could not utter a word; they asked to depart that same day. On meeting their lord they described Shizong's heroic bearing in full, beyond what any rival could match. The rebel ruler, at his wits' end, submitted a memorial confessing guilt and begging to keep Jiangnan to maintain his ancestral temples and pay tribute, in the most abject terms. Shizong agreed and said: "Rebellion brings punishment; submission brings mercy—that is my policy." He sent envoys with letters of reassurance and returned in triumph. Commentators hold that in pressing Jiangnan Shizong wielded not only force but also ritual instruction—truly the bearing of a great sovereign. Chen Tuan of Shaanxi was a poet who failed the examinations several times, embraced life beyond the world of affairs, and retired to Mount Hua, where his fame spread widely. While on the throne Shizong, with the realm not yet unified, sought to win over outstanding men. Because Tuan had entered the examination halls, failed to advance, and retired, Shizong assumed he must possess rare talent and summoned him to court as Left Remembrancer. Tuan declined and firmly begged to return to the mountains; Shizong consented. Soon after, Shizong sent him a letter: "To Chen Tuan: You have loftily withdrawn from the world, set your heart beyond material things, nourished the primordial breath of nature, and answered the star of the recluse. Unwilling to bow to princes, you gladly dwell in mountain depths, rejoice in Our harmonious rule, and celebrate this age of martial glory. Yet you traveled far from your peaks to visit the capital; for more than ten days We gained much from your presence. White clouds briefly rested in the imperial city, but fine rank cannot bind a man of true attainment." Of old the sage Emperor Yao had Youchao and Xu as ministers beyond the court; though We are unworthy, We hope to follow that precedent. Fearing you may lack for anything on the mountain, We have ordered the prefect of Hua to provide your needs. You have just returned to your mountain as spring unfolds; We recall your lofty withdrawal and trust all is well—hence this message of inquiry. May you receive it in good understanding." The letter was composed by Tao Gu. When first summoned, Tuan composed a poem: "From the wilds came Our Emperor's edict; Chen Tuan plots his course southward. Ten years a guest of the three peaks; one idle man in all the realm." The world's ways have ever been shallow; in poetry alone he found truth. Transcendent beyond the world of things—why need one serve as minister?" Enthusiasts gladly called it his poem replying to the imperial edict. Shizong greatly valued Zhang Zhaoyuan for his love of antiquity and upright character and asked him: "I want a worthy chancellor—who in court would you recommend?" Zhaoyuan replied: "In my view, none equals Li Tao." Shizong had always disdained Tao's character and was startled: "Li Tao is not grave or steady; he lacks the bearing of a great minister—why recommend him first?" Zhaoyuan said: "Your Majesty has heard only of his reputation and conduct—you have never asked about his talent and strategy." Moreover, serving Emperor Gaozu of Later Jin, Tao once memorialized that Zhang Yanzhe, military commissioner of Bin, harbored treasonous intent and should be dealt with early, lest he become a national disaster. Gaozu did not heed him; later the Khitan invaded south, Yanzhe brought about the catastrophe at Zhongdu, and the Jin dynasty perished. When Taizu was still in obscurity, Tao also memorialized to strip him of military authority against unforeseen change; the young emperor refused, yet soon Taizu won the realm. Before danger or safety had shown any sign, Tao had already foreseen it—if that is not wisdom, what is? That is precisely why I recommend him first. Shizong said: "Your argument is fair, yet this man can never serve in the Secretariat." Before long Tao died as well. Tao paid little heed to ritual propriety. Though deeply affectionate with his younger brother Huan, when they gathered, improper remarks often slipped out. Huan married the daughter of Minister of Rites Dou Ninggu, who was somewhat older. On the wedding night, when the bride came out to pay respects, Tao bowed low toward her. Huan cried in alarm: "Brother, have you lost your mind?" When a new bride greets her husband's elder brother, what return courtesy is there?" Tao replied: "I'm not mad—I thought she was my mother-in-law." Huan was both ashamed and furious. When they were seated the Dou lady bowed again; Tao clasped his hands to his chest and composed a trailing rhyme: "Ashamed to lack Dou Jian, wrongly playing Liangshan—yes, yes, yes!" All who heard it collapsed with laughter. Such was Tao's disregard for propriety even within the family; that Shizong judged him unfit for high office and never employed him again was only right. Shizong's ambition embraced the four quarters, yet he feared his reign would be brief and his achievements unfinished. Wang Pu was expert in numerology, and one day Shizong casually asked: "How many years have I?" He replied: "Your Majesty's heart is set on the people; Heaven is high yet hears the lowly—you will surely be blessed. I am crude, but by what I have learned I can say only that beyond thirty years I cannot know." Shizong was pleased and said: "If so, I shall spend ten years conquering the realm, ten years nurturing the people, and ten years bringing great peace—that will be enough." Later, turning back from Waqiao Pass, he died before reaching it; his reign lasted only five years and six months—five and six make thirty's rounded sum, Wang Pu's oblique way of speaking. In his final years Shizong launched a major campaign for Youzhou. Hearing he led the army in person, the Khitan court was terrified; border fortresses surrendered at the first approach, and tribal groups around Youzhou fled overnight. When the imperial procession reached Waqiao Pass and scouts confirmed the reports, he was delighted, believing triumph was assured; he climbed a height to review the army. Soon more than a hundred elders came with oxen and wine as gifts. Shizong asked: "What is this place called?" They answered: "For generations it has been called the Terrace of the Ailing Dragon." He fell silent, mounted at once, and galloped away. That night he fell ill; the next day his condition worsened. An edict ordered the army to turn back, but he died before reaching the pass. Earlier, while still a commoner, Shizong often dreamed that a divine being gave him a great umbrella the color of saffron, together with a scroll of the Dao Classic; afterward he won the realm. When he fell ill at Waqiao he dreamed again that the divine being came to reclaim the umbrella and classic; in the dream he returned them and woke with a start, telling his attendants: "This dream is ill-omened—has Heaven's mandate begun to leave me?" He summoned his ministers and charged them with affairs to come. When Youzhou heard the imperial procession was coming, some elders whispered: "This is no cause for alarm. The emperor's surname is Chai; Youzhou is Yan, and yan also means flame. Chai entering fire is an ill omen—how can he succeed?" In the end it proved exactly as they said.)

史臣曰：世宗頃在仄微，尤務韜晦，及天命有屬，嗣守鴻業，不日破高平之陣，逾年復秦、鳳之封，江北、燕南，取之如拾芥，神武雄略，乃一代之英主也。 加以留心政事，朝夕不倦，摘伏辯奸，多得其理。 臣下有過，必面折之，常言太祖養成二王之惡，以致君臣之義，不保其終，故帝駕馭豪傑，失則明言之，功則厚賞之，文武參用，莫不服其明而懷其恩也。 所以仙去之日，遠近號慕。 然稟性傷於太察，用刑失於太峻，及事行之後，亦多自追悔。 逮至末年，漸用寬典，知用兵之頻並，憫黎民之勞苦，蓋有意於康濟矣。 而降年不永，美誌不就，悲夫！
The historiographer writes: Shizong long dwelt in obscurity and practiced self-concealment; when Heaven's mandate fell to him he inherited the great enterprise, soon broke the enemy at Gaoping, within a year recovered Qin and Feng, and took lands north of the Yangtze and south of Yan as easily as picking up mustard seed—divinely martial and strategically bold, a heroic ruler of his age. He devoted himself tirelessly to government, working morning and night without rest; in exposing hidden crimes and judging treachery he usually hit the mark. When ministers erred he rebuked them to their faces. He often said that Taizu had indulged the two kings' wickedness until the bond between ruler and minister could not endure—so he ruled his heroes openly: faults he named plainly, merit he rewarded generously. Civil and military men alike served under him, and all submitted to his clarity and cherished his grace. When he departed this life, men far and near mourned him with cries of longing. Yet his nature was marred by excessive scrutiny, and his punishments were often too harsh; afterward he frequently regretted them himself. In his final years he turned gradually to lenient rule, recognizing how often he had waged war and pitying the people's suffering—he seems to have aimed at restoring peace and prosperity. Yet his years were cut short and his noble ambition unfulfilled—how lamentable!

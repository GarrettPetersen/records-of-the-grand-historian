職官誌
Treatise on Official Posts
2
夫官非位無以分貴賤，位非品無以定高卑，是以歷代史官，咸有所紀，皆窮源而討本，期與世以作程。 迨乎唐祚方隆，玄宗在宥，采累朝之故事，考眾職之遐源，申命才臣，著成《六典》，其勛階之等級，品秩之重輕，則已備載於其中矣。 故今之所撰，不敢相沿，祖述五代之命官，以踵百王之垂範，或厘革升降，則謹而誌之，俾後之為天官卿者，得以觀焉。
Without ranks, offices could not distinguish noble from common; without grades, ranks could not fix precedence high and low. That is why historians of every era recorded these matters, tracing each institution to its origin, so that their age might have a standard to follow. When Tang fortunes rose and Emperor Xuanzong held court, he collected precedents from successive reigns, traced the ancient origins of every office, and charged able ministers to compile the Administrative Regulations, which already set out in full the grades of merit ranks and the relative weight of titles and ranks. The present compilation therefore does not simply follow the Tang model but traces how the Five Dynasties appointed officials, continuing the precedents of earlier kings. Where offices were reorganized or ranks raised or lowered, those changes are recorded carefully, so that future Directors of the Bureau of Astronomy may consult them.
3
梁開平三年三月，詔升尚書令為正一品。 按《唐六典》，尚書令正二品，是時以將授趙州王镕此官，故升之。
In the third month of the third year of Kaiping (909), Liang issued an edict promoting the Minister-in-Chief to first rank, grade one. Under the Tang Administrative Regulations the Minister-in-Chief was second rank, grade one; because Wang Rong of Zhao was about to receive the post, the rank was raised.
4
後唐天成四年八月，詔曰：「朝廷每有將相恩命，準往例，諸道節度使帶平章事、兼侍中、中書令，並列銜於敕牒後，側書『使』字。 今兩浙節度使錢镠是元帥、尚父，與使相名殊，承前列銜，久未改正。 湖南節度使馬殷，先兼中書令之時，理宜齒於相位，今守太師、尚書令，是南省官資，不合列署敕尾。 今後每署將相敕牒，宜落下錢镠、馬殷官位，仍永為常式。」
In the eighth month of the fourth year of Tiancheng (929), Later Tang issued an edict: Whenever the court granted a favor to a general or minister, precedent required that circuit military commissioners who held Grand Councilor, concurrent Palace Attendant, or Director of the Secretariat have their titles listed after the edict, with the character "Commissioner" written beside them. Qian Liu of the Two Zhes was Grand Marshal and Esteemed Father, titles distinct from those of commissioner-ministers, yet he had long been listed in the old order without correction. Ma Yin of Hunan, when he had concurrently held Director of the Secretariat, properly ranked with the chief ministers; now he held Grand Preceptor and Minister-in-Chief, southern-department posts inappropriate for listing at the end of edicts. Henceforth, whenever edicts for generals and ministers were issued, Qian Liu's and Ma Yin's titles were to be omitted, and this was to remain the standing rule."
5
梁開平二年四月，改左右丞為左右司侍郎，避廟諱也。 至後唐同光元年十月，復舊為左右丞。
In the fourth month of the second year of Kaiping (908), Liang renamed the Left and Right Vice Directors as Left and Right Department Vice Ministers, to avoid the imperial temple taboo. In the tenth month of the first year of Tongguang (923), Later Tang restored the former titles of Left and Right Vice Director.
6
後唐長興元年九月，詔曰：「臺轄之司，官資並設，左右貂素來相類，左右揆不至相懸，以此比方，豈宜分別。 自此宜升尚書右丞官品，與左丞並為正四品。」
In the ninth month of the first year of Changxing (930), Later Tang issued an edict: In the censorial departments, offices and ranks were established together; the left and right Palace Attendants had always been comparable, and the left and right Vice Directors had never been far apart—by that analogy, how could they be treated differently? From then on the Right Vice Director of the Department of State Affairs was to be raised to the same rank as the left, both at fourth rank, grade one."

8
後唐長興四年九月，敕：「馮赟有經邦之茂業，宜進位於公臺，但緣平章事字犯其父名，不欲斥其家諱，可改同平章事為同中書門下二品。 」後至周顯德中，樞密使吳廷祚亦加同中書門下二品，避其諱也。
In the ninth month of the fourth year of Changxing (933), an edict said: Feng Yun had outstanding merit in governing the state and deserved advancement to the chief ministry, but because the title Grand Councilor violated his father's name and he wished to avoid his family taboo, Concurrent Grand Councilor was changed to Concurrent Second Rank of the Secretariat and Chancellery. Later, in the Xiande era of Zhou, Military Affairs Commissioner Wu Tingzuo received the same title, likewise to avoid his taboo.
9
晉天福五年二月，敕：「以門下侍郎、中書侍郎並為清望正三品。 」晉天福九月，詔曰：「《六典》云：中書舍人掌侍奉進奏參議表章，凡詔旨制敕、璽書策命，皆按故事起草進畫，既下，則署而行之。 其禁有四：一曰漏泄，二曰稽緩，三曰違失，四曰忘誤，所以重王命也。 古昔已來，典實斯在，爰從近代，別創新名。 今運屬興王，事從師古，俾仍舊貫，以耀前規。 其翰林學士院公事，宜並歸中書舍人。」
In the second month of the fifth year of Tianfu (940), Jin issued an edict: The Vice Directors of the Chancellery and Secretariat were both to be esteemed clear offices at third rank, grade one. In the ninth month of Tianfu, Jin issued an edict: The Administrative Regulations state that the Secretariat Drafter managed attendance, memorial submission, consultation, and drafting; all edicts, ordinances, imperial orders, sealed letters, and appointments were to be drafted according to precedent, submitted for imperial approval, and once issued signed and promulgated. Four prohibitions applied: leakage, delay, error, and forgetfulness or mistake—by these the royal command was honored. From antiquity the canonical practice had stood; only in recent times had a new name been created. Now that fortune belonged to a rising king, affairs were to follow the ancients, restoring the old practice to honor former standards. The official business of the Hanlin Academy was to be returned entirely to the Secretariat Drafters."
10
七年五月，中書門下上言：：有司檢尋長興四年八月二十一日敕：準《官品令》，侍中、中書令正三品，按《會要》，大歷二年十一月升為正二品； 左右常侍從三品，按《會要》，廣德二年五月升為正三品； 門下中書侍郎正四品，大歷二年十一月升為正三品； 諫議大夫正五品，按《續會要》，會昌二年十二月升為正四品，以備中書門下四品之闕； 御史大夫從三品，會昌二年十二月升為正三品； 御史中丞正五品，亦與大夫同時升為正四品。 」敕：「宜各準元敕處分，仍添入令文，永為定制。 」又詔：「門下侍郎，班在常侍之下，俸祿同常侍。」
In the fifth month of the seventh year, the Secretariat and Chancellery reported: The relevant offices had searched the edict of 21 August in the fourth year of Changxing: Under the Statutes on Official Ranks, the Palace Attendant and Director of the Secretariat were third rank, grade one; under the Institutional Compendium, in the eleventh month of the second year of Dali (767) they had been raised to second rank, grade one; the Left and Right Regular Attendants were third rank, grade two; under the Institutional Compendium, in the fifth month of the second year of Guangde (763) they had been raised to third rank, grade one; the Vice Directors of the Chancellery and Secretariat were fourth rank, grade one; in the eleventh month of the second year of Dali they had been raised to third rank, grade one; the Remonstrance Grand Master was fifth rank, grade one; under the Continued Institutional Compendium, in the twelfth month of the second year of Huichang (842) he had been raised to fourth rank, grade one, to fill the fourth-rank vacancy in the Secretariat and Chancellery; the Censor-in-Chief was third rank, grade two; in the twelfth month of the second year of Huichang he had been raised to third rank, grade one; the Vice Censor-in-Chief was fifth rank, grade one; he had been raised at the same time as the Censor-in-Chief to fourth rank, grade one. An edict ordered: Each post was to be handled according to the original edict and added to the statute text as a permanent regulation. Another edict said: The Vice Director of the Chancellery ranked below the Regular Attendant but received the same salary."
11
周顯德五年六月，敕：「諫議大夫宜依舊正五品上，仍班位在給事中之下。 」按《唐典》，諫議大夫四員，正五品上，皆隸門下省，班在給事中之下。 至會昌二年十一月，中書門下奏，升為正四品下，仍分為左右，以備兩省四品之闕，故其班亦升在給事中之上。 近朝自諫議大夫拜給事中者，官雖序遷，位則降等，至是以其遷次不倫，故改正焉。
In the sixth month of the fifth year of Xiande (958), Zhou issued an edict: The Remonstrance Grand Master was to remain at fifth rank, grade one upper, with court rank still below the Drafting Attendant. Under the Tang Regulations there were four Remonstrance Grand Masters at fifth rank, grade one upper, all subordinate to the Chancellery and ranking below the Drafting Attendant. In the eleventh month of the second year of Huichang (842), the Secretariat and Chancellery had memorialized to raise them to fourth rank, grade one lower, divided into left and right to fill fourth-rank vacancies in the two departments; their court rank was therefore raised above the Drafting Attendant. In recent courts, promotion from Remonstrance Grand Master to Drafting Attendant advanced the title in sequence but lowered rank; because this order was irregular, it was now corrected.

後唐清泰二年十一月，制：「以前同州節度使、檢校太尉、同平章事馮道為守同空。 」時議者曰：「自隋、唐以來，三公無職事，自非親王不恒置，於宰臣為加官，無單置者。 」道在相位時帶司空，及罷鎮，未命官，議者不練故事，率意行之。 及制出，言議紛然，或云便可綜中書書下事或云須冊拜開府。 及就列，無故事，乃不就朝堂敘班，臺官兩省官入就列，方入，宰臣退，踵後先退。 劉句又以罷相為僕射，出入就列，一與馮道同，議者非之。 及晉天福中，以李纮為司徒，周廣順初，以竇貞固為司徒，蘇禹珪為司空，遂以為例，議者不復有雲。
In the eleventh month of the second year of Qingtai (935), an appointment named the former Tong Prefecture military commissioner, Acting Grand Preceptor and Concurrent Grand Councilor Feng Dao Acting Minister of Works. Commentators at the time said: Since Sui and Tang the Three Dukes had held no duties; unless one was an imperial prince they were not regularly appointed; for chief ministers they were supplementary titles, never held alone. While in office Feng Dao had borne Acting Minister of Works; when he left his commission he received no new appointment, and commentators unversed in precedent acted on impulse. When the appointment was issued, debate was clamorous: some said he could at once oversee Secretariat business, others that he must receive investiture and open a bureau. When he took his place at court there was no precedent; he did not enter the hall to take rank in order. Censorate and two-department officials entered first; only then did he enter. When the chief ministers withdrew, he followed close behind and left first. Liu Xu, having left the chief ministry for Vice Director, entered and left court the same way as Feng Dao; commentators criticized this. In the Tianfu era of Jin, Li Hong was made Minister of Education; at the beginning of Guangshun of Zhou, Dou Zhengu was made Minister of Education and Su Yugui Minister of Works; it then became precedent, and commentators no longer objected.

後唐天成元年夏六月，以李琪為御史大夫，自後不復除。 其年冬十一月丙子，諸道進奏官上言：「今月四日，中丞上事，臣等禮合至臺，比期不越前規，依舊傳語，忽蒙處分通出，尋則再取指揮，要明審的。 又蒙問：大夫相公上事日如何？ 臣等訴云：大夫曾為宰相，進奏官伏事中書，事體之間，實為舊吏。 若以別官除授，合雲傳語勞來，又堅令通出。 臣等出身藩府，不會朝儀，拒命則恐有奏聞，遵稟則全隳則例，伏恐此後到臺參賀，儀則不定者。 」詔曰：「御史臺是大朝執憲之司，乃四海繩違之地，凡居中外，皆所整齊，藩侯尚展於公參，邸吏豈宜於抗禮。 遽觀論列，可驗侮輕，但以喪亂孔多，紀綱隳紊，霜威掃地，風憲銷聲。 今則景運惟新，皇圖重正，稍加提舉，漸止澆訛。 宜令御史臺，凡關舊例，並須舉行，如不稟承，當行朝典。 」時盧文紀初拜中丞，領事於御史府，諸道進奏官來賀，文紀曰：「事例如何？ 」臺吏喬德威等言：「朝廷在長安日，進奏官見大夫中丞，如胥吏見長官之禮。 及梁氏將革命，本朝微弱，諸藩強據，人主大臣姑息邸吏，時中丞上事，邸吏雖至，皆於客次傳語，竟不相見。 自經兵亂，便以為常。 」文紀令臺吏諭以舊儀相見，據案端簡，通名贊拜。 邸吏輩既出，怒不自勝，相率於閣門求見，騰口喧訴。 明宗謂趙鳳曰：「進奏官比外何官？ 」鳳對曰：「府縣發遞祗候之流也。 」明宗曰：「乃吏役耳，安得慢吾法官。 」乃下此詔。
In the sixth month of summer in the first year of Tiancheng (926), Later Tang appointed Li Qi Censor-in-Chief; thereafter the post was never again filled. That winter, on the day bingzi of the eleventh month, the circuit memorial-presenting officials submitted: On the fourth of that month, when the Vice Censor-in-Chief took office, they should by ritual have come to the Censorate. Expecting no change from precedent, they had asked to convey messages as before, but were suddenly ordered admitted through. They then sought instructions again, wanting a clear ruling. They were also asked: On what day would the Censor-in-Chief take office? They replied: The Censor-in-Chief had formerly been a chief minister; memorial-presenting officials attended upon the Secretariat—in substance they were his former subordinates. Had he been appointed to a different office, it would have been proper to convey messages of greeting, yet they were firmly ordered admitted through. They came from frontier commissions and did not know court ritual; to refuse risked a memorial to the throne, yet to obey would wholly destroy precedent—they feared that hereafter when they came to the Censorate to offer congratulations, the ritual would be unsettled. An edict said: The Censorate was the great court's department for enforcing the law, the place where the realm was bound for violations; all within and without were put in order by it. Even frontier lords still came to public audience—how could lodge clerks resist proper ritual? Their arguments at once showed contempt and disrespect; yet because disorders had been many, regulations had collapsed, censorial authority had been swept away, and the wind of censure had fallen silent. Now auspicious fortune was renewed and the imperial design set right again; with slight encouragement, the flood of corruption would gradually cease. The Censorate was ordered: wherever old precedents applied, they must all be carried out; if they were not obeyed, the court statutes would be enforced. At that time Lu Wenji had just been appointed Vice Censor-in-Chief and was conducting affairs at the Censorate. Circuit memorial-presenting officials came to congratulate. Wenji asked: What is the precedent? Censorate clerks Qiao Dewei and others said: When the court was in Chang'an, memorial-presenting officials who met the Censor-in-Chief or Vice Censor-in-Chief observed the ritual of clerks meeting their superiors. When the House of Liang was about to seize power, the court was weak and frontier commissions held sway; sovereign and ministers indulged the lodge clerks. When a Vice Censor-in-Chief took office, though lodge clerks came, they all conveyed messages in the reception room and never met face to face. Since the military disorders, this had been taken as normal. Wenji ordered the censorate clerks to instruct them in the old ritual of meeting face to face: seated upright at the desk with tablets in hand, announcing names and performing the bow of praise. When the lodge clerks left, unable to contain their anger, they went in a body to the Gate Office to seek audience and clamored in loud complaint. Emperor Mingzong asked Zhao Feng: Compared with outside offices, what rank were memorial-presenting officials? Feng replied: They were of the sort who dispatch couriers and attend at prefectures and counties. Mingzong said: They are mere clerical servants—how can they slight my law officers? Thereupon this edict was issued.
16
晉天福五年二月，以御史中丞為清望正四品。 按《唐典》，御史中丞正五品上，今始升之。 三年三月壬戌，御史臺奏：「按《六典》，侍御史掌糾舉百僚，推鞫獄訟，居上者判臺，知公廨雜事，次和西推、贓贖、三司受事，次知東推、理匭。 」敕宜依舊制。 遂以駕部員外郎兼侍御史知雜事劉皞為河南少尹，自是無省郎知雜者。
In the second month of the fifth year of Tianfu (940), Jin made the Vice Censor-in-Chief an esteemed clear office at fourth rank, grade one. Under the Tang Regulations the Vice Censor-in-Chief had been fifth rank, grade one upper; now he was raised for the first time. On the day renxu of the third month of the third year, the Censorate memorialized: Under the Administrative Regulations, the Attending Censor supervised impeachment of officials and investigation of lawsuits; the senior adjudicated the Censorate and managed bureau affairs; the next handled the Western Investigation, booty redemption, and cases received by the Three Offices; the next managed the Eastern Investigation and the complaint box. An edict ordered that the old regulations be followed. Thereupon Liu Hao, Vice Director of the Bureau of Equipage who concurrently served as Attending Censor in charge of miscellaneous affairs, was made Vice Governor of Henan; from then on no provincial bureau director served as miscellaneous-affairs censor.
17
開運二年八月，敕：「御史臺準前朝故事，以郎中、員外郎一人兼侍御史知雜事，近年停罷，獨委年深御史知雜。 振舉之間，紀綱未峻，宜遵舊事，庶葉通規。 宜卻於郎署中選清慎強幹者，兼侍御史知雜事。」
In the eighth month of the second year of Kaiyun (945), an edict said: The Censorate, following former-court precedent, had one Director or Vice Director concurrently serve as Attending Censor in charge of miscellaneous affairs; in recent years this had been suspended, and only a senior censor was entrusted with miscellaneous affairs. In the effort to restore vigor, regulations had not been strict; the old practice should be followed so that the general rules might be harmonized. From the bureau directors one who was pure, cautious, and capable should again be chosen to serve concurrently as Attending Censor in charge of miscellaneous affairs."

──右御史臺
— The Censorate —
19
後唐同光元年十月，崇政院依舊為樞密院，命宰臣郭崇韜兼樞密使，亦置直院一人。
In the tenth month of the first year of Tongguang (923), Later Tang restored the Court for Honoring Governance as the Bureau of Military Affairs; chief minister Guo Chongtao was ordered to serve concurrently as Military Affairs Commissioner, and one duty officer was also appointed.
20
晉天福四年四月，以樞密副使張從恩為宣徽使，權廢樞密院故也。 先是，晉祖以宰臣桑維翰兼樞密使，懇求免職，只在中書，遂以宣徽使劉處讓代之，每有奏議，多不稱旨。 其後處讓丁憂，乃以樞密印付中書門下，故有是厘改也。
In the fourth month of the fourth year of Tianfu (939), Jin made Vice Military Affairs Commissioner Zhang Cong'en Commissioner of the Palace Secretariat because the Bureau of Military Affairs had been provisionally abolished. Earlier, the Jin founder had chief minister Sang Weihan serve concurrently as Military Affairs Commissioner; he earnestly sought relief to remain only in the Secretariat, so Palace Secretariat Commissioner Liu Churang replaced him; whenever he submitted proposals, they often failed to please. Later Churang entered mourning, and the Military Affairs seal was handed to the Secretariat and Chancellery; hence this reorganization.
21
開運元年六月，敕依舊置樞密院，以宰臣桑維翰兼樞密使，從中書門下奏請也。
In the sixth month of the first year of Kaiyun (944), an edict restored the Bureau of Military Affairs as before and had chief minister Sang Weihan serve concurrently as Military Affairs Commissioner, following a memorial from the Secretariat and Chancellery.
22
周顯德六年六月，命司徒平章事範質、禮部尚書平章事王溥並參知樞密院事。
In the sixth month of the sixth year of Xiande (959), Zhou ordered Minister of Education and Grand Councilor Fan Zhi and Minister of Rites and Grand Councilor Wang Pu both to participate in Bureau of Military Affairs affairs.
23
梁開平元年四月，始置建昌院，以博王友文判院事，以太祖在藩時，四鎮所管兵車賦稅、諸色課利，按舊簿籍而主之。 其年五月，中書門下奏請以判建昌院事為建昌宮使，仍以東京太祖潛龍舊宅為宮也。 二年二月，以侍中 〈 （案：原本有闕文，據《五代會要》，以侍中韓建判建昌宮事。）〉 判建昌宮事。 至十月，以尚書兵部侍郎李皎為建昌宮副使。 三年九月，以門下侍郎平章事薛貽矩兼延資庫使，判建昌宮事。 至四年十二月，以李振為建昌宮副使。 乾化二年五月，以門下侍郎平章事於兢兼延資庫使，判建昌宮事。 其年六月，廢建昌宮，以河南尹魏王張宗奭為國計使，凡天下金谷兵戎舊隸建昌宮者悉主之。 至後唐同光四年二月，以吏部尚書李琪為國計使。 自後廢其名額不置。
In the fourth month of the first year of Kaiping (907), Liang first established the Jianchang Bureau, with Prince of Bo Youwen as director; it administered the troops, chariots, taxes, and various levies of the four commissions the Founder had governed while in the frontier, according to the old registers. That year, in the fifth month, the Secretariat and Chancellery memorialized that the Jianchang Bureau director should become Commissioner of the Jianchang Palace, using the Founder's former Eastern Capital residence where the hidden dragon had dwelt as the palace. In the second month of the second year, the Palace Attendant [ (Note: The original text is missing here. According to the Institutional Compendium of the Five Dynasties, Palace Attendant Han Jian was made director of Jianchang Palace affairs.)] He was made director of Jianchang Palace affairs. In the tenth month, Li Jiao, Vice Director of the Bureau of Personnel in the Department of State Affairs, was made Vice Commissioner of the Jianchang Palace. In the ninth month of the third year, Xue Yiju, Vice Director of the Chancellery and Grand Councilor, was made concurrent Commissioner of the Extended Resources Treasury and director of Jianchang Palace affairs. In the twelfth month of the fourth year, Li Zhen was made Vice Commissioner of the Jianchang Palace. In the fifth month of the second year of Qianhua (912), Yu Jing, Vice Director of the Chancellery and Grand Councilor, was made concurrent Commissioner of the Extended Resources Treasury and director of Jianchang Palace affairs. In the eleventh month of the first year of Tongguang (923), Later Tang made Li Shaohong, General of the Left Gate Guards and acting director of the Palace Domestic Service, concurrent Internal Auditor; all revenue registers of the realm were entrusted to his judgment and disposal. From then on the provisioning accounts of prefectures and counties became lavish and costly; commentators criticized this. Moreover, the title Internal Auditor was regarded as an inauspicious expression. In the first month of the second year (925), an edict placed the three offices of Salt and Iron, Revenue, and Census: all matters concerning money and goods were entrusted to the Commissioner of Tax and Corvée, following Liang's old system. In the fourth month of the first year of Tiancheng (926), an edict abolished the Tax and Corvée Bureau and restored the three offices of Salt and Iron, Census, and Revenue as before, with one chief minister exclusively adjudicating them. In the eighth month of the first year of Changxing (930), Zhang Yanlang, military commissioner of Xu Prefecture, was made Acting Minister of the Bureau of Works and Commissioner of the Three Offices, ranking below the Commissioner of the Palace Secretariat. The establishment of a commissioner for the Three Offices began with Yanlang. Since the Tang dynasty, the Census and Revenue offices had managed currency and goods; the Salt and Iron office sometimes had a commissioner by title, while the Census and Revenue offices were adjudicated by the directors and vice directors of their bureaus within the Department of State Affairs. In the Tianbao era, Yang Shenjin, Wang Hong, and Yang Guozhong in succession used methods of amassing revenue to flatter the sovereign and win favor; yet all retained their original posts in the Census and Revenue offices, bearing commissioner titles separately, and made no changes to the system. Later Liu Yan and Diwu Qi also followed the old system. Thereafter chief ministers also adjudicated one office each, and no commissioner title was established. After Qianfu, warfare arose throughout the realm; wherever needed a Commissioner of Tax and Corvée was appointed to manage mobilization, and when troops were dismissed the post ceased. Under Liang the Commissioner of Tax and Corvée was then established, monopolizing the realm's currency and goods. When Emperor Zhuangzong restored the dynasty, those in power were unversed in precedent and followed Liang's example, restoring the Commissioner of Tax and Corvée and entrusting the former Wei-Bo clerk Kong Qian exclusively with its affairs. The gathering of resentment throughout the realm and the cutting down of the royal house were in truth due to the abuses of the Tax and Corvée system. When Emperor Mingzong succeeded to the throne, he wished to reform its abuses; before he had even taken office, he issued an edict abolishing the commissioner title and ordering only one senior minister to adjudicate the affairs, called Adjudicator of the Three Offices. At this time Yanlang entered from Xu Prefecture to take charge of national accounts again; he informed the Military Affairs Commissioner and requested that the title of the Three Offices be established. The matter was announced to the Secretariat for deliberation. The chief ministers memorialized in reply according to the old system, appointing Yanlang Special Advancement and Acting Minister of the Bureau of Works, Commissioner of Salt and Iron and Transport for the circuits, and concurrently adjudicating Census and Revenue affairs, following the old system. Mingzong did not agree and ultimately used the title Commissioner of the Three Offices.

In the autumn, ninth month, day gengshen of the second year of Qingtai (935), the Bureau of Merit Evaluation submitted: In the fifth month of that year, within the sealed memorial submitted by Hanlin academician Cheng Xun, he requested that from the chief ministers and hundred executors, frontier military commissioners, and prefects—all officials concerned with public affairs—annual performance records be written and their relative merit compared. They then searched the evaluation provisions in the Book of Tang, the Administrative Regulations, and the Institutional Compendium, and ordered that evaluation grades be written. The request was approved. Commentators at the time said: The method of evaluating performance was the old system of Tang Yao and the Three Dynasties. Western Han used the six criteria of the inspector to examine prefects and governors; the five bureaus of the Department of State Affairs summarized all achievements—the law was especially precise, and officials were held to account. At the end of Han came disorder and separation, and the old regulations fell into disuse. Emperor Wu of Wei provisionally established grades in the army, debated the purity or corruption of officials, and appointed men according to clerical rank, abruptly departing from former rules. Since Sui and Tang it was first written into statutes. In Han, prefects and governors entered office as the Three Dukes; after Wei and Jin, government lay in the Secretariat, the Left and Right Vice Directors managed state affairs, viewing the forbidden precinct before noon and the departments after noon—all offices of the three terraces and hundred duties were under their overall supervision. Judging by this, upon what basis could chief ministers be evaluated? From the end of Tianbao, after commissioners were provisionally established for various affairs, all matters followed inertia; the various bureaus of the Department of State Affairs gradually became nominal without substance; the system had long been abandoned, and it was unknown upon what basis supervision and accountability could rest. What Cheng Xun submitted also did not clarify the origins in detail; though the relevant offices at the time made some exposition, for the most part officials still had no evaluations to compare.

In the eleventh month of the third year of Tianfu (938), Attendant of the Palace Secretariat Yin Peng memorialized: He had heard that under the Bureau of Appointments regulations, when civil and military officials within and without had just entered court rolls, those without living parents were at once given posthumous enfeoffment and gifts, while those whose parents were still alive received no grant and no enfeoffment. In his view this was truly impermissible: it esteemed the dead and slighted the living, abandoned the living man and honored the dead—what honor was there in that? Where was the principle in it? Regulations also held that when parents were living and rank and grade met the standard, only the mother was enfeoffed—the father was not included—yet a fief title was granted and she was styled Grand Lady. This made the wife seem as if she had no husband beside her and the son as if he had no father above him—could the father be base while the mother was noble, the husband low while the wife was exalted? If the father did not qualify for added favor, how could the mother receive the gift; if through the son one was fit to be ennobled, how could the father not be enfeoffed first? He submitted that the father is honored and the mother humble—such is the way of Heaven and Earth; honor admits no second supreme, and the state follows the same principle. To enfeoff the father without rank was contrary to name and teaching—nothing was graver than this. He humbly begged that hereafter, when civil and military officials' parents were living and already held office or rank, those parents be advanced in qualification and rank according to the regulations, or if unfit for salary and office be granted retirement status or concurrent regular rank, so that the wife might receive grant enfeoffment. Then both parents would be honored alike, and the filial son would feel no failure to reach them; the inner chambers would shine together, and the sage ruler would extend the grace of reward and gift. Ah! Those who received the emperor's breeze of filial depth and enjoyed the salary of honoring their parents—counting quietly on one's fingers—numbered no more than a few. The emperor could specially deliberate and put this into practice, compile it as a regulation, encourage all under Heaven to do good, and let the realm look to the wind—naturally revealing the lacunae of former ages and completing the great ceremony of the dynasty. Moreover, in the grace edict of the first year of Changxing (930) there was one passage reading "All officials within and without the court whose parents are living shall alike receive added favor." The Bureau of Appointments had not implemented the clear regulation but clung to the earlier text; if the new favor were promulgated together with the old edict, serving ruler and serving father would constantly follow one integrated rule; as son and as minister, one would not lose the meaning of fulfilling both duties. He also noted that under Bureau of Appointments regulations, officials within and without whose office rank reached fifth rank or above received enfeoffment of wife and yin privilege for sons—making no distinction between prestigious and lesser ranks, but speaking only of rank and grade. The Remonstrating Censor, Drafting Attendant, and Secretariat Drafter were all fifth rank; the Supporter of Goodness, Groom, Aide, and Palace Attendant were also fifth rank. But if one spoke of the court's entrustment, the chief ministers' deliberation, the grades of access and advance, and the regard within and without, they were as far apart as heaven and earth. Yet in grant enfeoffment they were treated alike—carried down to this point, it was very wrong. Moreover, the Northern Department served as the emperor's attendant ministers, the Southern Palace managed state planning, and the Censorate held law and discipline—they led all officials, together forming the Three Departments, and their posts were especially weighty, their responsibility and expectation by no means light. Thus those of the prestigious ranks might serve ten years without fulfilling their wish for conspicuous honor; while those of the mixed ranks, after two terms, at once received enfeoffment and yin privilege. The matter was not fair and should be reformed. He humbly begged that hereafter all officials of fifth rank and above follow the old system, while officials of the three departments' prestigious posts and of sixth rank and above receive enfeoffment and yin privilege at once. Since prestigious and lesser ranks differed, rank and grade should be raised; the matter should also be sent down to the appropriate offices to deliberate and fix as a permanent regulation. " The emperor approved the request.

On the day wuwu in the third month of spring in the first year of Yingshun (960), the Directorate of the Imperial Clan memorialized: "By precedent, each imperial tomb had one Director and one Vice Director; recently Director and Vice Director had not both been appointed, and the local county magistrate had been entrusted to serve concurrently. Because Henan and Luoyang were the capital region, they feared that concurrent service as Director and Vice Director would be inconvenient." " An edict then specially established one Director and one Vice Director of the Tomb Platform each.

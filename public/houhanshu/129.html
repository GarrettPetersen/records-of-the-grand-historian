外十二州，每州刺史一人，六百石。 本注曰：秦有监御史，监诸郡，汉兴省之，但遣丞相史分刺诸州，无常官。 孝武帝初置刺史十三人，秩六百石。 成帝更为牧，秩二千石。 建武十八年，复为刺史，十二人各主一州，其一州属司隶校尉。
Beyond the metropolitan region lay twelve provinces, each overseen by a single regional inspector at the six-hundred-picul grade. Commentary: Under the Qin, supervising clerks watched over the commanderies; the Han at first abolished the office and merely dispatched secretaries from the chancellor’s staff on rotating inspections of the provinces, with no standing appointment. Emperor Wu initially appointed thirteen regional inspectors, each at the six-hundred-picul salary level. Under Emperor Cheng the title was changed to regional shepherd, with pay raised to two thousand piculs. In Jianwu 18 the office reverted to inspector; twelve men each governed one province, while the remaining province fell under the Colonel Director of Retainers at the capital.

蔡質漢儀曰：「詔書舊典，刺史班宣，周行郡國，省察治政，黜陟能否，斷理冤獄，以六條問事，非條所問，卽不省。 一條，強宗豪右，田宅踰制，以強陵弱，以衆暴寡。 二條，二千石不奉詔書，遵承典制，倍公向私，旁詔守利，侵漁百姓，聚斂爲姦。 三條，二千石不卹疑獄，風厲殺人，怒則任刑，喜則任賞，煩擾苛暴，剝戮黎元，爲百姓所疾，山崩石裂，妖祥訛言。 四條，二千石選署不平，苟阿所愛，蔽賢寵頑。 五條，二千石子弟怙恃榮勢，請託所監。 六條，二千石違公下比，阿附豪強，通行貨賂，割損政令。 諸州刺史初除，比諸持板揖不拜。」 獻帝起居注曰：「建安十八年三月庚寅，省州幷郡，復禹貢之九州。 冀州得魏郡、安平、鉅鹿、河閒、淸河、博陵、常山、趙國、勃海、甘陵、平原、太原、上黨、西河、定襄、鴈門、雲中、五原、朔方、河東、河内、涿郡、漁陽、廣陽、右北平、上谷、代郡、遼東、遼東屬國、遼西、玄菟、樂浪，凡三十二郡。 省司隸校尉，以司隸部分屬豫州、冀州、雍州。 省涼州刺史，以幷雍州部，郡得弘農、京兆、左馮翊、右扶風、上郡、安定、隴西、漢陽、北地、武都、武威、金城、西平、西郡、張掖、張掖屬國、酒泉、敦煌、西海、漢興、永陽、東安南，凡二十二郡。 省交州，以其郡屬荊州。 荊州得交州之蒼梧、南海、九眞、交趾、日南，與其舊所部南陽、章陵、南郡、江夏、武陵、長沙、零陵、桂陽，凡十三〔郡〕。 益州本部郡有廣漢、漢中、巴郡、犍爲、蜀郡、牂牁、越巂、益州、永昌、犍爲屬國、蜀郡屬國、廣漢屬國，今幷得交州之鬱林、合浦，凡十四〔郡〕。 豫州部郡本有潁川、陳國、汝南、沛國、梁國、魯國，今幷得河南、滎陽都尉，凡八郡。 徐州部郡得下邳、廣陵、彭城、東海、琅邪、利城、城陽、東莞，凡八郡。 靑州得齊國、北海、東萊、濟南、樂安，凡五郡。」 獻帝春秋曰：「孫權以歩騭行交州刺史。」 東觀書曰：「交趾刺史，持節。」 諸州常以八月巡行所部郡國，胡廣注曰：「巡謂驛馬也。 縣次傳駕之，以走疾，猶古言附遂。」 録囚徒，胡廣曰：「縣邑囚徒，皆閲録視，參考辭狀，實其眞偽。 有侵冤者，卽時平理也。」 考殿最。 胡廣曰：「課第長吏不稱職者爲殿，舉免之。 其有治能者爲最。 察上尤異州，又狀州中吏民茂才異等，歳舉一人。」 初歳盡詣京都奏事，胡廣曰：「所察有條應繩異者，輒覆問之，不茹柔吐剛也。 歳盡，齎所狀納京師，名奏事，差其遠近，各有常會。」 中興但因計吏。 胡廣曰：「不復自詣京師，其所道皆如舊典。」 東觀書曰：「和帝初，張酺上言：『臣聞王者法天，熒惑奏事太微，故州牧刺史入奏事，所以通下問知外事也。 數十年以來，重其道歸煩撓，故時止勿奏事，今因以爲故事。 臣愚以爲刺史視事滿歳，可令奏事如舊典，問州中風俗，恐好惡過所道，事所聞見，考課衆職，下章所告，及所自舉有意者賞異之，其尤無狀，逆詔書，行罪法，冀敕戒其餘，令各敬愼所職，於以衰滅貪邪便佞。』」 韓詩外傳曰：「王者必立牧，方三人，所以使闚遠牧衆也。 遠方之民，有飢寒而不得衣食，獄訟而冤失，職賢而不舉者，入告天子。 天子於其君之朝也，揖而進之曰：『意朕之政教，有不得爾者邪？ 如何乃有飢寒而不得衣食，獄訟而冤失，職賢而不舉？』 然後其君退而與其卿大夫謀之。 遠方之民聞，皆曰『誠天子也』。 夫我居之辟，見我之近也； 我居之幽，見我之明也。 可欺乎哉！ 可欺乎哉！ 故牧者所以開四目，通四聰。」
Cai Zhi's Han Observances records: "By ancient edict, inspectors proclaimed imperial orders, toured commanderies and kingdoms, inspected governance, promoted or dismissed officials, redressed wrongful imprisonment, and investigated affairs under six articles; matters outside those articles were not pursued. Article one: powerful clans whose estates exceeded regulations, bullying the weak and oppressing the few. Article two: grand administrators who defied edicts, pursued private gain, preyed on the people, and amassed wealth corruptly. Article three: grand administrators who ignored doubtful cases, killed recklessly, punished in anger and rewarded in joy, tormented the people, and were hated amid omens and false reports. Article four: grand administrators who appointed unfairly, favored cronies, hid the worthy, and indulged the obstinate. Article five: sons and younger brothers of grand administrators who abused their fathers' power to seek favors within the inspector's jurisdiction. Article six: grand administrators who curried favor with the powerful, accepted bribes, and undermined government orders. Newly appointed provincial inspectors bowed with tablet in hand without performing the full prostration." Emperor Xian's Daily Records note: "In the third month of Jian'an 18, on gengyin, provinces were merged and commanderies cut to restore the nine provinces of Yu's Tribute. Jizhou absorbed thirty-two commanderies including Wei, Anping, Julu, Hejian, Qinghe, Boling, Changshan, Zhao, Bohai, Ganling, Pingyuan, Taiyuan, Shangdang, Xihe, Dingxiang, Yanmen, Yunzhong, Wuyuan, Shuofang, Hedong, Henei, Zhuo, Yuyang, Guangyang, Right Beiping, Shanggu, Dai, Liaodong, Liaodong Dependent State, Liaoxi, Xuantu, and Lelang. The Colonel Director of Retainers was abolished and the metropolitan region was divided among Yuzhou, Jizhou, and Yongzhou. Liangzhou was abolished and merged into Yongzhou, comprising twenty-two commanderies from Hongnong and Jingzhao west through Dunhuang and the frontier districts. Jiaozhou was abolished and its commanderies transferred to Jingzhou. Jingzhou absorbed Cangwu, Nanhai, Jiuzhen, Jiaozhi, and Rinan from the old Jiaozhou together with Nanyang, Zhangling, Nan, Jiangxia, Wuling, Changsha, Lingling, and Guiyang—thirteen commanderies in all. Yizhou retained Guanghan, Hanzhong, Ba, Qianwei, Shu, Zangke, Yuexi, Yizhou, Yongchang, and the three dependent states, and also received Yulin and Hepu from Jiaozhou—fourteen commanderies in all. Yuzhou originally comprised Yingchuan, Chen, Runan, Pei, Liang, and Lu and now also received Henan and the Xingyang commandant—eight commanderies in all. Xuzhou comprised Xiapi, Guangling, Pengcheng, Donghai, Langye, Licheng, Chengyang, and Dongguan—eight commanderies in all. Qingzhou comprised Qi, Beihai, Donglai, Jinan, and Le'an—five commanderies in all." The Chronicle of Emperor Xian records: "Sun Quan had Bu Zhi serve as acting inspector of Jiaozhou." The Book of the Eastern Lodge notes: "The inspector of Jiaozhi bore the credential staff." Each province customarily made an autumn circuit of its commanderies and kingdoms. Hu Guang explains: "The tour used relay horses. At each county relay teams drove them at speed, as in the ancient term for attached relay transport." They reviewed prisoners. Hu Guang says: "County prisoners were examined, testimony checked, and truth verified. Cases of oppression or injustice were promptly redressed." They rated administrative performance. Hu Guang says: "Officials ranked lowest for incompetence were recommended for dismissal. Those with governing ability were rated best. Outstanding cases were reported upward, and one man of special talent among the province's officials and people was recommended each year." Earlier, inspectors journeyed to the capital at year's end to report. Hu Guang says: "Doubtful or unusual findings were reinvestigated; they did not favor the weak over the strong. At year's end they carried their reports to the capital—called reporting affairs—with fixed schedules according to distance." After the restoration this duty devolved on the accounting clerks who came up with the tribute tallies. Hu Guang adds: "Inspectors no longer went in person; their reports followed the old canon." The Book of the Eastern Lodge records: "Early in Emperor He's reign Zhang Ti memorialized: 'The king models Heaven; Mars reports in the Supreme Palace Enclosure, so regional shepherds and inspectors reported at court to learn of distant affairs. For decades the journey burdened the court, so reporting was suspended and that suspension became precedent. I propose that after a full year in office inspectors resume reporting as of old—inquiring into provincial customs, reviewing all offices, rewarding merit, and punishing those who defy edicts, so the rest may be admonished and greedy flatterers diminished.'" Han Ying's Outer Commentary on the Odes says: "Kings must appoint shepherds—three for each direction—to watch from afar and govern the people. If distant peoples suffered hunger, wrongful imprisonment, or failure to recommend the worthy, the shepherd reported to the Son of Heaven. At that lord's audience the Son of Heaven bowed him forward and asked: 'Do you think my government has failed you? How then are there hunger and cold, wrongful lawsuits, and failure to recommend the worthy?' The lord then withdrew to consult his ministers and grandees. Hearing this, distant peoples said, 'Truly a Son of Heaven.' Though I dwell afar, he sees me as near; though I dwell in obscurity, he sees me as bright. Can one be deceived! Can one be deceived! Therefore shepherds open the four eyes and connect the four ears of the ruler."

臣昭曰：昔在先代，列爵殊等，九服不同，畿荒制異。 雖連帥相司，牧伯分長，而封疆置限，兼庸有數，如身之使臂，手之使指，故能髙卑相固，遠近維緝，群後克穆，共康兆庶。 爰及周衰，稍競呑廣，邦國侵爭，遞懷貪略，猶歴數百年，乃能成其幷一，豈非樹之有本，使其然乎？ 秦兼天下，開設郡縣，孤立獨王，卽以顛亡。 漢祖因循，雖不頓革，分置子弟，終龕諸呂之難，漸剖列郡，以減大都之權。 後嚴安之徒，猶慷慨發憤，謂千里之威，卽古之強國，慮非安本無窮之計也。 孝武之末，始置刺史，監糾非法，不過六條，傳車周流，匪有定鎭，秩裁數百，威望輕寡，得有察舉之勤，未生陵犯之釁。 成帝改牧，其萌始大，旣非識治之主，故無取焉爾。 世祖中興，監乎政本，復約其職，還遵舊制，斷親奏事，省入惜煩，漸得自重之路。 因茲以降，彌於歳年，母后當朝，多以弱守，六合危動，四海潰弊，財盡力竭，綱維撓毀，而八方不能内侵，諸侯莫敢入伐，豈非幹強枝弱，控制素重之所致乎？ 至孝靈在位，橫流旣及，劉焉徼偽，自爲身謀，非有憂國之心，專懷狼據之策，抗論昏世，薦議愚主，盛稱宜重牧伯，謂足鎭壓萬里，挾姦樹筭，苟罔一時，豈可永爲國本，長期勝術哉？ 夫聖主御世，莫不大庇生民，承其休謀，傳其典制。 猶雲事久獘生，無或通貫，故變改正服，革異質文，分爵三五，參差不一。 況在豎騃之君，挾姦詐之臣，共所創置，焉可仍因？ 大建尊州之規，竟無一日之治。 故焉牧益土，造帝服於岷、峨； 袁紹取冀，下制書於燕、朔； 劉表荊南，郊天祀地； 魏祖據兗，遂構皇業：漢之殄滅，禍源乎此。 及臻後代，任寄彌廣，委之邦宰之命，授之斧鉞之重，假之都督之威，開之征討之略。 晉太康之初，武帝亦疑其然，乃詔曰：「上古及中代，或置州牧，或置刺史，置監御史，皆總綱紀，而不賦政，治民之事，任之諸侯郡守。 昔漢末四海分崩，因以呉、蜀自擅，自是刺史内親民事，外領兵馬，此一時之宜爾。 今賴宗廟之靈，士大夫之力，江表平定，天下合之爲一，當韜戢干戈，與天下休息。 諸州無事者罷其兵，刺史分職，皆如漢氏故事，出頒詔條，入奏事京城。 二千石專治民之重，監司淸峻於上，此經久之體也。 其便省州牧。」 晉武帝又見其獘矣，雖有其言，不卒其事，後嗣纘繼，牧鎭愈重，據地分爭，竟覆天下。 昔王畿之大，不過千里，州之所司，廣袤兼遠。 爭強虎視之辰，遷鼎革終之日，未嘗不藉蕃兵之權，挾董司之力，逼迫伺隙，陵奪沖幼。 其甚者臣主揚兵，骨肉戰野，昆弟梟懸，伯叔屠裂。 末壯披心，尾大不掉，旣用此始，亦病以終。 傾輈愈襲，莫或途改，致雒京有銜璧之痛，秦臺有不守之酷。 胡、羌遞興，氐、鮮更起，摩滅群黎，流禍百世。 堅冰所漸，兼縁茲蠹。 嗚呼！ 後之聖王，必不久滯斯跡，靈長之終，當有神筭。 不然，則雄捍反拒之事，懼甚於此心，憑強作害之謀，方盛於後意。
Chen Zhao writes: In antiquity noble ranks were graded, the nine service zones followed distinct rules, and metropolitan and frontier administrations differed in form. Though overlords and regional shepherds shared governance, borders were fixed and subordinate holdings numbered, like arm and finger—high and low reinforced one another, near and far were knit together, and the feudal lords jointly secured the people. As Zhou decayed, states swallowed one another in greedy wars; centuries passed before unity returned—did not deep roots make that possible? Qin conquered all under Heaven, imposed commanderies and counties, left the emperor isolated—and swiftly fell. Han Gaozu kept much of the old order yet enfeoffed kin; still the Lü crisis erupted, and the court slowly split great domains to curb metropolitan power. Later critics such as Yan An protested that a thousand-li jurisdiction equaled an ancient great power and warned that such scale could not secure the dynasty forever. Late in Emperor Wu's reign inspectors were introduced to police misconduct under six articles; they toured by relay carriage without fixed headquarters, held modest rank, and could investigate without yet threatening commandery power. Emperor Cheng elevated them to regional shepherds and the office swelled; he was no master of statecraft, so the change merits no praise. Emperor Guangwu, restoring the dynasty, trimmed the office back to the old inspector model, ended personal reporting at court, and thereby gave the post a path to renewed authority. Thereafter empress dowagers often reigned from weakness while the realm tottered; treasury and strength failed and institutions frayed—yet outsiders did not invade and princes dared not rebel. Was this not because the center stayed strong while branches were kept weak? Under Emperor Ling, Liu Yan schemed for himself, not the realm, urging heavier regional shepherds to control ten thousand li—a treacherous expedient, not a lasting foundation of state. Sage rulers shelter the people, inherit sound policy, and pass down established institutions. Even so, institutions age and decay; hence robes, rites, and noble ranks were repeatedly revised and never uniform. How much less should the creations of foolish rulers and scheming ministers be perpetuated unchanged? The grand project of exalted provinces produced not a single day of good government. Liu Yan as regional shepherd expanded his domain and donned imperial regalia in the Min-E region; Yuan Shao seized Ji and issued edicts across Yan and the northern frontier; Liu Biao in southern Jing performed suburban rites to Heaven and earth; Cao Cao held Yan and built an imperial enterprise—the extinction of Han began here. Later ages widened provincial commissions—territorial command, military authority, regional commissioners, and punitive campaigns. Early in Jin Taikang, Emperor Wu also doubted the trend and decreed: "In antiquity and the middle ages, regional shepherds, inspectors, or supervising clerks oversaw discipline but did not govern the people; that duty belonged to feudal lords and commandery governors. When Han fell, Wu and Shu seized power and inspectors took civil and military duties—a temporary expedient. Now, with the south pacified and the realm reunited, arms should be sheathed and the people allowed to rest. Peaceful provinces should disband troops; inspectors should resume Han practice—promulgating edicts abroad and reporting at the capital. Grand administrators should govern the people while inspectors remain stern overseers above—this is the lasting order. Regional shepherds should therefore be abolished." Emperor Wu of Jin saw the danger but did not finish the reform; successors made regional shepherds and garrisons heavier until partition overturned the realm. Once the royal domain spanned barely a thousand li, yet a province might govern vastly more. In ages of fierce rivalry and dynastic collapse, men always leaned on frontier armies and provincial commissioners to press weaknesses and seize the young. At worst, lord and minister took up arms, kin slaughtered kin, brothers were displayed on poles, and uncles were torn apart. The branch grew strong enough to split the trunk and the tail too heavy to wag—so the disease began and so it ended. Reversals piled on reversals until none changed course—Luoyang knew the agony of surrender and the Qin terrace fell undefended. Hu, Qiang, Di, and Xianbei rose in turn, grinding down the people and spreading calamity for generations. Hard ice forms by degrees—and this rot had long been gathering. Alas! Later sage kings will not long follow this path; enduring fortune should end in providential correction. Otherwise defiance born of strength will breed greater fear, and plots to harm by force will grow still worse.

Where a province’s seat of supervision was the capital region, a single metropolitan intendant at two thousand piculs and one deputy were appointed; elsewhere each commandery had one grand administrator at two thousand piculs and one assistant. On border commanderies charged with defense, the assistant was redesignated chief clerk. Chancellors of princely kingdoms followed the same rule. Each dependent state placed one chief commandant at the “comparable to two thousand piculs” grade, plus a single assistant. Commentary: Commanderies and kingdoms were responsible for governing the populace, recommending talent, encouraging good service, adjudicating litigation, and suppressing crime. Each spring they made a circuit of subordinate counties to promote agriculture and silk production and to aid the destitute. In autumn and winter they dispatched incorruptible subordinates to review prisoners, align sentences with the code, and grade performance. The statutes mention incorrupt capital clerks—the same sort later called fair-minded officers. The Sounds and Meanings of the History of Han glosses the phrase as "in documents nothing was twisted or wronged." Xiao He won appointment as chief clerk of Pei on the strength of his incorrupt record-keeping. At the close of the year clerks carried the annual accounts to their superiors. They nominated candidates for the filial-and-incorrupt quota at the rate of one man per two hundred thousand registered persons. A commandant oversaw military readiness and police functions; Emperor Jing renamed the post commandant of the commandery. Emperor Wu further assigned one commandant to each of the three capital adjuncts to control traffic in and out of those regions. Frontier commanderies appointed agricultural commandants to manage military colonies and grain production. Chief commandants of dependent states were added to administer surrendered non-Chinese peoples. After the restoration, in Jianwu 6, commandery-level commandants were eliminated and their powers folded into the grand administrator’s office, ending the empire-wide autumn drills. Interior pass commandants were dropped; frontier areas often kept commandants and dependent-state commandants, sometimes with split counties whose magistrates ruled populations comparable to a full commandery. When the Qiang revolted and the three adjuncts had to secure the imperial mausolea, Emperor An restored the Right Fufeng commandant and the Jingzhao “tiger-teeth” commandant. Ying Shao's Han Offices argues: "Heaven gave the five materials for common use; none can be discarded—who can abolish the military? The institution of arms is very old. The Book of Changes says, "Bend wood into a bow and carve wood into arrows; bow and arrow awe the realm." The Spring and Autumn tradition holds, "Three seasons for agriculture, one for martial training." The Odes praise Duke Liu: "Not idle, not at ease—farming in peace and fighting in war, packing dry rations, bearing arms (Xi) [raised aloft], and none in the four quarters could stand against him." Once commanderies disbanded materiel officers and cavalry, local governments lost their guard and truly invited raiders. When trouble struck one sector, three others scrambled to help; mobilization crashed like thunder while everything was improvised on the spot and the common people fell into uproar. Without time to drill archery and chariotry or renew battle oaths, hurling raw levies against hardened foes was like sending doves after hawks or pigs against tigers—battle after battle was lost and imperial forces could not recover. Zhang Jiao spread demonic fraud; the realm shook; eight provinces rose together; smoke reddened the sky; governors were slaughtered and blood ran like rivers. Then distant frontier levies of alien custom—not our own people—were summoned; brutal and unchecked, they pressed good folk into service for private credit and treated wealth like refuse. Alas—the roots of mass displacement lie here; sending untrained men to battle is abandonment; trace the ruin and the lesson is no empty warning! The Spring and Autumn rule that households keep no armor unifies state authority and curbs private violence. Though the realm lies in ruins and royal authority is not yet secure, defense can still be managed at hand—hence the restoration of Right Fufeng commandant." Each administration staffed the usual bureaus with clerks and secretaries. The New Discourses says: "When Wang Mang created Xihai commandery, every clerk there served in person at the one-hundred-picul grade." Another account makes the grade four hundred piculs, with rotation and replacement every two years. Commentary: The bureau system paralleled the central ministries, minus the eastern and western personnel offices. A merit-clerk secretary handled selections and service records. A steward of the five bureaus oversaw the merit office and the remaining bureaus. Five route inspectors watched the dependent counties, each supported by a bureau clerk. The main gate was guarded by a single ward head. The master of records maintained correspondence and enforced meeting schedules. The post of order-clerk was omitted. Under the gate and in every bureau document assistants handled the written work.

Liu Shao's Treatise on Ranks begins: "The Spring and Autumn Annals mention Chief-of-the-Line Bao. The Lord of Shang established eighteen grades; with marquis-within-the-passes and full marquis there were twenty ranks in all, following ancient principle. In antiquity the Son of Heaven entrusted military affairs to the six ministers: in peace they farmed, in alarm they fought—trusted men leading the multitude. When Qi attacked Youhu he summoned the six ministers; grandees in the army served as generals. Zhou's six ministers likewise served in camp; in the state they bore civil titles, in the army military titles—hence the names differed. Qin followed antiquity: army ranks were granted in grades; leaders were corvée soldiers; merit ennoblement made them army officers. From the first rank through the fourth, Not Replaced, all were common soldiers. From grandee through the five ranks to Grand Grandee, they were comparable to grandees. The nine ranks followed the meaning of the nine commands. From Left Chief-of-the-Line to Great Chief-of-the-Line followed the meaning of the nine ministers. Marquis-within-the-passes followed the ancient meaning of viscounts and barons within the royal domain. Qin's capital lay west of the mountains with Guanzhong as the royal domain; hence the title marquis-within-the-passes. Full marquises followed the ancient meaning of feudal lords of ranked states. Thus the lower grades imitated antiquity, matching court institutions under different names to distinguish army and state. In antiquity one war chariot carried seventy-two foot soldiers flanking left and right. On the chariot the grandee stood left, the driver center, the warrior right—seventy-five men in all. The first rank, Public Warrior, was a ranked foot soldier. The second rank was Upper Accomplished. Accomplished means completed. In antiquity accomplished shi presented to the minister of education were called Accomplished Shi; though the name was borrowed, all were foot soldiers. The third rank, Hairpin Steed, drove the four-horse team. Yaoniao was an ancient famous horse. The four-horse driver resembled a hairpin; hence Hairpin Steed. The fourth rank was Not Replaced. Not Replaced meant chariot right, no longer sharing duties with ordinary corvée soldiers. The fifth rank was grandee. Grandee meant the one on the left of the chariot. The sixth through ninth ranks—Officer Grandee, Public Grandee, Public Chariot, and Grand Grandee—were all army officers. Commoners capped at Public Chariot could transfer rank to a son or same-birth kin. Thus Public Chariot was a high rank among army officers. Though not in battle one could obtain a public soldier's chariot; hence Public Chariot. Ranks ten through twenty ran from Left Chief-of-the-Line through Right Renewer, Lesser and Greater Upper Accomplished, Four-Chariot and Great Chief-of-the-Line, marquis-within-the-passes, and full marquis. From Left Chief-of-the-Line to Great Chief-of-the-Line all were ministers and grandees, all army generals. Those they led were commoners and corvée soldiers; hence the names commoner and renewer. Great Chief-of-the-Line was the grand general; Left and Right Chiefs-of-the-Line were left and right assistant generals."

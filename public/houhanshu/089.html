1
杜笃字季雅，京兆杜陵人也。 高祖延年，宣帝时为御史大夫。 笃少博学，不修小节，不为乡人所礼。 居美阳，与美阳令游，数从请托，不谐，颇相恨。 令怒，收笃送京师。 会大司马吴汉薨，光武诏诸儒诔之，笃于狱中为诔，辞最高，美帝之，赐帛免刑。
Du Du, whose courtesy name was Jiya, came from Duling in the metropolitan area around the capital. His forebear Yannian of the founding line had been imperial secretary under Emperor Xuan. As a young man he read widely but scorned petty etiquette, and the people of his village did not hold him in esteem. He lived at Meiyang and kept company with its magistrate, often asking favors through him; when those overtures failed, the two grew mutually bitter. The magistrate, in a fury, had him arrested and escorted to the capital. Just then Grand Marshal Wu Han died, and Guangwu summoned the literati to compose laments. From his cell Du Du produced one judged the finest; the emperor admired it, awarded him silk, and spared him further punishment.
2
笃以关中表里山河，先帝旧京，不宜改营洛邑，乃上奏《论都赋》曰：
Du Du argued that the Guanzhong heartland was shielded by mountains and rivers, that it had been the old capital of the Han founders, and that abandoning it for Luoyang was unwise; he therefore submitted his "Rhapsody on the Capital" in memorial form, which begins:
3
臣闻知而复知，是为重知。 臣所欲言，陛下已知，故略其梗概，不敢具陈。 昔般庚去奢，行俭于亳； 成周之隆，乃即中洛。 遭时制都，不常厥邑。 贤圣之虑，盖有优劣； 霸王之姿，明知相绝。 守国之势，同归异术； 或弃去阻厄，务处平易； 或据山带河，并吞六国； 或富贵思归，不顾见袭； 或掩空击虚，自蜀汉出，即日车驾，策由一卒； 或知而不从，久都墝埆。 臣不敢有所据。 窃见司马相如、杨子云作辞赋以讽主上，臣诚慕之，伏作书一篇，名曰《论都》，谨并封奏如左。
I have heard it said: to be told what one already knows is merely redundant knowledge. What I would say you already understand; I shall only sketch its gist and not rehearse every detail. Long ago Pan Geng renounced luxury and practiced austerity when he dwelt at Bo. When Chengzhou flourished, the royal house fixed itself in the central Luo plain. Each age set its capital as circumstance demanded; no single site was held forever. Even sages weighed such choices differently, some plans proving stronger than others. The posture of a hegemon and that of a true king spring from insight worlds apart. Defending a realm may aim at the same end yet follow contrary strategies. Some forsook defiles and rugged terrain for open, tractable country. Some hugged mountains and rivers and so swallowed the six rival kingdoms. Some, grown wealthy and exalted, longed for home and ignored the risk of a stab in the back. Some struck where defenses were thin, marching straight from Ba and Shu so that the imperial train rolled out in a day on a plan hatched by a single soldier. Some knew better yet refused to move, clinging for ages to bleak high ground. I do not presume to insist on any one doctrine. I have watched Sima Xiangru and Yang Xiong write fu to counsel their sovereigns; in humble emulation I have drafted a piece called "On the Capital," which I respectfully enclose herewith.
4
皇帝以建武十八年二月甲辰，升舆洛邑，巡于西岳。 推天时，顺斗极，排阊阖，入函谷，观厄于崤、黾，图险于陇、蜀。 其三月丁酉，行至长安。 经营宫室，伤愍旧京，即诏京兆，乃命扶风，斋肃致敬，告觐园陵。 凄然有怀祖之思，喟乎以思诸夏之隆。 遂天旋云游，造舟于渭，北<方亢>泾流。 千乘方毂，万骑骈罗，衍陈于岐、梁，东横乎大河。 瘗后土，礼□郊。 其岁四月，反于洛都。 明年，有诏复函谷关，作大驾宫、六王邸、高车厩于长安。 修理东都城门，桥泾、渭，往往缮离观。 东临霸、浐，西望昆明，北登长平，规龙首，抚未央，覛平乐，仪建章。
In the eighteenth year of Jianwu, on the day jiachen of the second month, the emperor left Luoyuan in his carriage and journeyed west toward the sacred peak. He read the celestial signs, followed the Pole Star, passed through the celestial gate, entered Hangu, surveyed the Xiao and Mian defiles, and took the measure of the Long and Shu fastnesses. That same month, on dingyou, he reached Chang'an. He inspected the palace works, mourned the fallen glory of the old capital, and at once commanded the metropolitan prefect and the governor of Feng to observe ritual purity and pay formal visit to the mausoleum parks. His heart filled with sorrow for the ancestors he had never seen; he sighed over the grandeur that had once been All-under-Heaven's center. Then the court wheeled like heaven's pivot and drifted like clouds: boats were launched on the Wei and the flotilla pressed north along the Jing's stream. A thousand chariots ran hub to hub, ten thousand horsemen filled the roads; battle lines stretched from Qi and Liang eastward across the Yellow River. He buried jade for the earth god at the suburban altar and offered the prescribed rites at the round-mound sacrifice. In the fourth month of that year he was back in the capital at Luoyang. The following year an edict reopened Hangu Pass and ordered the Grand Procession Palace, the six princes' hostels, and the tall-wheeled carriage stables rebuilt at Chang'an. The eastern capital's gates were repaired, bridges thrown across the Jing and Wei, and scattered lookout towers restored along the way. To the east he looked down on the Ba and Chan streams; to the west his eye fell on Kunming Pool; to the north he climbed Changping, traced Dragon Head Ridge, paced Weiyang Palace, scanned Pingle Lodge, and took in Jianzhang Hall.
5
是时山东翕然狐疑，意圣朝之西都，惧关门之反拒也。 客有为笃言：“彼□井之潢污，固不容夫吞舟； 且洛邑之渟瀯，曷足以居乎万乘哉？ 咸阳守国利器，不可久虚，以示奸萌。 ”笃未甚然其言也，故因为述大汉之崇，世据雍州之利，而今国家未暇之故，以喻客意。 曰：
Then the eastern provinces buzzed with unease, wondering whether the court meant to stay in the western capital and fearing that the inner passes might be closed in their faces. A visitor said to Du Du: "A roadside puddle beside a dried-up well cannot float a vessel meant to swallow the sea." As for Luoyang's shallow basin, how could it hold an empire of ten thousand chariots?" Xianyang is the keen blade of dynastic defense; leave it vacant too long and you only invite schemers to test the edge." Du Du was not wholly convinced; he therefore laid out the majesty of the Han, its long reliance on Yongzhou's strength, and the exigencies that now kept the court from a full return west—meant to answer his visitor's doubts. He said:
6
昔在强秦，爰初开畔，霸自岐、雍，国富人衍，卒以并兼，桀虐作乱。 天命有圣，托之大汉。 大汉开基，高祖有勋。 斩白蛇，屯黑云，聚五星于东井，提干将而呵暴秦。 蹈沧海，跨昆仑，奋彗光，扫项军，遂济人难，荡涤于泗、沂。 刘敬建策，初都长安。 太宗承流，守之以文。 躬履节俭，侧身行仁，食不二味，衣无异采。 赈人以农桑，率下以约已，曼丽之容不悦于目，郑、卫之声不过于耳，佞邪之臣不列于朝，巧伪之物不鬻于市，故能理升平而刑几措。 富衍于孝、景，功传于后嗣。
When all-powerful Qin first carved out its realm, hegemonic power rose from Qi and Yong; the land grew rich and populous until Qin swallowed every rival—then tyranny like Jie's provoked rebellion. Heaven reserved its mandate for a sage and laid it on the great Han. The Han founded its line, and the High Ancestor bore the chief merit. He slew the white serpent, camped beneath black clouds, watched the five planets cluster over the Eastern Well, raised his keen blade, and roared defiance at brutal Qin. He crossed the eastern sea, vaulted Kunlun, flashed like a comet's tail, shattered Xiang Yu's host, rescued the people from calamity, and rinsed the land clean along the Si and Yi. Liu Jing framed the strategy, and the capital was first fixed at Chang'an. Emperor Wen took up that legacy and preserved it through humane government. He lived plainly, bent every effort to kindness, took no second dish at meals, and wore no gaudy silks. He succored the people with tillage and silk, taught restraint by his own example, turned from sensual beauty, shut his ears to the licentious music of Zheng and Wei, kept sycophants from his hall, and banned shoddy wares from the market—so that an age of peace rose and the racks of punishment stood almost empty. Wealth swelled under Emperors Jing and his father; their achievement passed down the line.
7
是时，孝武因其余财府帑之蓄，始有钩深图远之意，探冒顿之罪，校平城之仇。 遂命票骑，勤任卫青，勇惟鹰扬，军如流星，深之匈奴，割裂王庭，席卷漠北，叩勒祁连，横分单于，屠裂百蛮。 烧罽帐，系阏氏，燔康居，灰珍奇，椎鸣镝，钉鹿蠡，驰坑岸，获昆弥，虏亻数侲，驱骡驴，御宛马，鞭□騠。 拓地万里，威震八荒。 肇置四郡，据守敦煌。 并域属国，一郡领方。 立侯隅北，建护西羌。 捶驱氐、僰，寥狼卭、莋。 东摩乌桓，蹂辚濊貊。 南羁钩町，水剑强越。 残夷文身，海波沫血。 郡县日南，漂概硃崖。 部尉东南，兼有黄支。 连缓耳，琐雕题，摧天督，牵象犀，椎蚌蛤，碎琉璃，甲玳瑁，戕觜觿。 于是同穴裘褐之域，共川鼻饮之国，莫不袒跣稽颡，失气虏伏。 非夫大汉之世盛，世借雍土之饶，得御外理内之术，孰能致功若斯！ 故创业于高祖，嗣传于孝惠，德隆于太宗，财衍于孝景，威盛于圣武，政行于宣、元，侈极于成、哀、祚缺于孝平。 传世十一，历载三百，德衰而复盈，道微而复章，皆莫能迁于雍州，而背于咸阳。 宫室寝庙，山陵相望，高显弘丽，可思可荣，羲、农已来，无兹著明。
Then Emperor Wu, drawing on accumulated treasure in the vaults, first conceived designs on distant horizons: he would reckon Modun's offenses and avenge the humiliation at Pingcheng. He summoned the Swift Cavalry, gave Wei Qing heavy responsibility, and sent armies shooting like meteors deep into the steppe: they shattered the royal court, swept the northern sands, stormed the Qilian range, cut the Chanyu in two, and butchered countless tribes. They torched the felt yurts, seized the khatun, put Kangju to the flame, reduced rarities to ash, smashed whistling arrows, pinned the Lu-li chieftain, charged the gully lines, seized the Kunmi ruler, drove off herds of pack animals, mounted blood-sweating horses from Ferghana, and whipped on the fleet coursers. They pushed the frontier ten thousand li; their majesty shook the eight outer regions. They founded the four western commanderies and planted a garrison at Dunhuang. They folded dependent states into the border and let a single commandery oversee the Western Regions. They planted marquises on the northern marches and created the office of Protector of the Western Qiang. They drove the Di and Bo peoples, and thinned the wolfish bands of Qiong and Zuo. To the east they brushed the Wuhuan and trampled the Hui and Mo tribes. To the south they haltered Gou Ding and crossed blades with defiant Yue. They broke the tattooed southerners until the sea foamed red. County government ran south to Rinan; rafts bore the army's awe to Zhuya. Southeastern outposts of the commandant reached even Huangzhi. They chained the long-eared envoys, collared the tattoo-browed chiefs, broke the "celestial duke," led off elephants and rhinos, smashed pearl shells and glassware, cracked tortoise carapaces and rhino-horn hilts. Then every land of fur-clad cave-dwellers and every tribe that drank from a common stream bared their feet, pressed brow to dust, and lay breathless in surrender. Had not the Han reached such zenith, generation on generation drawing on Yongzhou's fat soil and wielding arts of inner rule and outer command, who could have wrought feats like these?" The line began with the High Ancestor, passed through Emperor Hui, rose in virtue under Emperor Wen, swelled in wealth under Emperor Jing, blazed in might under Emperor Wu, ran smoothly under Emperors Xuan and Yuan, sank into excess under Cheng and Ai, and snapped under the boy Emperor Ping. Eleven reigns spanned three hundred years: virtue waned and waxed, the Way dimmed and brightened—yet none ever thought to abandon Yongzhou or turn away from Xianyang. Palaces and shrines, imperial tumuli shoulder to shoulder—lofty, bright, vast, glorious—since the age of Fu Xi and Shen Nong nothing has matched their splendor.
8
夫雍州本帝皇所以育业、霸王所以衍功，战士角难之场也。 《禹贡》所载，厥田惟上。 沃野千里，原隰弥望。 保殖五谷，桑麻条暢。 滨据南山，带以泾、渭，号曰陆海，蠢生万类。 楩楠檀柘，蔬果成实。 畎渎润淤，水泉灌溉，渐泽成川，粳稻陶遂。 厥土之膏，亩价一金。 田田相如，鐇䦆株林。 火耕流种，功浅得深。 既有蓄积，厄塞四临：四被陇、蜀，南通汉中，北据谷口，东阻嵚岩。 关函守嶢，山东道穷； 置列汧、陇，壅偃西戎； 拒守褒斜，岭南不通； 杜口绝津，朔方无从。 鸿、渭之流，径入于河； 大船万艘，转漕相过； 东综沧海，西纲流纱； 朔南暨声，诸夏是和。 城池百尺，厄塞要害。 关梁之险，多所衿带。 一卒举礧，千夫沉滞； 一人奋戟，三军沮败。 地势便利，介胄剽悍，可与守近，利以攻远。 士卒易保，人不肉袒。 肇十有二，是为赡腴。 用霸则兼并，先据则功殊； 修文则财衍，行武则士要； 为政则化上，篡逆则难诛； 进攻则百克，退守则有余：斯固帝王之渊囿，而守国之利器也。
Yongzhou is where emperors reared dynastic foundations and hegemons piled up achievement; it is the very arena where soldiers proved their mettle. The "Tribute of Yu" ranks its soil the finest grade. A thousand li of fertile plain roll away; marsh and bottomland stretch to the horizon. The five grains thrive in abundance; mulberry and hemp grow in luxuriant rows. It hugs the southern ranges and is belted by the Jing and Wei—what men call the "inland sea," teeming with every kind of creature. Piannan, sandalwood, and zhe timber flourish; orchards and kitchen gardens bear heavy fruit. Irrigation channels leach the silt, springs water the fields, marshes widen into streams, and both sticky and ordinary rice flourish without strain. The soil is so rich that a single mu once fetched a full ounce of gold. Plot matches plot in endless green; hoes and mattocks flash among the groves. Slash-and-burn and broadcast sowing yield ample harvests for modest toil. With granaries full, four sides are sealed by natural ramparts: Long and Shu fold round it, Hanzhong opens to the south, Gukou bars the north, and precipitous cliffs choke the east. Hangu's gate commands the heights; east of the mountains the road simply stops. Lines of defense on Qian and Long stifle the western Rong. Hold the Bao and Xie defiles and nothing crosses the southern ranges. Seal the passes and break the ferries, and the northern steppe has no way in. The Hong and Wei pour straight into the Yellow River. Ten thousand great barges ply the channels, grain convoys passing hull to hull. To the east they rope in the dark sea; to the west they net the drifting sands. From the northern marches to the southern coasts a single harmony reigns; the Hua heartland rests at peace. City walls rise a hundred feet; every defile marks a choke point. Bridges and mountain gates pinch the roads like a belt drawn tight. One man working a traction-trebuchet can bog a thousand attackers in the mud. A single halberdier on the wall can break an army's nerve. The ground favors the defender and the men wear fierce mail: you can hold the near at ease and strike the far with advantage. Troops find cover readily; no warrior need fight stripped to the skin. Twelve such districts form what men call the empire's fattest pasture. Use hegemonic force and you annex rivals; seize the ground first and your achievement is unmatched. Pursue civil arts and wealth multiplies; train warriors and bold knights flock to you. Rule uprightly and the people look upward in trust; would-be usurpers find no opening. Attack and you win every clash; defend and your stores overflow—such is the emperor's walled park and the keen blade of dynastic defense.
9
逮及亡新，时汉之衰，偷忍渊囿，篡器慢违，徒以势便，莫能卒危。 假之十八，诛自京师。 天畀更始，不能引维。 慢藏招寇，复致赤眉。 海内云扰，诸夏灭微。 群龙并战，未知是非。 于时圣帝，赫然申威，荷天人之符，兼不世之姿。 受命于皇上，获助于灵祇。 立号高邑，搴旗四麾。 首策之臣，运筹出奇； 虓怒之旅； 如虎如螭。 师之攸向，无不靡披。 盖夫燔鱼剸蛇，莫之方斯。 大呼山东，响动流沙。 要龙渊，首镆铘，命腾太白，亲发狼、弧。 南禽公孙，北背强胡，西平陇、冀，东据洛都。 乃廓平帝宇，济蒸人于涂炭，成兆庶之□，遂兴复乎大汉。
Even when Xin usurped the throne at Han's ebb, Wang Mang slunk within those same royal preserves, seized the regalia, and grew arrogant—yet for all his positional advantage he could not in the end avert ruin." Wang Mang's eighteen-year reign collapsed when he was put to death in the capital. Heaven raised the Gengshi emperor, but he could not keep the realm in hand. Loose guarding of the treasury drew marauders, and the Red Eyebrows rose in turn. The empire boiled like storm clouds; the heartland of the Xia almost perished. Rival warlords clawed like dragons in a single pool; no one knew which cause was just. Then our sage sovereign blazed forth in wrath, bore Heaven's mandate and men's hopes, and combined a presence unmatched in his age. High Heaven entrusted him with rule, and the spirits of land and altar rallied to his side. He proclaimed his reign at Gaoyi and unfurled the royal ensigns to the four quarters. The counselors who first laid the plan plotted behind the screen and struck where no foe expected. Armies snarled like tigers unleashed. They fought like tigers and hornless dragons—no force could stand before them. Every column he aimed swept all before it like a scythe. They say Qin Shi burned the sacred fish and carved the divine serpent—no tale of old outshone this feat. His shout rolled from Shandong to the western wastes until the sands trembled. He belted the sword Longyuan, brandished Mo Ye, read the omen of Venus at dawn, and himself bent the Wolf- and Archer-star bows. To the south he caged Gongsun Shu, to the north he threw back the Xiongnu, to the west he stilled Long and Ji, to the east he planted the standard at Luoyang. He swept the realm clean, snatched the people from fire and mire, gave the myriad households a footing again, and raised the great Han from its ashes.
10
今天下新定，矢石之勤始瘳，而主上方以边垂为忧，忿葭萌之不柔，未遑于论都而遗思雍州也。 方躬劳圣思，以率海内，厉抚名将，略地疆外，信威于征伐，展武乎荒裔。 若夫文身鼻饮缓耳之主，椎结左衽□鍝之君，东南殊俗不羁之国，西北绝域难制之邻，靡不重译纳贡，请为籓臣。 上犹谦让，而不伐勤。 意以为获无用之虏，不如安有益之民； 略荒裔之地，不如保殖五谷之渊； 远救于已亡，不若近而存存也。 今国家躬修道德，吐惠含仁，湛恩沾洽，时风显宣。 徒垂意于持平守实，务在爱育元元，苟有便于王政者，圣主纳焉。 何则？ 物罔挹而不损，道无隆而不移，阳盛则运，阴满则亏，故存不忘亡，安不讳危，虽有仁义，犹设城池也。
The empire is only just pacified and the wounds of war still closing, yet His Majesty's mind runs first to the frontiers, to stubborn Jiameng in the west; he has no spare moment to argue capitals or to forget the old seat in Yongzhou. He bends his own person to guide the realm, heartens his best commanders, pushes the frontier outward, lets campaign after campaign speak his authority, and shows the flag in the farthest barbarian lands. Whether tattooed nose-drinking chiefs with weighted earlobes, or topknotted left-lapped lords with bronze rings at the belt—southern tribes beyond our law, northwestern peoples beyond easy reach—all sent envoys through double translation, laden with gifts, suing to be Han vassals. Yet the emperor still answered with modest demurral and refused to trumpet his own toil. He judged that dragging home useless prisoners was poorer policy than cherishing citizens who could enrich the realm. Snatching barren steppe was less wise than tending the deep breadbasket of the central plain. To chase ghosts beyond the frontier was folly when the living heartland still needed care. Today the dynasty walks the path of virtue, speaks in mercy and justice, pours its grace into every corner, and lets honest policy ride on an open wind. It cares chiefly for even-handed rule and plain dealing, for feeding and fathering the common folk; whatever truly serves good government, the sage throne accepts. Why so? Nothing taken from the common stock fails to diminish the whole; no way climbs forever without turning; yang at its height must roll onward, yin at the full must ebb—hence the wise remember ruin in the hour of success and speak of peril in times of calm; even under humane rule, walls and gates remain needful.
11
客以利器不可久虚，而国家亦不忘乎西都，何必去洛邑之渟瀯与？
Your visitor says Xianyang must not lie open to intrigue—true enough—yet the court has not forgotten the western capital either; need that mean forsaking Luoyang's deeper basin?
12
笃后仕郡文学掾。 以目疾，二十余年不窥京师。
Du Du later held the post of literary clerk in his commandery. Eye trouble kept him from the capital for over twenty years.
13
笃之外高祖破羌将军辛武贤，以武略称。 笃常叹曰：“杜氏文明善政，而笃不任为吏； 辛氏秉义经武，而笃又怯于事。 外内五世，至笃衰矣！ ”
On his mother's side he counted the "Qiang-Smashing" general Xin Wuxian, celebrated for military stratagem. Du Du would sigh: "The Dus were lettered men who governed well, yet I am no good as an administrator. The Xins bore arms in a just cause, yet I am too timid for such work. Five generations of worth on either side—and it ends in me, a cipher! "
14
女弟适扶风马氏。 建初三年，车骑将军马防击西羌，请笃为从事中郎，战没于射姑山。
A younger sister wedded into the prominent Ma family of Fufeng. In Jianchu 3 the chariot-and-cavalry general Ma Fang campaigned against the western Qiang and took Du Du on staff as an attendant clerk; Du fell fighting at Mount Shegu.

When Emperor Guangwu took the throne he commended Xia's steadfast courage, summoned him as a gentleman of the interior, and twice advanced him to commandant of Taishan. Huang Xiang, courtesy name Wenqiang, was a native of Anlu in Jiangxia commandery. At nine he lost his mother; grief wasted him until he barely lived through the mourning period, and the villagers hailed him as a paragon of filial devotion. At twelve the grand administrator Liu Hu heard his fame and summoned him, naming him a filial attendant at his gate; the official doted on him and treated him with honor. The Huang household was poor—no maids within—so he shouldered every hardship himself and nursed his father with utter devotion. He went on to master the canon, probed the arts of the Way, and wrote well; the capital dubbed him "the matchless Huang boy of Jiangxia."

His first appointment was gentleman of the interior; in Yuanhe 1 Emperor Zhang sent Huang Xiang to the Eastern Pavilion to read texts he had never opened before. After a leave Huang Xiang returned to court just as the Prince of Qiansheng came of age; the emperor received the princes at the Prince of Zhongshan residence, called Huang Xiang forward below the dais, and said to his sons, "Here is the 'matchless Huang boy of Jiangxia' they all talk about." Every courtier in earshot sat up straighter and looked at him with new respect. He was later summoned to Anfu Hall to debate policy, promoted to gentleman of the masters of writing, where he spoke frankly on what worked and what failed—each time winning richer gifts. Huang Xiang often slept alone on the office terrace and never left the inner gate day or night; the emperor heard of it and approved.

When the court named Huang Xiang grand warden of Dong commandery, he submitted a memorial begging off: "I am a humble nobody from the southlands, a shallow student; my scholarship and conduct offer nothing worth the archive, having lived under an age of peace and ridden my ancestors' lingering fortune; still in my twenties I was singled out for office and climbed every rung until I stood at the head of the secretariat. I have never earned the slightest praise; I would gladly give my life for the throne, yet I never dreamed I would be singled out for such favor. Now I am given what exceeds all hope—a manifest appointment to a neighboring commandery, a thousand-li magistracy. I have heard that offices fit to talent leave no duty undone; and that rewards scaled to labor give each man, wise or dull, what he deserves. Moreover the pivot of state secrets is the weightiest trust of all—not a burden I should carry long. The edict struck me with panic; I do not know how to answer. I am still in the prime of life—fit to run errands for the court, not to rule a commandery. From then on Huang Xiang turned the central gears of government, won deep trust, and threw himself into every file as if the realm were his own household.

Huang Xiang drafted a separate finding that spared a great host of them. Whenever a province sent up an ambiguous case, he always looked for the mildest statute, cherished every life, and leaned toward mercy. He knew frontier business inside out and balanced civil and military policy so that each decision fit the facts. The emperor saw his tireless care and heaped favor on him again and again. When he fell ill the court sent physicians and drugs. In office he lifted many men to promotion; some critics mocked his run of exceptional luck.

In Yanping 1 he became grand warden of Wei commandery. The commandery had long kept inner and outer park lands share-cropped with commoners, yielding several thousand hu a year. Huang Xiang said: "The Field Ordinance says merchants do not till; the Royal Regulations say officials do not plough; men who live on ice-house salaries should not squeeze the commoners for profit." He therefore turned every plot over to the people and taxed only the harvest. A flood year brought famine; he split his salary and gifts to feed the destitute, and the wealthy families followed suit with "charity grain," refilling the public granaries on loan until the starving were saved. He later lost office over the flood case; a few months later he died at home.

Under Emperor He, Jia Kui praised him as a match for Sima Xiangru and Yang Xiong; the court called him to the Eastern Pavilion, ordered a rhapsody, and named him orchid terrace clerk. He rose by degrees to grandee remonstrant under Emperor An and was charged with Liu Zhen and others to draft the Han Records. Each aided her lord with ritual, steadied him with kindness, advanced worthies, and kept herself within the bounds of duty.
53
爰暨未叶，渐已穨亏。 贯鱼不叙，九御差池。 晋国之难，祸起于丽。 惟家之索。 牝鸡之晨。 专权擅爱，显已蔽人。 陵长间旧，圮剥至亲。 并后匹嫡，淫女毙陈。 匪贤是上，番为司徒。 荷爵负乘，采食名都。 诗人是刺，德用无怃。 暴辛惑妇，拒谏自孤。 蝠蛇其心，纵毒不辜。 诸父是杀，孕子是刳。 天怒地忿，人谋鬼图。 甲子昧爽，身首分离。 初为天子，后为人螭。
But by the withered twig of each line decay had already set in. The harem lost its proper order; the nine ranks of consorts fell into chaos. Jin's catastrophe began with Consort Li. As the Classic warns, "only the family's cord"—the house unravels. The hen that crows at dawn—women seizing the hour. They monopolized power and private favor, flaunted themselves, and blocked every rival. They browbeat elders, sowed discord with old allies, and tore their own kin bare. When two queens rivaled the heir's station and a wanton favorite ruled the harem, the state of Chen fell to ruin. Mediocrities rose to power; men like Fan Huo climbed to steward the realm. They loaded themselves with titles their shoulders could not bear and fed from the richest commanderies. The Odes skewered such houses, for virtue could find no resting place among them. King Zhou of Shang drowned in a consort's flattery, spurned every warning, and cut himself off from the world. His heart was viper-cold; he poured out cruelty and killed without cause. He butchered his own uncles and ripped the bellies of pregnant women. Heaven and earth turned against him; living men and vengeful shades alike conspired for his fall. At dawn on the day jiazi his head rolled from his shoulders. He had worn the crown of heaven; in the end he was less than a slave—mockery made a monster of a king.
54
非但耽色，母后尤然。 不相率以礼，而竞奖以权。 先笑后号，卒以辱残。 国家泯绝，宗庙烧燔。 末嬉丧夏，褒姒毙周，妲已亡殷，赵灵沙丘。 戚姬人豕，吕宗以败。 陈后作巫，卒死于外。 霍欲鸩子，身乃罹废。
The vice was not lust alone; the palace matrons outdid one another in it. Instead of guiding the court by rite, they fought for favor with naked force. They laughed while fortune lasted, then howled when the blade fell—every house ended in disgrace and the butcher's block. Dynastic fires went out and imperial shrines blazed to ash. Moxi sank the Xia, Baosi broke the Zhou, Daji poisoned the Shang, and King Ling of Zhao breathed his last on the dunes of Shaqiu. Consort Qi became Empress Lü's "human swine," and the Lü clan paid for it with extinction. Empress Chen's sorcery drove her from the palace to die in exile. Huo Chengsi plotted to poison the crown prince—and brought ruin on himself.
55
故曰：无谓我贵，天将尔摧； 无恃常好，色有歇微； 无怙常幸，爱有陵迟； 无曰我能，天人尔违。 患生不德，福有慎机。 日不常中，月盈有亏。 履道者固，杖势者危。 微臣司戚，敢告在斯。
Hence the warning: "Never boast of your height—Heaven stoops to strike the proud." "Never trust a face that will not fade—beauty always gutters out." "Never lean on a sovereign's whim—royal love slackens like a loosened cord." "Never cry 'I alone am wise'—Heaven and the people will abandon you." Disaster sprouts where virtue is thin; good fortune hinges on a hair. The sun does not pause at noon; the full moon must shrink again. Walk the Way and you stand on rock; lean on brute force and you tread ice. I, a minor officer charged with kin relations, lay this before you.
56
琦以言不从，失意，复作《白鹄赋》以为风。 梁冀见之，呼琦问曰：“百官外内，各有司存，天下云云，岂独吾人之尤，君何激刺之过乎！ ”琦对曰：“昔管仲相齐，乐闻机谏之言； 萧何佐汉，乃设书过之吏。 今将军累世台辅，任齐伊、公，而德政未闻，黎元涂炭。 不能结纳贞良，以救祸败，反复欲钳塞士口，杜蔽主听，将使玄黄改色，马鹿易形乎？ ”冀无以对，因遣琦归。
When Liang Ji ignored him, Cui Qi sulked and wrote the White Crane Rhapsody as a veiled rebuke. Liang Ji read it, summoned Cui Qi, and snapped: "Every bureau has its brief; the empire buzzes with gossip—do you think we alone are at fault? What gives you the right to slash so deep?" Cui Qi answered: "When Guan Zhong served Duke Huan he welcomed even whispered correction; Xiao He aided the Han founder and posted clerks whose sole duty was to note his missteps. Your house has propped the throne for generations; your charge rivals Yi Yin and the Duke of Zhou—yet the people hear no mercy from your rule, only fire and mire. You will not bind honest men to you to stave off ruin; you would rather gag every scholar and deafen the throne—until black becomes yellow and a stag is sold as a horse? Liang Ji had no reply and dismissed him.
57
后除为临济长，不敢之职，解印绶去。 冀遂令刺客阴求杀之。 客见琦耕于陌上，怀书一卷，息辄偃而咏之。 客哀其志，以实告琦，曰：“将军令吾要子，今见君贤者，情怀忍忍。 可亟自逃，吾亦于此亡矣。 ”琦得脱走，冀后竟捕杀之。
He was later named magistrate of Linji but refused the post, laid down his seal, and walked away. Liang Ji then sent killers to hunt him down. One assassin found him furrowing a lane with a satchel of texts; at each pause he lay in the dust and recited aloud. The man pitied such single-mindedness and confessed: "The general sent me to kill you; seeing a scholar of your stamp, I cannot strike. Run now—I shall die here in your place. Cui Qi escaped, but Liang Ji's agents caught him in the end and put him to death.
58
所著赋、颂、铭、诔、箴、吊、论、《九咨》、《七言》，凡十五篇。
His rhapsodies, hymns, inscriptions, laments, admonitions, elegies, essays, Nine Consultations, and Seven Lines number fifteen titles.
59
边韶字孝先，陈留浚仪人也。 以文章知名，教授数百人。 韶口辩，曾昼日假卧，弟子私嘲之曰：“边孝先，腹便便。 懒读书，但欲眠。 ”韶潜闻之，应时对曰：“边为姓、考为字。 腹便便，《五经》笥。 但欲眠，思经事。 寐与周公通梦，静与孔子同意。 师而可嘲，出何典记？ ”嘲者大惭。 韶之才捷皆此类也。
Bian Shao, courtesy name Xiaoxian, came from Junyi in Chenliu commandery. He won fame as a writer and kept several hundred pupils. Bian Shao was quick of tongue; one noon he pretended to nap, and his students whispered, "Bian Xiaoxian—what a round belly. Too lazy to read—only good for snoring. Bian Shao heard them and shot back: "Bian is my clan, Xian my style. This belly is the coffer of the Five Classics. This nap is meditation on the canon. Asleep I trade words with the Duke of Zhou; awake my mind walks with Confucius. Tell me which classic licenses students to mock their master? The students crept away in shame. Bian Shao's wit was always that sharp.
60
桓帝时，为临颍侯相，征拜太中大夫，著作东观。 再迁北地太守，入拜尚书令。 后为陈相，卒官。 著诗、颂、碑、铭、书、策，凡十五篇。
Under Emperor Huan he served the Marquis of Linying as minister, was called to court as grandee of the palace, and wrote at the Eastern Pavilion. He rose twice to grand warden of Beidi, then entered the capital as overseer of the masters of writing. He ended as minister of Chen and died in harness. His poems, hymns, inscriptions, laments, letters, and memorials fill fifteen pieces.

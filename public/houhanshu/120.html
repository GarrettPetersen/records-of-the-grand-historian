The Geography Treatise in the Book of Han exhaustively traces every commandery and county in the empire, notes remarkable mountains and rivers, and explains how regional customs arose. This chapter records only post-restoration changes among commanderies and counties, plus battle-related placenames where the Spring and Autumn and the three standard histories agree—hence the title Commanderies and States. Any county listed in the earlier Geography but omitted here was folded into another unit or struck from the rolls under Emperor Guangwu. Entries that had no Western Han predecessor were added in subsequent reigns. The county named first in each list is the commandery seat.

帝王世記曰：「自天地設闢，未有經界之制。三皇尚矣。諸子稱神農之王天下也，地東西九十萬里，南北八十五萬里。及黃帝受命，始作舟車，以濟不通。乃推分星次，以定律度。自斗十一度至婺女七度，一名須女，曰星紀之次，於辰在丑，謂之赤奮若，於律為黃鍾，斗建在子，今吳、越分野。自婺女八度至危十六度，曰玄枵之次，一名天黿，於辰在子，謂之困敦，於律為大呂，斗建在丑，今齊分野。自危十七度至奎四度，曰豕韋之次，一名娵訾，於辰在亥，謂之大淵獻，於律為太蔟，斗建在寅，今衛分野。自奎五度至胃六度，曰降婁之次，於辰在戌，謂之閹茂，於律為夾鍾，斗建在卯，今魯分野。自胃七度至畢十一度，曰大梁之次，於辰在酉，謂之作噩，於律為姑洗，斗建在辰，今趙分野。自畢十二度至東井十五度，曰實沈之次，於辰在申，謂之涒灘，於律為中呂，斗建在巳，今晉、魏分野。自井十六度至柳八度，曰鶉首之次，於辰在未，謂之協洽，於律為蕤賓，斗建在午，今秦分野。自柳九度至張十七度，曰鶉火之次，於辰在午，謂之敦牂，一名大律，於律為林鍾，斗建在未，今周分野。自張十八度至軫十一度，曰鶉尾之次，於辰在巳，謂之大荒落，於律為夷則，斗建在申，今楚分野。自軫十二度至氐四度，曰壽星之次，於辰在辰，謂之執徐，於律為南呂，斗建在酉，今韓分野。自氐五度至尾九度，曰大火之次，於辰在卯，謂之單閼，於律為無射，斗建在戌，今宋分野。自尾十度至斗十度百三十五分而終，曰析木之次，於辰在寅，謂之攝提格，於律為應鍾，斗建在亥，今燕分野。凡天有十二次，日月之所躔也；地有十二分，王侯之所國也。故四方方七宿，四七二十八宿，合百八十二星。東方蒼龍三十二星，七十五度；北方玄武三十五星，九十八度四分度之一；西方白虎五十一星，八十度；南方朱雀六十四星，百一十二度。周天三百六十五度四分度之一。一度二千九百三十二里，分為十二次，一次三十度三十二分度之十四，各以附其七宿閒。距周天積百七萬九百一十三里，徑三十五萬六千九百七十一里。陽道左行，故太歲右轉，凡中外官常明者百二十四，可名者三百二十，合二千五百星。微星之數，凡萬一千五百二十星，萬物所受，咸系命焉。此黃帝創制之大略也。而佗說稱日月所照三十五萬里。考諸子所載，神農之地，過日月之表，近為虛誕。及少昊氏之衰，九黎亂德，其制無聞矣。洎顓頊之所建，帝嚳受定，則孔子稱其地北至幽陵，南暨交阯，西蹈流沙，東極蟠木，日月所照，莫不底焉，是以建萬國而制九州。至堯遭洪水，分為十二州，今虞書是也。及禹平水土，還為九州，今禹貢是也。是以其時九州之地，凡二千四百三十萬八千二十四頃，定墾者九百三十萬六千二十四頃，不墾者千五百萬二千頃，民口千三百五十五萬三千九百二十三人。至于塗山之會，諸侯承唐虞之盛，執玉帛亦有萬國。是以山海經稱禹使大章步自東極，至于西垂，二億三萬三千五百里七十一步。又使豎亥步自南極，盡於北垂，二億三萬三千五百里七十五步。四海之內，則東西二萬八千里，南北二萬六千里，出水者八千里，受水者八千里，經名山五千三百五十，六萬四千五十六里。出銅之山四百六十七，出鐵之山三千六百九。以供財用，儉則有餘，奢則不足。以男女耕織，不奪其時，故公家有三十年之積，私家有九年之儲。及夏之衰，棄稷弗務，有窮之亂，少康中興，乃復禹跡。孔甲之至桀行暴，諸侯相兼，逮湯受命，其能存者三千餘國，方於塗山，十損其七。民離毒政，將亦如之。殷因於夏，六百餘載，其閒損益，書策不存，無以考之。又遭紂亂，至周剋商，制五等之封，凡千七百七十三國，又減湯時千三百矣。民眾之損，將亦如之。及周公相成王，致治刑錯，民口千三百七十一萬四千九百二十三人，多禹十六萬一千人，周之極盛也。其後七十餘歲，天下無事，民彌以息。及昭王南征不反，穆王失荒，加以幽、厲之亂，平王東遷，三十餘載，至齊桓公二年，周莊王之十三年，五千里內，非天王九儐之御，自世子公侯以下至於庶民，凡千一百八十四萬七千人，除有土老疾，定受田者九百萬四千人。其後諸侯相并，當春秋時，尚有千二百國。二百四十二年之中，殺君三十六，亡國五十二，諸侯奔走不得保社稷者，不可勝數。至于戰國，存者十餘。於是從橫短長之說，相奪於時，殘民詐力之兵，動以萬計。故崤有匹馬之禍，宋有易子之急，晉陽之圍，縣釜而炊，長平之戰，血流漂鹵。周之列國，唯有燕、衛、秦、楚而已。齊及三晉，皆以篡亂，南面稱王。衛雖得存，不絕若綫。然考蘇、張之說，計秦及山東六國，戎卒尚存五百餘萬，推民口數，尚當千餘萬。及秦兼諸侯，置三十六郡，其所殺傷，三分居二；猶以餘力，行參夷之刑，收太半之賦，北築長城四十餘萬，南戍五嶺五十餘萬，阿房、驪山七十餘萬，十餘年閒，百姓死沒，相踵于路。陳、項又肆其餘烈，故新安之坑，二十餘萬，彭城之戰，睢水不流。至漢祖定天下，民之死傷，亦數百萬。是以平城之卒，不過三十萬，方之六國，五損其二。自孝惠至文、景，與民休息，六十餘歲，民眾大增，是以太倉有不食之粟，都內有朽貫之錢。武帝乘其資畜，軍征三十餘歲，地廣萬里，天下之眾亦減半矣。及霍光秉政，乃務省役，至于孝平，六世相承，雖時征行，不足大害，民戶又息。元始二年，郡、國百三，縣、邑千五百八十七，地東西九千三百二里，南北萬三千三百六十八里，定墾田八百二十七萬五百三十六頃，民戶千三百二十三萬三千六百一十二，口五千九百一十九萬四千九百七十八人，多周成王四千五百四十八萬五十五人，漢之極盛也。及王莽篡位，續以更始、赤眉之亂，至光武中興，百姓虛耗，十有二存。中元二年，民戶四百二十七萬千六百三十四，口二千一百萬七千八百二十人。永平、建初之際，天下無事，務在養民，迄于孝和，民戶滋殖。及孝安永初、元初之閒，兵飢之苦，民人復損。至于孝桓，頗增於前。永壽二年，戶千六百七萬九百六，口五千六萬六千八百五十六人，墾田亦多，單師屢征。及靈帝遭黃巾，獻帝即位而董卓興亂，大焚宮廟，劫御西遷，京師蕭條，豪桀並爭，郭汜、李傕之屬，殘害又甚，是以興平、建安之際，海內凶荒，天子奔流，白骨盈野，故陝津之難，以箕撮指，安邑之東，后裳不完，遂有寇戎，雄雌未定，割剝庶民，三十餘年。及魏武皇帝剋平天下，文帝受禪，人眾之損，萬有一存。景元四年，與蜀通計民戶九十四萬三千四百二十三，口五百三十七萬二千八百九十一人。又案正始五年，揚威將軍朱照日所上吳之所領兵戶凡十三萬二千，推其民數，不能多蜀矣。昔漢永和五年，南陽戶五十餘萬，汝南戶四十餘萬，方之於今，三帝鼎足，不踰二郡，加有食祿復除之民，凶年飢疾之難，見可供役，裁若一郡。以一郡之人，供三帝之用，斯亦勤矣。自禹至今二千餘載，六代損益，備於茲焉。」臣昭案：謐記云春秋時有千二百國，未知所出。班固云周之始，爵五而土三，蓋千八百國。轉相吞滅，數百年閒，列國耗盡，至春秋時，尚有數十。
The Diwang shiji says, “From the opening of heaven and earth there was not yet any system of territorial boundaries. The Three Sovereigns were too remote. The various masters say that when Shennong ruled all under heaven, the land measured 900,000 li east to west and 850,000 li north to south. When the Yellow Emperor received the mandate, he first made boats and carts to cross what had not been passable. He then calculated the stellar stations and lunar lodges in order to fix the pitch-pipes and measures. From 11 degrees of Dipper to 7 degrees of Weaving Maid, also called Maid, was the station of Xingji; among the earthly branches it was at chou, called Chifenruo; in the pitch-pipes it was Huangzhong; when the Dipper handle pointed to zi, this was today the field-allocation of Wu and Yue. From 8 degrees of Weaving Maid to 16 degrees of Rooftop was Xuanxiao, also called Tianyuan; at zi, called Kundun; in the pitch-pipes Dalu; with the Dipper handle at chou; today the field-allocation of Qi. From 17 degrees of Rooftop to 4 degrees of Legs was Shiwei, also called Zouzi; at hai, called Dayuanxian; in the pitch-pipes Taicu; with the Dipper handle at yin; today the field-allocation of Wei. From 5 degrees of Legs to 6 degrees of Stomach was Jianglou; at xu, called Yanmao; in the pitch-pipes Jiazhong; with the Dipper handle at mao; today the field-allocation of Lu. From 7 degrees of Stomach to 11 degrees of Net was Daliang; at you, called Zuo’e; in the pitch-pipes Guxian; with the Dipper handle at chen; today the field-allocation of Zhao. From 12 degrees of Net to 15 degrees of Eastern Well was Shishen; at shen, called Tuntan; in the pitch-pipes Zhonglü; with the Dipper handle at si; today the field-allocation of Jin and Wei. From 16 degrees of Well to 8 degrees of Willow was Chunshou; at wei, called Xiaqia; in the pitch-pipes Ruibin; with the Dipper handle at wu; today the field-allocation of Qin. From 9 degrees of Willow to 17 degrees of Extended Net was Chunhuo; at wu, called Dunzang, also called Dalü; in the pitch-pipes Linzhong; with the Dipper handle at wei; today the field-allocation of Zhou. From 18 degrees of Extended Net to 11 degrees of Chariot was Chunwei; at si, called Dahuangluo; in the pitch-pipes Yize; with the Dipper handle at shen; today the field-allocation of Chu. From 12 degrees of Chariot to 4 degrees of Root was Shouxing; at chen, called Zhixu; in the pitch-pipes Nanlü; with the Dipper handle at you; today the field-allocation of Han. From 5 degrees of Root to 9 degrees of Tail was Dahuo; at mao, called Dan’e; in the pitch-pipes Wuyi; with the Dipper handle at xu; today the field-allocation of Song. From 10 degrees of Tail through 10 degrees and 135 parts of Dipper was Ximu; at yin, called Shetige; in the pitch-pipes Yingzhong; with the Dipper handle at hai; today the field-allocation of Yan. In all, heaven has twelve stations, the paths trodden by sun and moon; earth has twelve divisions, the states of kings and lords. Thus each of the four quarters has seven lodges; four times seven makes twenty-eight lodges, comprising 182 stars. The Azure Dragon of the east has 32 stars and 75 degrees; the Dark Warrior of the north has 35 stars and 98 and one-quarter degrees; the White Tiger of the west has 51 stars and 80 degrees; the Vermilion Bird of the south has 64 stars and 112 degrees. The circuit of heaven is 365 and one-quarter degrees. One degree is 2,932 li, divided into twelve stations; one station is 30 degrees and 14/32 of a degree, each attached between its seven lodges. The circumference distant around heaven totals 1,070,913 li, and the diameter is 356,971 li. The solar path moves left, so Grand Year turns right. In all, the constantly bright inner and outer offices are 124; those that can be named are 320; together they make 2,500 stars. As for the number of small stars, there are altogether 11,520 stars, and the destinies received by the myriad things are all bound to them. This is the broad outline of the Yellow Emperor’s founding system. But another account says what the sun and moon illuminate is 350,000 li. Examining what the masters record, Shennong’s land extended beyond the surface of the sun and moon, which is close to absurdity. When the line of Shaohao declined, the Jiuli threw virtue into disorder, and nothing is heard of its system. When it came to what Zhuanxu established and Emperor Ku received and fixed, Confucius said its land reached Youling in the north, Jiaozhi in the south, the Flowing Sands in the west, and Panmu in the east; wherever the sun and moon shone, none did not reach it. Therefore he established the myriad states and regulated the Nine Provinces. When Yao encountered the flood, he divided them into twelve provinces; this is the present Book of Yu. When Yu calmed the waters and lands, they returned to nine provinces; this is the present Yu Tribute. Thus in his time the land of the Nine Provinces amounted to 24,308,024 qing; fixed cultivated land was 9,306,024 qing, uncultivated land was 15,002,000 qing, and the population was 13,553,923 persons. At the assembly at Tushan, the lords inherited the prosperity of Tang and Yu, and those who held jade and silk numbered also ten thousand states. Therefore the Classic of Mountains and Seas says Yu had Dazhang pace from the eastern limit to the western border, 200,033,571 paces, and also had Shuhai pace from the southern limit to the northern border, 200,033,575 paces. Within the four seas, east to west was 28,000 li, north to south 26,000 li; the lands from which waters emerged were 8,000 li, and those that received waters were 8,000 li. The classic names 5,350 famous mountains over 64,056 li. There were 467 mountains producing copper and 3,609 producing iron. To supply resources, with frugality there was surplus, and with extravagance there was insufficiency. Because men and women plowed and wove without having their seasons taken from them, public houses had stores for thirty years, and private households had reserves for nine years. When Xia declined, it abandoned grain and did not attend to it. In the disorder of Youqiong and the restoration under Shaokang, Yu’s traces were restored. From Kong Jia to Jie, conduct was violent, and the lords annexed one another. By the time Tang received the mandate, more than three thousand states could still survive; compared with Tushan, seven in ten had been lost. The people’s departure from poisonous rule was probably like this too. Yin followed Xia for more than six hundred years; records of losses and gains in that interval do not survive, so they cannot be examined. It also met the disorder of Zhou. When Zhou overcame Shang and created the five grades of enfeoffment, there were 1,773 states, again 1,300 fewer than in Tang’s time. The loss in population was probably like this too. When the Duke of Zhou assisted King Cheng, he brought about such order that punishments were set aside; the population was 13,714,923, 161,000 more than under Yu, the height of Zhou prosperity. For more than seventy years afterward, all under heaven had no affairs, and the people grew ever more numerous. When King Zhao went south and did not return, King Mu lost himself in distant wandering, and the disorders of You and Li were added, King Ping moved east. More than thirty years later, in the second year of Duke Huan of Qi, the thirteenth year of King Zhuang of Zhou, within five thousand li, excluding the heavenly king’s nine grades of attendants, from heirs apparent, dukes, and marquises down to the common people, there were altogether 11,847,000 people; subtracting those with land, the aged, and the infirm, those fixed to receive fields numbered 9,004,000. After that, the lords annexed one another; in the Spring and Autumn period there were still 1,200 states. In 242 years, thirty-six rulers were killed and fifty-two states perished, and the lords who fled about unable to preserve their altars of soil and grain were beyond counting. By the Warring States, those that survived numbered a little more than ten. Thereupon the doctrines of alliance and opposition, long and short strategies, competed in the age, and armies that harmed the people and relied on deceitful force moved in tens of thousands. Thus Xiao had the disaster of the single horse; Song had the extremity of exchanging children; in the siege of Jinyang, cauldrons were hung up for cooking; at the battle of Changping, blood flowed enough to float shields. Of Zhou’s arrayed states, only Yan, Wei, Qin, and Chu remained. Qi and the Three Jin all faced south as kings through usurpation and disorder. Though Wei survived, it was like a thread not yet cut. Yet examining the sayings of Su and Zhang, counting Qin and the six states east of the mountains, there were still more than five million soldiers, and reckoning the population there still should have been more than ten million people. When Qin annexed the lords and established thirty-six commanderies, those killed and wounded made up two parts in three. Still, with its remaining strength, it carried out the punishment of exterminating three kin-groups, collected taxes of more than half, used more than 400,000 men to build the Great Wall in the north, more than 500,000 to garrison the Five Ranges in the south, and more than 700,000 at Epang and Mount Li. In little more than ten years, commoners died one after another along the roads. Chen Sheng and Xiang Yu further unleashed the remaining violence; therefore the pit at Xin’an took more than 200,000, and in the battle of Pengcheng the Sui River would not flow. By the time the Han founder settled all under heaven, deaths and injuries among the people also numbered several million. Therefore the soldiers at Pingcheng were no more than 300,000; compared with the six states, two-fifths had been lost. From Emperor Hui to Wen and Jing, the court rested with the people for more than sixty years, and the population greatly increased. Therefore the grand granary had grain that went uneaten, and the capital treasury had strings of cash that rotted. Emperor Wu relied on these stores, but military campaigns lasted more than thirty years, his territory expanded by ten thousand li, and the population under heaven was also reduced by half. When Huo Guang held government, he attended to reducing labor services. By Emperor Ping, six reigns had succeeded one another; although there were occasional campaigns, they were not enough to do great harm, and the households again increased. In Yuanshi 2, there were 103 commanderies and kingdoms and 1,587 counties and towns. The land measured 9,302 li east to west and 13,368 li north to south. Fixed cultivated fields were 8,270,536 qing; households numbered 13,233,612, and persons 59,194,978, which was 45,480,055 more than under King Cheng of Zhou: the height of Han prosperity. When Wang Mang usurped the throne, followed by the disorders of Gengshi and the Red Eyebrows, down to Guangwu’s restoration, the common people were exhausted, and only two in ten survived. In Zhongyuan 2, households numbered 4,271,634 and persons 21,007,820. In the Yongping and Jianchu periods, all under heaven had no affairs and the work was to nourish the people; down to Emperor He, households multiplied. During the Yongchu and Yuanchu periods of Emperor An, the hardships of war and famine again reduced the people. By Emperor Huan, they had somewhat increased over before. In Yongshou 2, households numbered 16,070,906 and persons 50,066,856; cultivated land was also abundant, though single armies were repeatedly sent out. When Emperor Ling encountered the Yellow Turbans, Emperor Xian came to the throne and Dong Zhuo raised disorder, burned the palaces and temples, and forced the imperial carriage west. The capital was desolate, strongmen contended together, and the likes of Guo Si and Li Jue caused still worse destruction. Therefore in the Xingping and Jian’an years, within the seas there was famine and devastation. The Son of Heaven wandered in flight; white bones filled the fields. At the disaster of Shaan Ford, fingers were gathered with dustpans; east of Anyi, the empress had no complete skirt. Then there were raids and warfare, no victor yet determined, and the common people were stripped and cut away for more than thirty years. When Emperor Wu of Wei had conquered and pacified all under heaven and Emperor Wen received the abdication, of the population only one in ten thousand survived. In Jingyuan 4, counting together with Shu, households numbered 943,423 and persons 5,372,891. Also, according to the report submitted in Zhengshi 5 by Zhu Zhao, General Who Displays Might, the military households commanded by Wu altogether numbered 132,000; extrapolating its population, it could not have been more than Shu. Formerly, in Han Yonghe 5, Nanyang had more than 500,000 households and Runan more than 400,000; compared with the present, the three emperors standing like the feet of a tripod do not exceed two commanderies. Add to this the people who receive stipends and exemptions, and the difficulties of famine years and epidemics: those who can be presented for service amount scarcely to one commandery. To use the people of one commandery to supply the needs of three emperors is indeed burdensome. From Yu to the present, more than two thousand years have passed, and the increases and decreases of six dynasties are fully set out here.” Chen Zhao notes: Mi’s record says there were 1,200 states in the Spring and Autumn period; it is not known where this comes from.

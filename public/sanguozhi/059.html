孫登字子高，權長子也。 魏黃初二年，以權爲吳王，拜登東中郎將，封萬戶侯，登辭疾不受。 是歲，立登爲太子，選置師傅，銓簡秀士，以爲賔友，於是諸葛恪、張休、顧譚、陳表等以選入，侍講詩書，出從騎射。 權欲登讀《漢書》，習知近代之事，以張昭有師法，重煩勞之，乃令休從昭受讀，還以授登。 登待接寮屬，略用布衣之禮，與恪、休、譚等或同輿而載，或共帳而寐。 太傅張溫言於權曰：「夫中庶子官最親密，切問近對，宜用儁德。 」於是乃用表等爲中庶子。 後又以庶子禮拘，復令整巾侍坐。 黃龍元年，權稱尊號，登爲皇太子，以恪爲左輔，休右弼，譚爲輔正，表爲翼正都尉，是爲四友，而謝景、范慎、刁玄、羊衜等皆爲賔客， 〈衜音道。〉 於是東宮號爲多士。 〈《吳錄》曰：慎字孝敬，廣陵人，竭忠知己之君，纏綿三益之友，時人榮之。 著論二十篇，名曰《矯非》。 後爲侍中，出補武昌左部督，治軍整頓。 孫皓移都，甚憚之，詔曰：「慎勳德俱茂，朕所敬憑，宜登上公，以副衆望。 」以爲太尉。 慎自恨久爲將，遂託老耄。 軍士戀之，舉營爲之隕涕。 鳳凰三年卒，子耀嗣。 玄，丹楊人。 衜，南陽人。 《吳書》曰：衜初爲中庶子，年二十。 時廷尉監隱蕃交結豪傑，自衞將軍全琮等皆傾心敬待，惟衜及宣詔郎豫章楊迪拒絕不與通，時人咸怪之。 而蕃後叛逆，衆乃服之。 《江表傳》曰：登使侍中胡綜作賔友目曰：「英才卓越，超踰倫匹，則諸葛恪。 精識時機，達幽究微，則顧譚。 凝辨宏達，言能釋結，則謝景。 究學甄微，游夏同科，則范慎。 」衜乃私駁綜曰：「元遜才而疏，子嘿精而狠，叔發辯而浮，孝敬深而狹。 」所言皆有指趣。 而衜卒以此言見咎，不爲恪等所親。 後四人皆敗，吳人謂衜之言有徵。 位至桂陽太守，卒。〉
Sun Deng, whose courtesy name was Zigao, was the eldest son of Sun Quan. In Wei’s second Huangchu year, Cao Wei invested Sun Quan as King of Wu, named Sun Deng General of the Household Gentlemen of the East, and offered him a fief of ten thousand households; Sun Deng pleaded illness and refused the title. The same year he was made crown prince. Sun Quan picked tutors for him, sifted talented men to serve as his companions, and Zhuge Ke, Zhang Xiu, Gu Tan, Chen Biao, and others were chosen to join him—indoors they lectured on the poetry and histories; outdoors they drilled riding and archery. Sun Quan wanted Sun Deng to read the Han shu and learn recent history. Zhang Zhao was the acknowledged master for that kind of reading, but Sun Quan hesitated to overwork him, so he had Zhang Xiu study under Zhang Zhao and then pass the lessons on to Sun Deng. When he met his staff, he kept the manners of a private gentleman rather than a palace heir—he rode in the same carriage with Zhuge Ke, Zhang Xiu, and Gu Tan, and sometimes shared a tent and slept beside them. Grand Tutor Zhang Wen urged Sun Quan: "The Household Grandee for All Purposes stands closest to the heir; whoever holds it must field sharp questions at his side, so the post calls for men of real moral weight." On that advice Chen Biao and others were named Household Grandees for All Purposes. Later, finding the grandees’ ritual too stiff, he had them dress their caps properly and sit with him in a more relaxed audience. In the first Huanglong year Sun Quan took the throne; Sun Deng was named imperial heir. Zhuge Ke became Left Support, Zhang Xiu Right Support, Gu Tan Rectifier of the Heir, Chen Biao Chief Commandant for Wing Rectification—the “Four Friends”—and Xie Jing, Fan Shen, Diao Xuan, Yang Dao, and others joined the heir’s circle as guests. 〈The gloss reads the character written Dao with the same pronunciation as the common graph for 'way.'〉 From then on people said the heir’s quarters were crowded with talent. 〈The Wu lu says Fan Shen, courtesy name Xiaojing, came from Guangling; he gave himself wholly to a lord who truly knew him and to friends who sharpened him on three fronts, and contemporaries thought that distinction enviable. He wrote twenty treatises collected as Correcting Error. He rose to Palace Attendant, then was posted out as Left Commander at Wuchang and ran the garrison with notable discipline. After Sun Hao shifted the capital he grew wary of Fan Shen and issued an edict praising his “abundant merit and virtue,” calling him a pillar of the throne, and saying he should be raised to the highest rank to satisfy public expectation. Fan Shen was named Grand Commandant. Fan Shen, bitter that he had spent his life in uniform, pleaded senility. The troops loved him; the entire camp wept when he left. He died in the third Fenghuang year; his son Fan Yao succeeded him. Diao Xuan was from Danyang. Yang Dao was from Nanyang. The Wu shu records that Yang Dao first held the post of Household Grandee for All Purposes at twenty. At the time the Court Supervisor Yin Fan was networking with local powerbrokers, and even Guard General Quan Zong courted him—only Yang Dao and the Edict Clerk Yang Di of Yuzhang would have nothing to do with Yin, which contemporaries found odd. When Yin Fan later turned traitor, everyone conceded that Yang Dao and Yang Di had been right. The Jiang biao zhuan says Sun Deng had Palace Attendant Hu Zong draft appraisals of his companions: “For brilliance that outstrips the common run, there is Zhuge Ke.” “For reading the times and sounding the depths, there is Gu Tan.” “For poised judgment, wide grasp, and words that cut through tangles, there is Xie Jing.” “For scholarship that sifts the fine points and matches the disciples You and Xia, there is Fan Shen.” Yang Dao quietly contradicted Hu Zong: “Yuansun is gifted but careless; Zimo is sharp but ruthless; Shufa is quick-tongued but shallow; Xiaojing is thoughtful but cramped.” Each verdict hit a nerve. He was resented for those remarks and never became intimate with Zhuge Ke’s clique. When all four later fell, Wu opinion decided that Yang Dao had seen them clearly. He ended his career as prefect of Guiyang and died.〉
2
權遷都建業，徵上大將軍陸遜輔登鎮武昌，領宮府留事。 登或射獵，當由徑道，常遠避良田，不踐苗稼，至所頓息，又擇空閑之地，其不欲煩民如此。 嘗乘馬出，有彈丸過，左右求之。 有一人操彈佩丸，咸以爲是，辭對不服，從者欲捶之，登不聽，使求過丸，比之非類，乃見釋。 又失盛水金馬盂，覺得其主，左右所爲，不忍致罰，呼責數之，長遣歸家，勑親近勿言。 後弟慮卒，權爲之降損，登晝夜兼行，到賴鄉，自聞，即時召見。 見權悲泣，因諫曰：「慮寢疾不起，此乃命也。 方今朔土未一，四海喁喁，天戴陛下，而以下流之念，減損大官殽饌，過於禮制，臣竊憂惶。 」權納其言，爲之加膳。 住十餘日，欲遣西還，深自陳乞，以乆離定省，子道有闕，又陳陸遜忠勤，無所顧憂，權遂留焉。 嘉禾三年，權征新城，使登居守，總知留事。 時年穀不豐，頗有盜賊，乃表定科令，所以防禦，甚得止姦之要。
Sun Quan transferred the seat of government to Jianye and called Lu Xun north to steady Sun Deng at Wuchang, leaving him in charge of the heir’s household and everything that stayed behind at the old capital. On hunts he kept to side tracks, gave wide berth to farmers’ fields, and never trampled the crops; when he camped he picked bare ground so commoners would not be put out—he was that careful of the realm’s labor. Once a sling bullet whistled past his horse while he was out riding, and his escort wanted to hunt down whoever had fired it. They seized a passerby with a sling and shot; everyone assumed he was guilty, but he protested his innocence. Sun Deng forbade a beating, had the actual pellet recovered, and proved it did not match the man’s ammunition, so they let him go. When a gold horse-head water bowl went missing, he knew which attendant had taken it yet could not bring himself to punish the thief: he summoned him, scolded him, dismissed him for good, and told his intimates to keep silent. When his younger brother Sun Lu died, Sun Quan went into mourning and curtailed court life; Sun Deng raced day and night to Lai Township, announced himself, and was admitted at once. Finding his father in tears, he urged him: “Sun Lu’s illness ran its course—that is heaven’s decree.” “The north is still unconquered; the world watches you alone. Yet grief has cut your palace meals below what ritual allows—I fear for what that signals.” Sun Quan took the point and ordered richer fare again. After a fortnight Sun Quan meant to send him back to Wuchang, but Sun Deng begged to stay: he had missed too many days of filial duty, he said, and Lu Xun was loyal enough to hold the west without him—so Sun Quan let him remain. In the third Jiahe year Sun Quan marched on Xincheng and left Sun Deng to mind the capital and every rear-area decision. Grain was short and bandits active, so he drafted statutes for home defense that struck exactly at the roots of lawlessness.

初，登所生庶賤，徐夫人少有母養之恩，後徐氏以妬廢處吳，而步夫人最寵。 步氏有賜，登不敢辭，拜受而已。 徐氏使至，所賜衣服，必沐浴服之。 登將拜太子，辭曰：「本立而道生，欲立太子，宜先立后。 」權曰：「卿母安在？ 」對曰：「在吳。 」權嘿然。 〈《吳書》曰：弟和有寵於權，登親敬，待之如兄，常有欲讓之心。〉
Sun Deng’s birth mother had been a humble concubine, but Lady Xu had raised him for a time; later Lady Xu was deposed for jealousy and sent to Wu, while Lady Bu became Sun Quan’s favorite. When Lady Bu’s people sent presents he accepted with a bow and never made more of it. If Lady Xu sent clothing, he bathed before he put it on. When they were about to name him crown prince, he objected: “The root must be set before the Way grows—if you mean to install an heir, establish the empress first.” Sun Quan asked, “Where is your mother?” “In Wu,” Sun Deng answered. Sun Quan said nothing. 〈The Wu shu adds that he honored his younger brother Sun He as an elder brother would and more than once thought of yielding precedence to him.〉
4
立凡二十一年，年三十三卒。 臨終，上疏曰：「臣以無狀，嬰抱篤疾，自省微劣，懼卒隕斃。 臣不自惜，念當委離供養，埋胔后土，長不復奉望宮省，朝覲日月，生無益於國，死貽陛下重慼，以此爲哽結耳。 臣聞死生有命，長短自天，周晉、顏回有上智之才，而尚夭折，況臣愚陋，年過其壽，生爲國嗣，沒享榮祚，於臣已多，亦何悲恨哉！ 方今大事未定，逋寇未討，萬國喁喁，係命陛下，危者望安，亂者仰治。 願陛下棄忘臣身，割下流之恩，脩黃老之術，篤養神光，加羞珍膳，廣開神明之慮，以定無窮之業，則率土幸賴，臣死無恨也。 皇子和仁孝聦哲，德行清茂，宜早建置，以繫民望。 諸葛恪才略博達，器任佐時。 張休、顧譚、謝景，皆通敏有識斷，入宜委腹心，出可爲爪牙。 范慎、華融矯矯壯節，有國士之風。 羊衜辯捷，有專對之材。 刁玄優弘，志履道真。 裴欽博記，翰采足用。 蔣脩、虞翻，志節分明。 凡此諸臣，或宜廊廟，或任將帥，皆練時事，明習法令，守信固義，有不可奪之志。 此皆陛下日月所照，選置臣宮，得與從事，備知情素，敢以陳聞。 臣重惟當今方外多虞，師旅未休，當厲六軍，以圖進取。 軍以人爲衆，衆以財爲寶，竊聞郡縣頗有荒殘，民物凋弊，姦亂萌生，是以法令繁滋，刑辟重切。 臣聞爲政聽民，律令與時推移，誠宜與將相大臣詳擇時宜，博采衆議，寬刑輕賦，均息力役，以順民望。 陸遜忠勤於時，出身憂國，謇謇在公，有匪躬之節。 諸葛瑾、步隲、朱然、全琮、朱據、呂岱、吾粲、闞澤、嚴畯、張承、孫怡忠於爲國，通達治體。 可令陳上便宜，蠲除苛煩，愛養士馬，撫循百姓。 五年之外，十年之內，遠者歸復，近者盡力，兵不血刃，而大事可定也。 臣聞『鳥之將死其鳴也哀，人之將死其言也善』，故子囊臨終，遺言戒時，君子以爲忠，豈況臣登，其能已乎？ 願陛下留意聽采，臣雖死之日，猶生之年也。 」旣絕而後書聞，權益以摧感，言則隕涕。 是歲，赤烏四年也。 謝景時爲豫章太守，不勝哀情，棄官奔赴，拜表自劾。 權曰：「君與太子從事，異於他吏。 」使中使慰勞，聽復本職，發遣還郡。 謚登曰宣太子。 〈《吳書》曰：初葬句容，置園邑，奉守如法，後三年改葬蔣陵。〉
He held the position of heir for twenty-one years and died at thirty-three. Near death he sent up a memorial: “I have behaved poorly and now lie under a heavy sickness; I know I am slight and fear I may fall at any moment.” “I do not pity myself, only that I must leave your service, be laid in the earth, and never again attend your dawn and dusk levees—useless in life, I will add another wound in death.” “I know fate fixes life and span; princes Jin and Yan Hui were prodigies yet died young—how much more a dull man who has already outlived his share? I was heir to Wu in life and will take honor with me in death; I have had more than I deserve and bear no grudge.” “The great work is unfinished, the enemy still in the field, and every district hangs on you—people in danger want peace; people in chaos want order.” “Forget me, set grief aside, nourish yourself as the Yellow Emperor’s school advises, take richer food, widen your counsel, and secure the dynasty’s future—then though I die I die content.” “Prince He is humane, filial, and quick-witted, with clear virtue—name him heir soon so the people have something to hold to.” “Zhuge Ke’s grasp of policy is wide and deep—keep him at the center of affairs.” “Zhang Xiu, Gu Tan, and Xie Jing are sharp judges—use them inside as confidants and outside as blades.” “Fan Shen and Hua Rong are iron in integrity—true ‘state knights.’” “Yang Dao is quick on his feet in debate—send him when words must carry the day.” “Diao Xuan is broad-minded and steady—his life matches his principles.” “Pei Qin’s memory and brush are equal to any task you give him.” “Jiang Xiu and Yu Fan hold their integrity without blur.” “Each man here belongs either in the council chamber or in the field; all know the times, the code, and unbreakable loyalty.” “You lit their careers yourself; I have watched them at my side and can vouch for them.” “Beyond the passes trouble still boils; keep the hosts ready for the next advance.” “Armies live on men and men on wealth—yet I hear counties are ruined, folk exhausted, and crime spreading, which is why law piles on law and punishments bite harder.” “Good rule bends to the people’s need—widen consultation, lighten tax and labor, and move the code with the times.” “Lu Xun gives himself wholly to the realm—frank, selfless, the backbone of your government.” “Zhuge Jin, Bu Zhi, Zhu Ran, Quan Zong, Zhu Ju, Lü Dai, Wu Can, Kan Ze, Yan Jun, Zhang Cheng, and Sun Yi are loyal servants who understand how rule actually works.” “Charge them to report what helps the people—scrap petty exactions, tend the armies, soothe the districts.” “Given five years, perhaps ten, exiles will drift home, neighbors will bend to the plow, and you can finish the great work without another slaughter.” “They say a dying bird’s cry is sad and a dying man’s words are true—Zi-nang’s last counsel was called loyal; how can I fall silent?” “Listen, I beg you—if you take this advice, my death will feel like life.” The memorial arrived only after he had died; Sun Quan broke when he read it and wept whenever he spoke of it. It was the fourth Chiwu year. Xie Jing was prefect of Yuzhang; overwhelmed, he threw down his seal and raced to the mourning hall, then memorialized to impeach himself. Sun Quan answered, “Your service to the heir was not the same as other clerks’.” He sent a chamberlain to comfort Xie Jing, let him return to his post, and sent him back to his commandery. Sun Deng was given the posthumous title “Heir Apparent Xuan.” 〈The Wu shu notes he was first buried at Jurong with a full funerary estate, then moved three years later to the imperial tombs at Jiangling.〉

子璠、希，皆早卒，次子英，封吳侯。 五鳳元年，英以大將軍孫峻擅權，謀誅峻，事覺自殺，國除。 〈《吳歷》曰：孫和以無罪見殺，衆庶皆懷憤歎，前司馬桓慮因此招合將吏，欲共殺峻立英，事覺，皆見殺，英實不知。〉
His sons Sun Fan and Sun Xi died young; the second son, Sun Ying, was made marquis of Wu. In the first Wufeng year Sun Ying tried to kill regent Sun Jun for usurping authority; the plot leaked and Sun Ying took his own life, and his fief was struck off. 〈The Wu li claims the people mourned the innocent Sun He, that the former major Huan Lu then gathered officers to murder Sun Jun and enthrone Sun Ying, that the plot was exposed and everyone executed, and that Sun Ying knew nothing of it.〉
6
謝景者字叔發，南陽宛人。 在郡有治迹，吏民稱之，以爲前有顧劭，其次即景。 數年卒官。
Xie Jing, courtesy name Shufa, came from Wan in Nanyang. As prefect he left a record of honest rule; officials and commoners ranked him just below the celebrated Gu Shao. He died in office a few years later.
7
孫慮字子智，登弟也。 少敏惠有才藝，權器愛之。 黃武七年，封建昌侯。 後二年，丞相雍等奏慮性聦體達，所尚日新，比方近漢，宜進爵稱王，權未許。 乆之，尚書僕射存上疏曰：「帝王之興，莫不襃崇至親，以光羣后，故魯衞於周，寵冠諸侯，高帝五王，封列于漢，所以藩屏本朝，爲國鎮衞。 建昌侯慮稟性聦敏，才兼文武，於古典制，宜正名號。 陛下謙光，未肯如舊，羣寮大小，咸用於邑。 方今姦寇恣睢，金鼓未弭，腹心爪牙，惟親與賢。 輒與丞相雍等議，咸以慮宜爲鎮軍大將軍，授任偏方，以光大業。 」權乃許之，於是假節開府，治半州。 〈《吳書》載權詔曰：「期運擾亂，凶邪肆虐，威罰有序，干戈不戢。 以慮氣志休懿，武略夙昭，必能爲國佐定大業，故授以上將之位，至以殊特之榮，寵以兵馬之勢，委以偏方之任。 外欲威振敵虜，厭難萬里，內欲鎮撫遠近，慰卹將士，誠慮建功立事竭命之秋也。 慮其內脩文德，外經武訓，持盈若沖，則滿而不溢。 敬慎乃心，無忝所受。」〉 慮以皇子之尊，富於春秋，遠近嫌其不能留意。 及至臨事，遵奉法度，敬納師友，過於衆望。 年二十，嘉禾元年卒。 無子，國除。
Sun Lu, courtesy name Zizhi, was Sun Deng’s younger brother. Clever and accomplished as a boy, he became Sun Quan’s favorite. In Huangwu 7 he was made marquis of Jianchang. Two years on, Chancellor Zhang Yong and colleagues urged that Sun Lu’s quick mind and balanced judgment set a new standard, that Han precedent called for a princely title, and Sun Quan—for the moment—refused. Time passed, and a vice director of the Secretariat surnamed Cun (the text preserves only this fragment of his name) memorialized: “Every dynasty exalts close kin so the peerage shines—Lu and Wei under Zhou and Gaozu’s five princes in Han were the shields of the throne.” “Sun Lu of Jianchang is clever by nature and master of both pen and sword; classical precedent says his title should be regularized.” Your Majesty's humility keeps you from following established honors, and every rank at court is sunk in gloom. “Rebels still swagger; drums have not quieted—heart, claw, and fang must be kin and worthy men.” “Zhang Yong and I agree: make him General Who Guards the Army and give him a flank command to magnify the great work.” Sun Quan relented: Sun Lu received the credential-axe, opened his own command, and took charge of half the lower Yangzi circuit. 〈The Wu shu quotes an edict of Sun Quan: “Fate has turned violent and the wicked trample the realm; law still bites, yet the spears never rest.” “Sun Lu’s temper is steady, his strategy proven—he can anchor our great work—so I give him a field marshal’s table, singular honors, real troops, and a border command.” “I want him to overawe the foe beyond the passes and steady the army within—this is the hour to build a name or spend a life trying.” “Let him polish civil rule at home and harden the camps abroad; stay humble though exalted, and he will rise without overflowing.” “Guard your charge with care and never disgrace the trust.”〉 People worried that a prince so young would lose focus on real work. Once he took office he followed every statute, listened to his tutors, and outran every expectation. He died in Jiahe 1 at twenty. Without an heir, his marquisate was struck off.

Sun He, whose courtesy name was Zixiao, was Sun Lu’s younger brother. His mother Lady Wang’s favor won him Sun Quan’s affection; at fourteen he received a household guard and Kan Ze, as director of the secretariat, tutored him in letters. He loved books, treated scholars with deference, and earned wide praise. Chiwu 5 made him crown prince; he was nineteen. Kan Ze took the grand tutorship, Xue Zong the junior post, and Cai Ying, Zhang Chun, Feng Fu, Yan Wei, and others formed his informal retinue. 〈The Wu shu says he was clever as a boy, so Sun Quan kept him always at hand and showered him with dress, rank, and treasures no other son matched. He excelled at literature and horsemanship, studied under noted teachers, showed sharp judgment, honored his tutors, and cultivated talent. Whenever Cai Ying and his circle came to congratulate him, Sun He set rank aside and greeted them warmly. He led seminars on the canon, weighed right and wrong, polled ministers on policy, and rated men’s performance with cool consistency. Later Zhuge Yi pretended to rebel in order to bait Zhuge Dan of Wei, while Sun Quan hid an army to spring the trap. With his father camped in the field—war being an ill omen—Sun He could not bring himself to feast; he sent repeated memorials urging caution and total victory, and only when Sun Quan came home did he sleep soundly. Zhang Chun, courtesy name Yuanji, was Zhang Dun’s son. The Wu lu records that Zhang Chun hardened his character young, read widely, shot sharp questions and quicker answers, and carried himself impressively. He rose to palace gentleman, then magistrate of Guangde with a distinguished record, and was pulled up to chief commandant supporting the heir.〉

Bureaucrats had begun sending written interrogatories; Sun He argued that schemers would twist answers to sow disaster and asked Sun Quan to forbid the practice. When Area Commander Liu Bao and Household Grandee Ding Yan traded accusations, Sun He told Ding Yan, “How many able officers do we have? If you tear at each other’s files you invite ruin, not fortune.” He made them drop the charges and reconcile. He lectured that scholars should study useful arts and drill bow and chariot for public duty, not waste days at gaming tables. At a later banquet he argued that dice and boards wasted spirit, achieved nothing, and did not build character or career. A man of purpose hoards time and strength; a gentleman looks to the high road and is ashamed to settle for less. Heaven and earth roll on while man flickers past like the proverbial white colt at a crevice—age turns, and splendor does not return. The trap is appetite; cut useless craving, drop busywork, and the path to reputation opens. Men need recreation, but feasts, music, books, and riding serve as well as gambling. He told the eight companions at table each to draft an essay against gaming. Household Grandee Wei Yao drafted a piece for him; Sun He passed it to his circle. Cai Ying was addicted to weiqi, and clerks copied him—Sun He meant the essays as a barb at them.

Later Lady Wang fell out with the Princess of Quan. When Sun Quan fell ill, Sun He sacrificed at the ancestral shrine; his consort’s uncle Zhang Xiu lived next door and asked him in. The princess of Quan set spies and reported that the heir had skipped the shrine to plot at his in-laws’. She added that Lady Wang smiled when she heard the emperor was sick. Sun Quan raged; Lady Wang died of fright; Sun He’s favor thinned and he lived in fear of deposition. Prince Sun Ba openly coveted the throne; Lu Xun, Wu Can, and Gu Tan insisted on the heir–cadet distinction, while Quan Ji and Yang Zhu backed Sun Ba and daily poured poison into Sun Quan’s ear. Wu Can was jailed and killed; Gu Tan was exiled to Jiao. Sun Quan hesitated for years. 〈Yin Ji’s Tong yu says that at first Sun He and Sun Ba still lived in the same palace and their ranks were not yet split. Ministers argued that crown prince and royal son must differ in rank, so separate households were ordered—and the feud began. Attendants and guests split into two camps, and suspicion climbed the ladder to the great officers. Lu Xun, Zhuge Ke, Gu Tan, Zhu Ju, Teng Yin, Shi Ji, Ding Mi, and others backed the heir; Bu Zhi, Lü Dai, Quan Zong, Lü Ju, Sun Hong, and others backed the Prince of Lu—court and camp were torn in two. Sun Quan told Sun Jun, “If brothers feud and officers pick sides, we repeat the Yuan disaster and become a laughingstock.” “Name one heir and how can the realm stay quiet?” Plans to replace the heir took shape. Pei Songzhi remarks that Yuan Shao and Liu Biao truly preferred younger sons, whereas Sun Quan first installed Sun He then pampered Sun Ba—he courted the disaster himself and outdid Yuan and Liu in folly. Bu Zhi was a statesman of reputation—why did he cling to Sun Ba like Yang Zhu? Once the heir was fixed, decency forbade favoring the cadet—especially when Sun Ba had done nothing to earn the throne. Villains are not wholly base—one bad choice erases every virtue. If Bu Zhi really chose that faction, nothing else he did can redeem him. Lü Dai and Quan Zong scarcely merit mention.〉 Sun He was imprisoned in isolation. Zhu Ju and Vice Director Qu Huang led officers with earth on their foreheads and ropes on their wrists to the palace gate day after day pleading for Sun He. Sun Quan mounted the White Heron Tower, hated the sight, and barked at them to stop their clamor. He meant to depose Sun He for Sun Liang; Chen Zheng and Chen Xiang cited Duke Xian of Jin’s murder of Crown Prince Sheng; Zhu Ju and Qu Huang still would not relent. Sun Quan massacred Chen Zheng’s and Chen Xiang’s families and had Zhu Ju and Qu Huang flogged a hundred strokes in court, 〈The Wu li says Qu Huang shouted from the floor that the crown prince’s virtue was known everywhere. “The three kingdoms brace like a tripod—this is no time to unsettle the heir.” “Spare one thought for wisdom, and though I die I live again.” He beat his head on the stone until the blood ran but would not yield. Sun Quan refused him and sent him home to farm. Sun Hao later edicted that Qu Huang had died trying to save the dynasty. He ennobled Qu Huang’s son Qu Xu and named his brothers commandants. Qu Xu rose to vice director of the secretariat in his turn. Qu Huang came from Runan—see Hu Chong’s dialogues. The Wu shu adds that Zhang Chun argued to the death, was jailed, then executed in the marketplace.〉 Sun He was banished to Guzhang; a dozen officials who protested were killed or dismissed. Public opinion called it a gross injustice. 〈The Wu shu claims Sun Quan near death wanted Sun He restored but the Princess of Quan, Sun Jun, and Sun Hong blocked him.〉

When Sun Xiu died Sun Hao took the throne, posthumously named his father Emperor Wen, reburied him at Mingling with a two-hundred-household guard. Baoding 2, seventh month: he ordered Xue Xu to raise a mortuary hall called the Pure Temple. Sun Ba, courtesy name Ziwei, was Sun He’s younger brother. Sun He held the position of crown prince. Sun Ba was Prince of Lu, yet Sun Quan showered him with the same favor he gave the heir. Soon word of the brothers’ feud reached Sun Quan, who barred their courts from each other and called it “dedication to scholarship.” Yang Dao, as army supervisor envoy, wrote: “Ancient kings always drew a bright line between heir and younger sons and enfeoffed the rest, both to honor the ancestors and to fence the throne.” The dual investiture won praise across the realm as the bedrock of Wu’s strength.” “Now both households have shut their doors to visitors, and the whole country is uneasy.” “From what I hear, both princes are bright; three years under their proper titles they have earned praise at home and respect in Wei and Shu.” “We expected you to answer that loyalty by letting both courts again host men from every quarter so distant powers would wish to submit.” “Instead you issued an edict stripping guards and guests—perhaps to keep them at their books—but it is not what your servants hoped to see.” “Rumors say both courts break precedent—that is what keeps me awake.” “If there is fault, investigate quietly; do not let gossip spread.” “Suspicion curdles into slander, and the northwest borders are close enough that rumor crosses to the enemy.” “When that talk starts, men will say the princes plot treason—how will you answer Wei and Shu?” “If you cannot convince outsiders, you cannot calm your own people.” “Domestic doubt and foreign calumny do not nurture majesty or steady the altars.” “Issue a generous edict, reopen both courts, restore the old courtesies—then heaven clears, earth rests, and the realm rejoices.”

After Sun Ba’s death Sun Quan executed Quan Ji, Wu An, and Sun Qi for plotting with him against Sun He.

孫奮字子揚，霸弟也，母曰仲姬。 太元二年，立爲齊王，居武昌。 權薨，太傅諸葛恪不欲諸王處江濵兵馬之地，徙奮於豫章。 奮怒，不從命，又數越法度。 恪上牋諫曰：「帝王之尊，與天同位，是以家天下，臣父兄，四海之內，皆爲臣妾。 仇讎有善，不得不舉，親戚有惡，不得不誅，所以承天理物，先國後身，蓋聖人立制，百代不易之道也。 昔漢初興，多王子弟至於太彊，輒爲不軌，上則幾危社稷，下則骨肉相殘，其後懲戒，以爲大諱。 自光武以來，諸王有制，惟得自娛於宮內，不得臨民，干與政事，其與交通，皆有重禁，遂以全安，各保福祚。 此則前世得失之驗也。 近袁紹、劉表各有國土，土地非狹，人衆非弱，以適庶不分，遂滅其宗祀。 此乃天下愚智，所共嗟痛。 大行皇帝覽古戒今，防芽遏萌，慮於千載。 是以寢疾之日，分遣諸王，各早就國，詔策殷勤，科禁嚴峻，其所戒勑，無所不至，誠欲上安宗廟，下全諸王，使百世相承，無凶國害家之悔也。 大王宜上惟太伯順父之志，中念河間獻王、東海王彊恭敬之節，下當裁抑驕恣荒亂以爲警戒。 而聞頃至武昌以來，多違詔勑，不拘制度，擅發諸將兵治護宮室。 又左右常從有罪過者，當以表聞，公付有司，而擅私殺，事不明白。 大司馬呂岱親受先帝詔勑，輔導大王，旣不承用其言，令懷憂怖。 華錡先帝近臣，忠良正直，其所陳道，當納用之，而聞怒錡，有收縛之語。 又中書楊融，親受詔勑，所當恭肅，云『正自不聽禁，當如我何』？ 聞此之日，大小驚怪，莫不寒心。 里語曰：『明鏡所以照形，古事所以知今。 』大王宜深以魯王爲戒，改易其行，戰戰兢兢，盡敬朝廷，如此則無求不得。 若棄忘先帝法教，懷輕慢之心，臣下寧負大王，不敢負先帝遺詔，寧爲大王所怨疾，豈敢忘尊主之威，而令詔勑不行於藩臣邪？ 此古今正義，大王所照知也。 夫福來有由，禍來有漸，漸生不憂，將不可悔。 向使魯王早納忠直之言，懷驚懼之慮，享祚無窮，豈有滅亡之禍哉？ 夫良藥苦口，惟疾者能甘之。 忠言逆耳，惟達者能受之。 今者恪等慺慺欲爲大王除危殆於萌芽，廣福慶之基原，是以不自知言至，願蒙三思。」
Sun Fen, courtesy name Ziyang, was Sun Ba’s brother; his mother was Lady Zhongji. Taiyuan 2 made him prince of Qi with his seat at Wuchang. After Sun Quan’s death Zhuge Ke refused to leave princes on the Yangzi garrison line and transferred Sun Fen to Yuzhang. Sun Fen refused the order and broke the rules again and again. Zhuge Ke wrote: “The Son of Heaven shares rank with heaven—he owns the realm and even kin are his subjects.” “He must reward a foe’s virtue and punish a kinsman’s crime—state before self; that law does not change.” “Early Han princes grew too strong, nearly toppled the throne, and turned on one another—later dynasties took warning.” “Since Guangwu, princes lived under strict house arrest—no rule over commoners, no politics, heavy ban on private contacts—and so stayed safe.” History proves the point.” “Yuan Shao and Liu Biao were strong yet ruined themselves by blurring heir and younger son.” “The world still groans over that lesson.” “Our late emperor read history, nipped intrigue in the bud, and thought a thousand years ahead.” “On his sickbed he sent every prince to his fief with stern edicts—he meant to secure temple and family for generations.” “You should think of Taibo’s deference, of Han’s model princes, and rein in pride before it becomes Sun Ba’s disaster.” “Yet since you reached Wuchang you have ignored edicts, called up troops on your own, and put them to building your palace.” “When attendants broke the law you should have reported them to the ministries; instead you executed them secretly.” “Grand Marshal Lü Dai holds your father’s charge to guide you; your defiance terrifies him.” “Hua Qi spoke truth for the late emperor; you answered with threats to bind him.” “Palace Secretary Yang Rong bore an imperial order; rumor says he sneered, ‘If I ignore the rules, what can he do?’” “When that line spread, every official shuddered.” “As the proverb says, the mirror shows the face; history shows the present.” “Take Sun Ba as your warning, change your ways, fear the throne, and you may yet keep everything you seek.” “Ignore your father’s law and we will obey him, not you—we would rather earn your hatred than let your defiance stand.” “That is the justice every age knows.” “Fortune has roots; disaster creeps in degree by degree—ignore the first step and there is no turning back.” “Had Sun Ba listened while there was time, he would still be alive.” “Bitter medicine helps only the patient who swallows it.” “Harsh counsel helps only the ruler wise enough to hear it.” “We plead only to cut your risk at the root; forgive our bluntness and think thrice.”
15
奮得牋懼，遂移南昌，游獵彌甚，官屬不堪命。 及恪誅，奮下住蕪湖，欲至建業觀變。 傅相謝慈等諫奮，奮殺之。 〈慈字孝宗，彭城人，見《禮論》，撰《喪服圖及變除》行於世。〉 坐廢爲庶人，徙章安縣。 太平三年，封爲章安侯。 〈《江表傳》載亮詔曰：「齊王奮前坐殺吏，廢爲庶人，連有赦令，獨不見原，縱未宜復王，何以不侯？ 又諸孫兄弟作將，列在江渚，孤有兄獨爾云何？ 」有司奏可，就拜爲侯。〉
Sun Fen took fright, moved to Nanchang, and hunted harder than ever while his staff groaned under his whims. After Zhuge Ke’s murder he camped at Wuhu, eyeing a march on the capital. His tutors Xie Ci and others remonstrated; he executed them. 〈Xie Ci, courtesy name Xiaozong, came from Pengcheng, wrote ritual treatises still in circulation.〉 He was degraded to commoner rank and banished to Zhang’an county. Taiping 3 restored him as marquis of Zhang’an. 〈The Jiang biao zhuan quotes Sun Liang: “Sun Fen killed officials and was cast out; amnesties passed him by—if not a prince, why not a marquis?” “Other Suns command the river—why is my brother alone disgraced?” The ministry approved and Sun Fen received the seal.〉
16
建衡二年，孫皓左夫人王氏卒。 皓哀念過甚，朝夕哭臨，數月不出，由是民間或謂皓死，訛言奮與上虞侯奉當有立者。 奮母仲姬墓在豫章，豫章太守張俊疑其或然，掃除墳塋。 皓聞之，車裂俊，夷三族，誅奮及其五子，國除。 〈《江表傳》曰：豫章吏十人乞代俊死，皓不聽。 奮以此見疑，本在章安，徙還吳城禁錮，使男女不得通婚，或年三十四十不得嫁娶。 奮上表乞自比禽獸，使男女自相配偶。 皓大怒，遣察戰齎藥賜奮，奮不受藥，叩頭千下，曰：「老臣自將兒子治生求治，無豫國事，乞丐餘年。 」皓不聽，父子皆飲藥死。 臣松之案：建衡二年至奮之死，孫皓即位，尚猶未乆。 若奮未被疑之前，兒女年二十左右，至奮死時，不得年三十四十也。 若先已長大，自失時未婚娶，則不由皓之禁錮矣。 此雖欲增皓之惡，然非實理。〉
Jianheng 2: Sun Hao’s senior consort Lady Wang died. Sun Hao mourned so wildly that he vanished from court for months; rumor said he was dead and named Sun Fen or Sun Feng of Shangyu as the next ruler. Sun Fen’s mother was buried in Yuzhang; Prefect Zhang Jun swept her tomb, hedging in case the rumor was true. Sun Hao had Zhang Jun torn between carts, extirpated three clans, killed Sun Fen and five sons, and erased the princedom. 〈Ten Yuzhang clerks offered to die for Zhang Jun; Sun Hao refused. Sun Fen was then suspected, moved from Zhang’an to Wu city under house arrest, and his household barred from marriage until some children were past thirty. He begged in a memorial to be treated like livestock free to breed among themselves. Sun Hao sent an “inspector of warfare” with poison; Sun Fen refused, kowtowed a thousand times, and swore he minded only his sons’ livelihood and never politics, pleading for a few more years. Sun Hao ignored him; father and sons drank the draught and died. Pei Songzhi notes: from Jianheng 2 to Sun Fen’s death Sun Hao had reigned only a short while. If the children were about twenty when he fell under suspicion, they could not have been thirty or forty when he died. If they were already of age before his disgrace, Sun Hao’s marriage ban cannot explain their celibacy. The tale piles horror on Sun Hao but does not quite add up.〉

【評】
Appraisal
18
評曰：孫登居心所存，足爲茂美之德。 慮、和並有好善之姿，規自砥礪，或短命早終，或不得其死，哀哉！ 霸以庶干適，奮不遵軌度，固取危亡之道也。 然奮之誅夷，橫遇飛禍矣。
The verdict: Sun Deng’s character was everything a crown prince should be. Sun Lu and Sun He loved learning and honed themselves—one died young, one was destroyed; both stir pity. Sun Ba usurped the heir’s place; Sun Fen scorned the law—each courted ruin by choice. Sun Fen’s slaughter was sheer mischance—guilt by rumor and a tyrant’s panic.

杜微字國輔，梓潼涪人也。 少受學於廣漢任安。 劉障辟為從事，以疾去官。 及先主定蜀，微常稱聾，閉門不出。
Du Wei, courtesy name Guofu, came from Fu in Zitong commandery. As a young man he studied under Ren An of Guanghan. Liu Zhang appointed him adjutant, but illness forced him to resign. After the First Lord conquered Shu, Wei feigned deafness and shut himself away.
2
建興二年，丞相亮領益州牧。 選迎皆妙簡舊德，以秦宓為別駕，五梁為功曹，微為主簿。 微固辭，舉而致之。 既致，亮引見微，微自陳謝。 亮以微不聞人語，於坐上與書曰：
In the second year of Jianxing, Chancellor Zhuge Liang added the title of shepherd of Yi Province. His appointments favored established worthies: Qin Mi became chief clerk on tour, Wu Liang merit assessor, and Du Wei chief secretary. Wei refused repeatedly until they virtually escorted him to office. Once there, Liang received him in audience; Wei apologized and begged off. Knowing Wei could not hear conversation, Liang wrote him a note at the table:
3
服聞德行，飢渴歷時，清濁異流，無緣諮覯。 王元泰、李伯仁、王文儀、楊季休、丁君乾、李永南兄弟、文仲寶等，每歎高志，未見如舊。 猥以空虛，統領貴州，德薄任重，慘慘憂慮、朝廷主公今年始十八，天姿仁敏，愛德下士。 天下之人思慕漢室，欲與君因天順民，輔此明主，以隆季興之功，著勳於竹帛也。 以謂賢愚下相為謀，故自割絕，守勞而已，不圖自屈也。
I have long admired your character, yet our paths never crossed— like clear and muddy waters that never mingle. Wang Yuantai, Li Borun, Wang Wenyi, Yang Jixiu, Ding Junqian, the Li Yongnan brothers, Wen Zhongbao, and the rest often spoke of your high aims and regretted losing touch. I govern this land with scant virtue and a crushing burden, anxious day and night. Our sovereign has only just turned eighteen— gifted, humane, and eager to honor men of talent. All under Heaven yearn for Han. I hope you will join me in aiding this enlightened ruler, Heaven and the people willing, to rebuild the dynasty's fortunes and win a place in history. I thought men of different gifts seldom counsel one another, so I kept my distance and minded my own office— I never meant to impose on you.
4
微自乞老病求歸，亮又與書答曰：
Du Wei pleaded age and infirmity and asked to retire. Liang wrote again:
5
曹丕篡弒，自立為帝，是猶土龍芻狗之有名也。 欲與羣賢因其邪偽，以正道滅之。 怪君未有相誨，便欲求還於山野。 丕又大興勞役，以向吳、楚。 今因丕多務，且以閉境勤農，育養民物，並治甲兵，以待其挫，然後伐之，可使兵不戰民不勞而天下定也。 君但當以德輔時耳，不責君軍事，何為汲汲欲求去乎！
Cao Pi murdered his sovereign and stole the throne— a puppet emperor, as hollow as a clay dragon or a paper dog. I mean to join every able man in using his own treason to crush him with the righteous cause. I am surprised you offer no counsel yet already seek the hills and forests. Cao Pi has again launched vast levies against the southeast. While he is overstretched, we should seal the passes, encourage farming, rest the people, and forge arms until his strength flags— then strike so that armies need not clash and the realm may be pacified without exhausting the common folk. I ask only that you lend the age your moral weight, not that you bear arms— why this desperate hurry to withdraw?
6
其敬微如此，拜為諫議大夫，以從其志。 五梁者，字德山，犍為南安人也，以儒學節操稱。 從議郎遷諫義大夫、五官中朗將。
Such was Liang's deference: he named Wei remonstrance counsellor and let him live as he wished. Wu Liang of Nanan in Qianwei, courtesy name Deshan, was known for scholarship and moral fiber. He rose from master of discussion to remonstrance grandee and general of the household for the five offices.
7
周羣字仲直，巴西閬中人也。 父舒，字叔布，少學術於廣漢楊厚，名亞董扶、任安。 數被徵，終不詣。 時人有問：「《春秋讖》曰『代漢者當塗高』，此何謂也？ 」舒曰：「當塗高者，魏也。 」鄉黨學者私傳其語，羣少受學於舒，專心候業。 於庭中做小樓，家富多奴，常令奴更直於樓上視天災，才見一氣，即白羣，羣自上樓觀之，不避晨夜，故凡有氣候，無不見之者，是以所言多中。 州牧劉璋辟以為師友從事。 〈《續漢書》曰：建安七年，越巂有男子化為女人，時羣言哀帝時亦有此，將易代之祥也。 至二十五年，獻帝果封於山陽。 十二年十月，有星孛於鶉尾，荊州分野，羣以為荊州牧將死而失土。 明年秋，劉表卒，曹公平荊州。 十七年十二月，星孛於五諸侯，羣以為西方專據土地者皆將失土。 是時，劉璋據益州，張魯據漢中，韓遂據涼州，宋建據枹罕。 明年冬，曹公遣偏將擊涼州。 十九年，獲宋建，韓遂逃於羌中，被殺。 其年秋，璋失益州。 二十年秋，曹公攻漢中，張魯降。〉 先主定蜀，署儒林校尉。
Zhou Qun, courtesy name Zhongzhi, came from Langzhong in Ba commandery. His father Zhou Shu, courtesy name Shubu, studied under Yang Hou of Guanghan in youth and stood just below Dong Fu and Ren An in reputation. Court summoned him repeatedly; he never answered. A contemporary once asked how to read the prophecy, "He who replaces Han shall be High on the Road." Zhou Shu answered, "High on the Road points to Wei." Scholars repeated his gloss in private. Zhou Qun studied under his father and devoted himself to celestial divination. He put up a small tower in the courtyard and set slaves to watch the sky in shifts day and night. The moment a portent appeared they roused Qun, who climbed to study it himself. Because he missed nothing, his forecasts usually came true. Liu Zhang, as shepherd, named him adjutant for teachers and friends. The Continued Han History records that in the seventh year of Jianan a man in Yuexi turned into a woman. Qun likened it to an omen from Emperor Ai's reign and called it a sign of dynastic change. By the twenty-fifth year of Jianan the abdicated emperor had indeed been enfeoffed at Shanyang. In the tenth month of the twelfth year of Jianan a comet swept the Quail's Tail asterism, Jing Province's celestial field. Qun predicted the death of Jing's shepherd and the loss of his territory. The following autumn Liu Biao died and Cao Cao took Jing Province. In the twelfth month of the seventeenth year a comet crossed the Five Feudatories. Qun read it as doom for every western warlord who clung to territory. Liu Zhang still held Yi, Zhang Lu Hanzhong, Han Sui Liangzhou, and Song Jian the Fuhan salient. The next winter Cao Cao sent a column against Liangzhou. In the nineteenth year Song Jian was taken; Han Sui fled to the Qiang and died there. That same autumn Liu Zhang lost Yi Province. In the autumn of the twentieth year Cao Cao invaded Hanzhong and Zhang Lu yielded. After the First Lord settled Shu he named Qun colonel of the forest of scholars.
8
先主欲曹公爭漢中，問羣，羣對曰：「當得其地，不得其民也，若出偏軍，必不利，當戒慎之！ 」時州後部司馬蜀郡張裕亦曉佔候，而天才過羣， 〈裕字南和。〉 諫先主曰：「不可爭漢中，軍必不利。 」先主竟不用裕言，果得地而不得民也。 遣將軍吳蘭、雷銅等入成都，皆沒不還，悉如羣言。 於是舉羣茂才。
Planning to wrest Hanzhong from Cao Cao, the First Lord asked Qun's opinion. Qun said, "You will take the ground but not the population. A detached column will fare badly— proceed with utmost care." Zhang Yu of Shu, the province's rear army marshal, was an even keener astrologer than Qun, Zhang Yu's courtesy name was Nanhe. He warned the First Lord, "Do not contest Hanzhong— the expedition will end badly." The First Lord ignored Zhang Yu and, as predicted, won the terrain but not the people. He sent Wu Lan, Lei Tong, and others on the detached columns; they were swallowed up without return— exactly as Zhou Qun had foretold. Qun was then recommended as flourishing talent.
9
裕又私語人曰：「歲在庚子，天下當易代，劉氏祚盡矣。 主公得益州，九年之後，寅卯之間當失之。 」入密白其言。 初，先主與劉璋會涪，時裕為璋從事，侍坐，其人饒須，先主嘲之曰：「昔吾居涿縣，特多毛姓，東西南北皆諸毛也，涿令稱曰『諸毛繞涿居乎』！ 」裕即答曰：「昔有作上黨潞長，遷為涿令者，去官還家，時人與書，欲署潞則失涿，欲署涿則失潞，乃署曰：『潞涿君』。 先主無須，故裕以此及之。 先主常銜莫不遜，加忿其漏言，乃顯裕諫爭漢中不驗，下獄，將誅之。 諸葛亮表請其罪，先主答曰：「勞蘭生門，不得不鋤。 」裕遂棄市。 後魏氏之立，先主之薨，皆如裕所刻。 又曉相術，每舉鏡視面，自知刑死，未常不撲之於地也。
Zhang Yu also whispered that in the gengzi year the mandate would turn and the house of Liu would be finished. Our lord had taken Yi Province; within nine years, between the yin and mao branches, he would lose it." An informer carried his words to the throne. Long ago, when the First Lord met Liu Zhang at Fu, Zhang Yu sat with Zhang's staff, heavily bearded. The First Lord teased him: "When I lived in Zhuo, every lane seemed full of Maos; the magistrate joked, The Maos wrap Zhuo on every side!" Yu shot back: "Once a man was magistrate of Lu in Shangdang and moved to Zhuo. Letters addressed to either place dropped the other title, so folk simply called him Lord Lu-Zhuo." The First Lord was beardless— Yu's barb hit its mark. The First Lord never forgave the slight, and Yu's loose talk sealed his fate: his failed warning about Hanzhong was trumped up as false prophecy and he was jailed for execution. Zhuge Liang pleaded for him, but the First Lord answered, "When weeds spring beside the orchid, you have to cut them down." Zhang Yu was executed in the marketplace. Later events— Wei's rise and the First Lord's death— unfolded on the schedule Zhang Yu had scratched out. He also read faces: every time he lifted a mirror he saw his own doom and always hurled it down.

羣卒，子臣頗傳其術。
After Qun died his son Zhou Chen inherited part of his craft.
11
杜瓊字伯瑜，蜀郡成都人也。 少受學於任安，精究安術。 劉璋時辟為從事，先主定益州，領牧，以瓊為議曹從事。 後主踐阼，拜諫議大夫，遷左中郎將、大鴻臚、太常。 為人靜默少言，闔門自守，不與世事。 蔣琬、費禕等皆器重之。 雖學業入深，初不視天文有所論說。 後近通儒譙周常問其意，瓊答曰：「欲明此術甚難，須當身視，識其形色，不可信人也。 晨夜苦劇，然後知之，復憂漏洩，不如不知，是以不復視也。 」周因問曰：「昔周徵君以為當塗高者魏也，其義何也？ 」瓊答曰：「魏，闕名也，當塗而高，聖人取類而言耳。 」又問周曰：「寧復有所怪邪？ 」周曰：「未達也。 」瓊又曰：「古者名官職不言曹，始自漢已來，名官盡言曹。 吏言屬曹，卒言侍曹，此殆天意也。」
Du Qiong, courtesy name Boyu, was a native of Chengdu in Shu commandery. He studied under Ren An in youth and mastered Ren's methods. Liu Zhang had appointed him adjutant; after the First Lord took Yi Province and the shepherd's seal, he made Qiong adjutant of the deliberation bureau. Under the Later Lord he rose from remonstrance grandee to general of the left in the palace, grand herald, and grand master of ceremonies. He was a man of few words who kept his doors shut and held aloof from public life. Jiang Wan, Fei Yi, and their circle held him in high regard. For all his learning he rarely lectured on astrology. Later Qiao Zhou pressed him on the subject. Qiong replied, "That art is hard to teach: you must watch the sky yourself and learn the shapes— you cannot take another's word." It means sleepless nights of observation, and even then you dread letting secrets slip— ignorance may be safer, so I no longer watch the heavens." Zhou pressed him: "Your father read High on the Road as Wei— what did he mean?" Qiong said, "Wei names the palace gate; standing high on the road— the sage was speaking in riddles." Zhou asked again whether anything else seemed odd. Zhou admitted he still did not see it. Qiong added, "Ancient offices were not called cao; since Han every bureau is a cao. Officials speak of their cao, soldiers of attending cao— that may be Heaven's own pun on the house of Cao."
12
瓊年八十餘，延熙十三年卒。 著《韓詩章句》十餘萬言，不教諸子，內學無傳業者。 周緣瓊言，乃觸類而長之曰：
He died in the thirteenth year of Yanxi, aged over eighty. He wrote over ten thousand characters of commentary on the Han version of the Odes but taught none of his sons; his esoteric learning died with him. Taking his cue from Du Qiong, Qiao Zhou pressed the same logic further:
13
《春秋傳》著秦穆候名太子曰仇，弟曰成師。 師服曰：『異哉君之名子也！ 嘉耦曰妃，怨耦曰仇，今君名太子曰仇，弟曰成師，始兆亂矣，兄其替乎？ 』其後果如服言。 及漢靈帝名二子曰史候、董候，既立為帝，後皆免為諸侯，與師服言相似也。 先主諱備，其訓具也，後主諱禪，其訓授也，如言劉已具矣，當授與人也； 意者甚於穆候、靈帝之名子。
The Zuo tradition tells how Duke Mu of Qin named his heir Chou and his younger son Chengshi. Shi Fu exclaimed, "What strange names the duke has chosen for his sons!" A loving match is fei; a hostile match is chou. To call the heir Chou and the younger brother Chengshi is to invite strife— will the elder lose his place?" Events proved Shi Fu right. Emperor Ling named two sons Marquis of Shi and Marquis of Dong; both briefly mounted the throne and were then reduced to common nobles— another echo of Shi Fu's warning. The First Lord's name Bei means "complete"; the Later Lord's name Shan means "to yield"— as if the house of Liu were finished and ready to pass the mandate on; a darker omen than Duke Mu's or Lingdi's choice of names.
14
後宦人黃皓弄權於內，景耀五年，宮中大樹無故自折，周深憂之，無所與言，乃書柱曰：「眾而大，期之會，具而授，若何復？ 」言曹者眾也，魏者大也，眾而大，天下其當會也，具而授，如何復有立者乎？ 蜀既亡，咸以周言為驗。 周曰：「此雖己所推尋，然有所因，由杜君之辭而廣之耳，殊無神思獨至之異也。」
Later, as Huang Hao meddled at court, a great tree in the palace snapped without wind in the fifth year of Jingyao. Zhou scratched a couplet on a pillar: "Many and great— the hour draws near; complete then yielded— what can rise again?" Cao means "host of officials"; wei means "great." Many and great— the realm will unite under Wei. Complete and yielded— how could Shu stand again?" After Shu fell, everyone called Zhou a prophet. Qiao Zhou said, "I only extended what Du Qiong had already hinted; there was no mystic flash of my own."
15
許慈字仁篤，南陽人也。 師事劉熙，善鄭氏學，治《易》、《尚書》、《三禮》、《毛詩》、《論語》。 建安中，與許靖等俱自交州入蜀。 時又有魏郡胡潛，字公興，不知其所以在益土。 潛雖學不沾洽，然卓犖強識，祖宗制度之儀，喪紀五服之數，皆指掌畫地，舉手可採。 先主定蜀，承喪亂歷紀，學業衰廢，乃鳩合典籍，沙汰眾學。 慈、潛並為學士，與孟光、來敏等典掌舊文。 值庶事草創，動多疑議，慈、潛更相剋伐，謗讟忿急，形於聲色； 書籍有無，不相通借，時尋楚撻，以相震攇。 〈攇，虛晚反。〉 其矜己妒彼，乃至於此。 先主愍其若斯，羣僚大會，使倡家假為二子之容，效其訟鬩之狀，酒酣樂作，以為嬉戲。 初以辭義相難，終以刀杖相屈，用感切之。 潛先沒，慈後主世稍遷至大長秋，卒。 〈孫盛曰：蜀少人士，故慈、潛等並見載述。〉 子勳傳其業，復為博士。
Xu Ci, courtesy name Rendu, came from Nanyang. He studied under Liu Xi, mastered the Zheng school tradition, and worked through the Changes, Documents, the three ritual texts, Mao's Poetry, and the Analects. During the Jianan years he crossed from Jiaozhou into Shu with Xu Jing and others. Hu Qian of Wei commandery, courtesy name Gongxing, had also turned up in Shu for reasons now unknown. Qian's scholarship was patchy, but his memory was prodigious: ancestral temple protocol, mourning grades, and five-garment rules— he could trace them in the dust from memory. After the First Lord conquered Shu, years of war had ruined scholarship; he gathered texts and culled the scholars. Xu Ci and Hu Qian became court erudites alongside Meng Guang and Lai Min, charged with curating the classics. In those rough early days every policy sparked quarrels; Ci and Qian sniped at each other with public insults and black looks; they hoarded books from each other and sometimes squared off with rods, trying to intimidate one another. The character xian is glossed with the reading xu-wan. Their vanity and mutual jealousy had sunk to such depths. The First Lord took pity on their feud. At a full court banquet he had actors mimic the two scholars, acting out their bickering for comic relief once the wine flowed. The skit began with verbal sparring and ended in a mock brawl with props— a pointed lesson in how ugly their rivalry had become. Hu Qian died first. Xu Ci survived into the Later Lord's reign, rose to grand prolonger of autumn, and died there. Sun Sheng notes that Shu produced few famous scholars, which is why Xu Ci and Hu Qian earned a place in the record. His son Xu Xun inherited his scholarship and again held the title of court erudite.
16
孟光字孝裕，河南洛陽人，漢太尉孟鬱之族。 〈《續漢書》曰：鬱，中常侍孟賁之弟。〉 靈帝末為講部吏。 獻帝遷都長安，遂逃人蜀，劉焉父子待以客禮。 博物識古，無書不覽，尤銳意三史，長於漢家舊典。 好《公羊春秋》而譏呵《左氏》，每與來敏爭此二義，光常譊譊讙咋。 〈譊音奴交反。 讙音休袁反。 咋音徂格反。〉 先主定益州，拜為儀郎，與許慈等並掌制度。 後主踐阼，為符節令、屯騎校尉、長樂少府，遷大司農。
Meng Guang, courtesy name Xiaoyu, came from Luoyang in Henan and belonged to the line of Han grand commandant Meng Yu. The Continued Han History identifies Meng Yu as the younger brother of the eunuch Meng Ben. Under Emperor Ling he served as a clerk in the lecture bureau. When the emperor moved the court west to Chang'an, Guang fled into Shu, where Liu Yan and Liu Zhang received him as an honored guest. He was encyclopedic, read everything, and took special interest in the three histories and Han institutional precedent. He favored the Gongyang commentary and scorned the Zuo; with Lai Min he argued the point endlessly, shouting himself hoarse. The character nao is glossed nu-jiao. The character huan is glossed xiu-yuan. The character zha is glossed zu-ge. After the First Lord took Yi Province he named Guang master of ceremonies alongside Xu Ci to codify court ritual. Under the Later Lord he rose from marshal of credentials and colonel of the agile cavalry to junior steward of Changle palace and finally grand minister of agriculture.
17
延熙九年秋，大赦，光於眾中責大將軍費禕曰：「夫赦者，偏枯之物，非明世所宜有也。 衰弊窮極，必不得已，然後乃可權而行之耳。 今主上仁賢，百僚稱職，有何旦夕之危，倒懸之急，而數施非常之恩，以惠姦宄之惡乎？ 又鷹隼始擊，而更原宥有罪，上犯天時，下違人理。 老夫耄朽，不達治體，窮謂其法難以經久，豈具瞻之高美，所望於明德哉。 」禕但顧謝踧踖而已。 光之指摘痛癢，多如是類。 故執政重臣，心不能悅，爵位不登，每直言無所迴避，為代所嫌。 太常廣漢鐔承、 〈《華陽國志》曰：承字公文，歷郡守少府。〉 光祿勛河東裴儁等，年資皆在光後，而登據上列，處光之右，蓋以此也。 〈傅暢《裴氏家記》曰：儁字奉先，魏尚書令潛弟也。 儁姊夫爲蜀中長史，儁送之，時年十餘歲，遂遭漢末大亂，不復得還。 既長知名，爲蜀所推重也。 子越，字令緒，爲蜀督軍。 蜀破，遷還洛陽，拜議郎。〉
When a general amnesty was proclaimed in the ninth year of Yanxi, Guang publicly scolded Fei Yi: "Mercy decrees are a crutch for failing states, not the tool of a well-ordered realm. They belong to ages of collapse, when there is truly no alternative— only then may you resort to them." Our sovereign is wise, his ministers capable— what sudden peril warrants showering criminals with repeated pardons?" Autumn is the season of the law, yet you spare felons— you offend both the calendar and common sense." I am an old fool out of touch with policy, yet I know this habit cannot last— is this what the world expects of enlightened rule?" Fei Yi could only bow and mumble apologies, squirming in his seat. Guang's blunt rebukes were typical of his manner. The great officers of state resented him, he rose no higher in rank, and his habit of blunt truth-telling made him widely disliked. Tan Cheng of Guanghan, grand master of ceremonies, the Huayang Guo zhi gives his courtesy name as Gongwen and lists him as a former commandery governor and junior steward. Minister of the household Pei Jun of Hedong and others were junior to Guang in age and service yet ranked above him— largely because Guang had alienated the powerful. The court teems with talent like fish in the sea or birds in the forest— one more voice neither crowds nor empties it. Sun and moon have their eclipses; Yu prayed at Yangxu and floods ended; Tang prayed at the mulberry grove and rain returned. The Huainanzi tells how Yu offered himself at Yangxu to stop floods and Tang at the mulberry grove to end drought— such was sage care for the people." The Lüshi Chunqiu records Tang's prayer after conquering Jie: "Blame me, not the people; if the people suffer, the fault is mine— do not let one dull ruler cost heaven its care for life." He cut his hair and nails and offered himself as a victim to heaven. The people rejoiced. The rains came in torrents. Action and rest follow the way; doors open and close in season. Confucius taught: blame neither heaven nor man; accept fate with humility— what answer need I add? When argument fails I return to the classics, follow Confucius' path in small phrases, envy the ease of Shu Xi and the flight of the hermits, hope to live content in a humble lane and escape this age's regrets— yet my heart is still uneasy, fearing the mire ahead, and I cry out this oath from my breast. Jiufang Gao read the spirit of horses; Qin Ya studied their bones; The Huainanzi: Duke Mu asked Bole for a successor to find horses. Bole replied, "A common good horse shows in bone and sinew. A horse of heaven seems absent yet outruns dust and ruts." My sons are middling; I can teach common steeds, not heaven's steeds. For heaven's horses use my cordwood-gatherer Jiufang Ying— his eye matches mine— send for him." Mu received him and sent him out; three months later he reported a horse at Sandy Hill. "What kind of horse?" Mu asked. "A yellow mare," he said. The groom brought back a black stallion. Mu was furious: "Your man is useless! He could not tell sex or color— how could he judge quality!" Bole sighed, "Has he truly reached that level! That is why he is worth ten thousand (leagues) of ordinary judges and still stands beyond count among them." Ying sees the spirit inside the beast and forgets hide and color— that is why he names heaven's horse." When the horse came, it was indeed the finest in the realm. The Huainanzi lists many judges— each used a different method yet grasped the same truth; Jiufang read the spirit, Qin Ya the body." Xue Zhu judged swords and won fame, the Yue jue shu tells how Goujian of Yue owned five famous blades. He summoned the connoisseur Xue Zhu to view them. He produced Haocao and Juque; Zhu said they were not the best. When shown Chunjun and Zhanlu he praised the starry edge, the watery gleam, the melting-ice grain— "This is Chunjun." The king agreed. The king offered three towns, a thousand horses, and two capitals of a thousand households for the sword. Zhu refused. He told how Ou Yezi forged them when heaven opened Mount Chijin for tin and dried Ruoye for copper, spirits sweeping and drumming while essence fell— making Chunjun and Zhanlu. Those mines are sealed, the smith is dead— no heap of gold or pearls can buy such a blade again. Your towns and horses are nothing beside it!" Hu Liang drew music from strings, the Huainanzi says Hu Ba's se charmed fish from the deep." It also says anyone may copy Hu Liang's tune, but no one can sing like Hu Liang." A Qi retainer slapped his thighs to mimic a cock and save his lord, Pei Songzhi notes this is the cock-crow guest who saved Lord Mengchang. Mimicking a cock begins with slapping the thigh like beating wings." A Chu thief slipped into camp to save the state, the Huainanzi tells how general Zi Fa of Chu loved odd talents. A thief offered his craft as a soldier's skill. Zi Fa rushed out half-dressed to honor him. His staff protested honoring a thief. He said, "You do not understand." Soon Qi invaded Chu. Zi Fa met them and fell back three times. Every Chu stratagem failed while Qi grew stronger. The thief stepped forward: "Let me use my small skill for you." The lord agreed. That night the thief slipped into the Qi camp, cut loose the general's tent hangings, and brought them to Zi Fa. Zi Fa sent them back with word that a foraging soldier had "found" the general's drapery and wished to return it politely. The next night he stole the pillow; again Zi Fa returned it. The third night he took the hairpin; again Zi Fa sent it back. The Qi army panicked; the council cried, "If we stay, Chu will have our heads!" They broke camp and fled." Yongmen Zhou plucked the zither and moved his hearer with argument, Huan Tan's New Discourses: Yongmen Zhou played for Lord Mengchang, who asked whether music could sadden him. Zhou listed the fallen noble, the ruined rich man, the recluse shunned by neighbors; the worthy slandered and disbelieved; lovers parted without quarrel across endless distance; the orphan with no kin, homeless between marsh and cave— such a man weeps at wind and birds; I have only to play and he floods with tears." But you, my lord, live in mansions hung with silk, cooled by breeze, with singers, sycophants, Chu melodies, Zheng dancers— every sense feasted; on water you race dragon boats under feather flags; on land you hunt the plain with crossbow and spear; you drink until heaven and earth seem smaller than your thumb— what player could sadden you?" Mengchang agreed. Zhou answered, "Yet I grieve for you nonetheless." You humbled Qin and led the league against Chu— the world never rests: vertical alliance or horizontal, one makes Chu king, the other makes Qin emperor." Against Qin or Chu, little Xue is a mushroom under an axe— every thinking man shudders for you. Heaven turns; after ages your line may no longer smell the smoke of sacrifice; your towers will fall, your pools fill in, thorns choke the tombs, and herd boys will sing over your bones, "So passed Lord Mengchang's glory!" Mengchang wept until tears hung on his lashes. Zhou played a slow, wrenching tune until Mengchang sobbed, "Your music has made me a man of a fallen kingdom." Han Ai mastered the chariot and won renown; the Lüshi Chunqiu credits Han Ai with the art of driving. Wang Bao's rhapsody praises Wang Liang and Han Ai: at dawn, whip in hand, they drive so fast they outrun wind and lightning across the realm— man and steed as one." So runs the praise of their perfect partnership." Lu Ao roamed the dark void; the transcendent leapt into the clouds; the Huainanzi tells how Lu Ao met a strange being in the far north who hid from him. Lu Ao peeked and saw him munching a pear on a tortoise shell. Lu boasted of his lonely travels beyond the world. He said he had seen every pole but the north and now begged friendship. The figure gave a cricket's laugh: "Ha!" "You are a townsman of the heartland— what brings you here?" "This place still has sun, moon, and seasons— beside the nameless void it is only a chimney nook." "I roam voids beyond north, south, east, and west where there is neither sky nor ground— vaster than you can leap in a thousand leagues— and I have not reached their limit." "You have barely started yet call yourself a traveler— presumptuous!" "Stay if you like; I am due to meet Hanman above the nine heavens— I cannot linger." He stretched and rose into the clouds. Lu Ao cried, "Beside him I am a mayfly beside a swan— pathetic!" I cannot match those masters; I keep still and find peace in myself."

景耀六年，後主從譙周之計，遣使請降於鄧艾。 其書，正所造也。 明年正月，鐘會作亂成都，後主東遷洛陽，時擾攘倉卒。 蜀之大臣無冀從者，惟正及殿中督汝南張通，捨妻子單身隨侍。 後主賴正相導宜適，舉動無闕，乃慨然歎息，恨知正之晚，時論嘉之。 賜爵關內候。 泰始中，除安陽令，遷巴西太守。 泰始八年詔曰：「正昔在成都，顛沛守義，不違忠節，及見受用，盡心幹事，有治理之績，其以正為巴西太守。 」咸寧四年卒。 凡所著述詩論賦之屬，垂百篇。
In Jingyao 6 the Later Lord adopted Qiao Zhou's counsel and sent Deng Ai terms of surrender. Xi Zheng had drafted that letter. The next New Year Zhong Hui mutinied at Chengdu; the court moved east to Luoyang in chaos. No great officer chose exile with the sovereign except Xi Zheng and Zhang Tong, who abandoned their families to go. The Later Lord leaned on Zheng for deportment at the Wei court and sighed that he had known him too late; contemporaries praised them both. He was enfeoffed marquis within the passes. Under the Jin he became magistrate of Anyang, then prefect of Ba. A Taishi 8 edict cited his loyalty in Shu and his good work as prefect of Ba. He died in the fourth year of Xianning. His writings— verse, essays, rhapsodies— number nearly a hundred.

Du Wei lived in seclusion like the ancient recluses. Zhou Qun read the sky truly; Du Qiong was reserved and exact— models of scholarly integrity. Xu Ci, Meng Guang, Lai Min, and Li were encyclopedic; Yin Mo mastered the Zuo commentary— none were famed as moral paragons, yet all were leading scholars.

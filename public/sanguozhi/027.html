徐邈字景山，燕國薊人也。 太祖平河朔，召爲丞相軍謀掾，試守奉高令，入爲東曹議令史。 魏國初建，爲尚書郎。 時科禁酒，而邈私飲至於沈醉。 校事趙達問以曹事，邈曰：「中聖人。」 達白之太祖，太祖甚怒。 度遼將軍鮮于輔進曰：「平日醉客謂酒清者爲聖人，濁者爲賢人，邈性脩慎，偶醉言耳。」 竟坐得免刑。 後領隴西太守，轉爲南安。 文帝踐阼，歷譙相，平陽、安平太守，潁川典農中郎將，所在著稱，賜爵關內侯。 車駕幸許昌，問邈曰：「頗復中聖人不？」 邈對曰：「昔子反斃於穀陽，御叔罰於飲酒，臣嗜同二子，不能自懲，時復中之。 然宿瘤以醜見傳，而臣以醉見識。」 帝大笑，顧左右曰：「名不虛立。」 遷撫軍大將軍軍師。
Xu Miao, courtesy Jingshan, hailed from Ji in the Yan princedom. After Cao Cao subdued Hebei he enlisted Xu Miao as army strategist, tried him as acting magistrate of Fenggao, then appointed eastern bureau clerk. When Wei was founded Xu Miao joined the masters of writing. Although alcohol was banned at the time, Xu Miao drank secretly until he was dead drunk. When the investigator Zhao Da asked him about office business, Xu Miao answered, 'I have hit the sage.' Zhao Da reported this to Cao Cao, who was furious. Xianyu Fu, General Who Measures the Liao, came forward and said, 'Among habitual drinkers, clear liquor is called "sage" and cloudy liquor "worthy." Xu Miao is by nature disciplined and cautious; this was only a drunken remark.' In the end he was spared punishment. Later he served as administrator of Longxi and was transferred to Nan'an. After Emperor Wen took the throne, Xu Miao served successively as chancellor of Qiao, administrator of Pingyang and Anping, and Household General Who Directs Agriculture in Yingchuan. Wherever he served he gained renown, and he was granted the rank of marquis within the passes. When the imperial carriage visited Xuchang [the emperor] asked Miao 'Have you hit middle sage again?' Miao replied 'Formerly Zifan died at Guyang Yushu was punished for drinking—I crave drink like those two and cannot restrain myself—I sometimes hit again. Ugly Suiliu became legend yet Xu Miao claimed fame only as a drunk." The emperor laughed greatly looked at left and right saying 'The name was not vainly established.' He rose to strategist under the Pacifies-the-Army general.

Emperor Cao Rui made Xu Miao Liangzhou inspector—remote frontier facing Shu—with staff and Qiang-colonel rank. He arrived as Zhuge Liang marched from Qishan and Longyou rose; Xu Miao threw aides and the Jincheng governor against Nan'an rebels and broke them. Longzhou was dry so Xu Miao rebuilt Wuwei and Jiuquan salt works to trade grain for nomad barley opened paddies for poor tenants until bins overflowed. He gauged surplus grain bought silk horses and gold for the capital. He slowly confiscated illegal weapons for the armory. Then he taught ritual ran schools banned wasteful funerals and cults rewarded virtue—the customs turned and the people rallied. Trade flowed west tribute arrived—all Xu Miao's doing. He crushed rebel Qiang Kewu earned village marquis of the capital district three hundred households and rank Establishes Might general. With tribes he overlooked petty slips; major crimes went first to tribal heads—executions came only after public notice so tribes feared and obeyed. He gave every bonus to the troops—his family went short; The emperor heard and funded his household. He purged corruption until Liangzhou ran clean.

Han Guan, styled Manyou, from Xu Miao's commandery, had discernment and administrative capacity. His reputation equaled Xu Miao's, and he ranked ahead of Sun Li and Lu Yu. As inspector of Yu Province he achieved notable order and died in office. 〈Memorials of Famous Wei Ministers preserves a memorial by Du Shu, Gentleman of the Yellow Gates, which says, 'Han Guan and Wang Chang truly possess comprehensive talent. High office and weighty responsibility should be theirs, not merely three provinces.'〉 Lu Qin wrote of Xu Miao, 'Lord Xu had lofty aims, pure conduct, broad talent, and fierce energy. In practice he was lofty without being narrow, pure without being brittle, learned yet disciplined, fierce yet capable of lenience. The sages regard purity as difficult, yet Lord Xu found it easy." Someone asked Lu Qin, 'In Emperor Wu's time people considered Lord Xu accommodating, but after he was in Liangzhou and returned to the capital, people considered him rigid. Why?' Lu Qin answered: "Formerly, when Mao Jie and Cui Yan held office, they prized pure and plain gentlemen. At that time everyone changed carriages and clothing to seek a lofty reputation, but Lord Xu did not alter his usual ways, so people thought him accommodating. Recently the world has grown extravagant and people imitate one another, while Lord Xu's refined principles remain unchanged and he does not follow common fashion. Thus yesterday's accommodation has become today's rigidity. The world changed Xu Miao did not."

Deep, steady, and inwardly perceptive, he never judged others by his own standards and was missed wherever he served. He died in Jiaping 2 (250) leaving only court robes and a book chest. The army supervisor reported his death; he was posthumously made Marquis of Yangling village, fief of one hundred households, styled Marquis Zhen. His son Hu Wei succeeded. In the sixth year an edict praised Hu Zhi’s integrity and granted his household money and grain. The account appears in the biography of Xu Miao.〉 In Xianxi, Hu Wei rose to inspector of Xu Province. The Jin Yang Autumn says he was styled Bohu. From youth he aimed high and cultivated spotless conduct. While Hu Zhi governed Jingzhou, Hu Wei traveled from the capital to visit him. Poor and without carriage or servants, Wei drove a donkey alone to see his father. He stayed in the stable over ten days, then asked leave to return home. On parting Hu Zhi gave him one bolt of silk for the road. Wei knelt and asked, "You live so plainly—where did this silk come from?" Hu Zhi answered, "It is what remains of my salary, set aside for your journey." Wei accepted it and went home. At every inn he unsaddled the donkey, gathered firewood, cooked, ate, and rejoined the road—so he traveled both ways. A commandant in Hu Zhi’s staff, unknown to Wei, took leave, traveled ahead over a hundred li, met him on the road, and for hundreds of li helped with supplies and food. Wei grew suspicious, questioned him quietly, learned he was the commandant, returned the silk in thanks, and sent him away. Later he reported the whole affair to Hu Zhi in another letter. Hu Zhi had the commandant beaten one hundred strokes and removed him from the rolls. Father and son were scrupulous to this degree. His reputation spread, and he served in successive governorships. Emperor Wu of Jin received him, discussed frontier affairs, and spoke of their lives. The emperor praised Hu Zhi’s integrity and asked Wei, "Who is purer—you or your father?" Wei answered, "I am not his equal." The emperor asked, "How are you not his equal?" He said, "My father feared people would learn of his integrity; I fear they will not—that is how far I fall short." 景元二年，襄陽太守表吳賊鄧由等欲來歸化，基被詔，當因此震蕩江表。 基疑其詐，馳驛陳狀。 且曰：「嘉平以來，累有內難，當今之務，在於鎮安社稷，綏寧百姓，未宜動衆以求外利。」 文王報書曰：「凡處事者，多曲相從順，鮮能確然共盡理實。 誠感忠愛，每見規示，輙敬依來指。」 後由等竟不降。 〈司馬彪《戰略》載基此事，詳於本傳。 曰：「景元二年春三月，襄陽太守胡烈表上『吳賊鄧由、李光等，同謀十八屯，欲來歸化，遣將張吳、鄧生，并送質任。 克期欲令郡軍臨江迎拔』。 大將軍司馬文王啟聞。 詔征南將軍王基部分諸軍，使烈督萬人徑造沮水，荊州、義陽南屯宜城，承書夙發。 若由等如期到者，便當因此震蕩江表。 基疑賊詐降，誘致官兵，馳驛止文王，說由等可疑之狀。 『且當清澄，未宜便舉重兵深入應之』。 又曰：『夷陵東道，當由車御，至赤岸乃得渡坦，西道當出箭谿口，乃趣平土，皆山險狹，竹木叢蔚，卒有要害，弩馬不陳。 今者筋角弩弱，水潦方降，廢盛農之務，徼難必之利，此事之危者也。 昔子午之役，兵行數百里而值霖雨，橋閣破壞，後糧腐敗，前軍縣乏。 姜維深入，不待輜重，士衆飢餓，覆軍上邽。 文欽、唐咨，舉吳重兵，昧利壽春，身歿不反。 此皆近事之鑒戒也。 嘉平以來，累有內難。 當今之宜，當鎮安社稷，撫寧上下，力農務本，懷柔百姓，未宜動衆以求外利也。 得之未足爲多，失之傷損威重。』 文王累得基書，意疑。 尋勑諸軍已上道者，且權停住所在，須後節度。 基又言於文王曰：『昔漢祖納酈生之說，欲封六國，寤張良之謀，而趣銷印。 基謀慮淺短，誠不及留侯，亦懼襄陽有食其之謬。』 文王於是遂罷軍嚴，後由等果不降。」〉
In Jingyuan 2, the Grand Administrator of Xiangyang reported that the Wu men Deng You and others wished to defect. Wang Ji was ordered to use the opening to unsettle the south. Wang Ji suspected a trick and sent an urgent report explaining the situation. He further said, "Since the Jiaping era there have repeatedly been internal troubles. The task now is to secure the state and pacify the people; it is not yet proper to mobilize the masses in pursuit of gains abroad." Prince Wen replied, "In handling affairs, most men bend and go along; few can stand firm and fully examine principle and fact. I am truly moved by your loyal concern, and whenever I receive your corrections and advice, I respectfully follow your guidance." In the end, Deng You and the others did not defect. 〈Sima Biao's Zhanlüe records this affair of Wang Ji in more detail than the main biography. It says, "In the spring, third month, of Jingyuan 2, Xiangyang Grand Administrator Hu Lie memorialized, 'The Wu men Deng You, Li Guang, and others have joined eighteen camps in a plot to defect. They have sent the generals Zhang Wu and Deng Sheng, along with hostages. They have fixed a date for the commandery army to come to the river and bring them across.' Grand General Sima Zhao reported this to the throne. An edict ordered General Who Campaigns South Wang Ji to deploy the armies, sent Hu Lie with ten thousand men straight to the Ju River, and had the southern garrisons of Jing Province and Yiyang camp at Yicheng; when the letter arrived, they set out at dawn. If Deng You and the others arrived as promised, this could be used to shake the south. Wang Ji suspected the enemy was feigning surrender to lure in government troops, and sent an express message asking Prince Wen to stop, explaining why Deng You and the others were suspicious. 'Moreover, we should first clarify the situation; it is not fitting to send a large force deep in response.' He also said, 'East of Yiling the road follows a cart track, and only at Chian can one cross onto level ground; westward, one must come out at Jianxi mouth before reaching open land. Both routes are steep, narrow, and choked with bamboo and trees. If a pass is suddenly blocked, crossbows and cavalry cannot deploy. Now our composite bows are weakened, the rains and floods are arriving, and we would abandon the work of peak farming season to chase an uncertain gain through hardship. This is the danger in the affair. In the former Ziwu campaign, the army marched several hundred li into steady rain; bridges and galleries collapsed, rear grain rotted, and the vanguard was left in want. Jiang Wei pushed deep without waiting for his baggage train; his soldiers went hungry, and his army was ruined at Shanggui. Wen Qin and Tang Zi brought Wu's main forces to Shouchun, were blinded by gain, and died without returning. All these recent affairs are warnings. Since the Jiaping era, internal troubles have come one after another. What is appropriate now is to stabilize the altars of soil and grain, soothe those above and below, strengthen agriculture and attend to the root, and cherish and pacify the people; it is not yet appropriate to move the masses in pursuit of external gain. Success would not amount to much; failure would damage our authority and prestige.' After receiving repeated letters from Wang Ji, Prince Wen grew doubtful. Soon he ordered the armies already on the road to halt in place and await further instructions. Wang Ji again told Prince Wen, 'Formerly the Han Founder accepted Li Yiji's proposal to enfeoff the Six Kingdoms; when Zhang Liang made him see the danger, he hurried to melt down the seals. My plans are shallow and short-sighted and truly do not measure up to the Marquis of Liu, but I too fear that Xiangyang has made Li Yiji's mistake.' Prince Wen then called off the army's readiness, and afterward Deng You and the others indeed did not defect."〉

是歲基薨，追贈司空，謚曰景侯。 子徽嗣，早卒。 咸熈中，開建五等，以基著勳前朝，改封基孫廙，而以東武餘邑賜一子爵關內侯。 晉室踐阼，下詔曰：「故司空王基旣著德立勳，又治身清素，不營產業，乆在重任，家無私積，可謂身沒行顯，足用勵俗者也。 其以奴婢二人賜其家。」
Ji died that year and was posthumously made Minister of Works, styled Marquis Jing. His son Hui succeeded but died young. During Xianxi, when the five noble ranks were instituted, Ji’s grandson Yi was enfeoffed for his grandfather’s service, and one son received Dongwu’s remaining fief as a secondary marquis. When the Jin took the throne, an edict declared, "The late Minister of Works Wang Ji won merit and lived plainly, held high office for years without private wealth—conduct that outlived him and could edify the age. Grant his household two servants."

The Book of Jin (Jinshu, 晉書) contains a detailed biography of Chen Shou, who authored the Records of the Three Kingdoms (Sanguo Zhi, 三國志). He was said to love books from a young age, becoming a clerk in the imperial library of Shu-Han. His early career was mired in controversy. When the eunuch Huang Hao dominated the Shu-Han court, Chen Shou refused to ingratiate himself with him, and was repeatedly demoted and censured. Later, when Chen Shou's father died, he himself fell ill, and he violated ritual by having a servant girl prepare his medicine. Others found out about this, and Chen Shou was disgraced. His reputation would not recover for several years, even outliving the Shu-Han dynasty, which was invaded and toppled by Wei dynasty forces.

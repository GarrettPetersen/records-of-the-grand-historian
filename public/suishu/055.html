杜彥杜彥，雲中人也。 父遷，屬葛榮之亂，徙家於幽。 彥性勇果，善騎射。 仕周，釋褐左侍上士，後從柱國陸通擊陳將吳明徹於土州，破之。 又擊叛蠻，克倉塠、白楊二柵，並斬其渠帥。 進平郢州賊帥樊志，以戰功拜大都督。 尋遷儀同，治隆山郡事。 明年，拜隴州刺史，賜爵永安縣伯。 高祖為丞相，從韋孝寬擊尉迥於相州，每戰有功，賜物三千段，奴婢三十口。 進位上開府，改封襄武縣侯，拜魏郡太守。 開皇初，授丹州刺史，進爵為公。 後六歲，征為左武衛將軍。 平陳之役，以行軍總管與新義公韓擒相繼而進。 軍至南陵，賊屯據江岸，彥遣儀同樊子蓋率精兵擊破其柵，獲船六百餘艘。 渡江，擊南陵城，拔之，擒其守將許翼。 進至新林，與擒合軍。 及陳平，賜物五千段，粟六千石，進位柱國，賜子寶安爵昌陽縣公。 高智慧等之作亂也，複以行軍總管從楊素討之，別解江州圍。 智慧餘黨往往屯聚，保投溪洞，彥水陸兼進，攻錦山、陽父、若、石壁四洞，悉平之，皆斬其渠帥。 賊李陀擁眾數千，據彭山，彥襲擊破之，斬陀，傳其首。 又擊徐州、宜豐二洞，悉平之。 賜奴婢百餘口。 拜洪州總管，甚有治名。
Du Yan was from Yunzhong. His father Qian had moved the family to You during the turmoil of Ge Rong's rebellion. Yan was by nature courageous and decisive, and excelled at horsemanship and archery. He entered Zhou service and received his first appointment as Left Attendant Upper Gentleman; later he followed the pillar of state Lu Tong in an attack on the Chen general Wu Mingche at Tuzhou and routed him. He also campaigned against rebellious tribes, taking the stockades at Cangchi and Baiyang and beheading their leaders. In a further advance he subdued the bandit chief Fan Zhi in Yingzhou and, for his military achievements, was made a grand commander. He was soon promoted to yitong and put in charge of Longshan commandery. The following year he was appointed inspector of Longzhou and ennobled as Baron of Yong'an. When Emperor Gaozu was still grand chancellor, Yan followed Wei Xiaokuan against Yuwen Jiong at Xiangzhou; he distinguished himself in every engagement and was rewarded with three thousand bolts of silk and thirty household slaves. He was raised to upper director of departments, created Marquis of Xiangwu, and appointed administrator of Wei commandery. At the start of the Kaihuang era he was made inspector of Danzhou and advanced to ducal rank. Six years later he was recalled to serve as general of the Left Martial Guards. During the conquest of Chen he served as campaign commander, advancing in turn with Han Qin, Duke of Xinyi. When the army reached Nanling, enemy forces held the riverbank. Yan sent Fan Zigai, a yitong officer, with picked troops to break their stockade and seized more than six hundred vessels. He crossed the Yangzi, stormed Nanling, took the city, and captured its commandant, Xu Yi. He advanced to Xinlin and united his forces with Han Qin's. After Chen was subdued he received five thousand bolts of goods and six thousand shi of grain, was promoted to pillar of state, and his son Bao'an was ennobled as Duke of Changyang. When Gao Zhihui and others rebelled, he again served as campaign commander under Yang Su and on his own broke the siege of Jiangzhou. Zhihui's scattered followers often gathered in mountain ravines and cave strongholds. Yan advanced by land and river alike, stormed the four strongholds of Jinshan, Yangfu, Ruo, and Shibi, reduced them all, and executed their leaders. The rebel Li Tuo rallied several thousand men and held Pengshan. Yan surprised and routed him, killed Tuo, and sent his head to the capital. He also subdued the strongholds at Xuzhou and Yifeng. He was rewarded with more than a hundred slaves. Appointed governor-general of Hongzhou, he won a strong reputation for effective administration.
2
歲餘，雲州總管賀婁子幹卒，上悼惜者久之，因謂侍臣曰：「榆林國之重鎮，安得子幹之輩乎？ 」後數日，上曰：「吾思可以鎮榆林者，莫過杜彥。 」於是征拜雲州總管。 突厥來寇，彥輒擒斬之，北夷畏憚，胡馬不敢至塞。 後數年，朝廷複追錄前功，賜子寶虔爵承縣公。 十八年，遼東之役，以行軍總管從漢王至營州。 上以彥曉習軍旅，令總統五十營事。 及還，拜朔州總管。 突厥複寇雲州，上令楊素擊走之，是後猶恐為邊患，以彥素為突厥所憚，複拜雲州總管。 未幾，以疾征還，卒，時年六十。 子寶虔，大業末，文城郡丞。 高勱高勱，字敬德，渤海蓚人也，齊太尉、清河王嶽之子也。 幼聰敏，美風儀，以仁孝聞，為齊顯祖所愛。 年七歲，襲爵清河王。 十四為青州刺史，曆右衛將軍、領軍大將軍、祠部尚書、開府儀同三司，改封樂安王。 性剛直，有才幹，甚為時人所重。 斛律明月雅敬之，每有征伐，則引之為副。 遷侍中、尚書右僕射。 及後主為周師所敗，勱奉太后歸鄴。 時宦官放縱，儀同苟子溢尤稱寵倖，勱將斬之以徇。 太后救之，乃釋。 劉文殊竊謂勱曰：「子溢之徒，言成禍福，何得如此！ 」勱攘袂曰：「今者西寇日侵，朝貴多叛，正由此輩弄權，致使衣冠解體。 若得今日殺之，明日受誅，無所恨也。 」文殊甚愧。 既至鄴，勱勸後主：「五品已上家累，悉置三台之上，因脅之曰：'若戰不捷，則燒之。 '此輩惜妻子，必當死戰，可敗也。 」後主不從，遂棄鄴東遁。 勱恆後殿，為周軍所得。 武帝見之，與語，大悅，因問齊亡所由。 勱發言流涕，悲不自勝，帝亦為之改容。 授開府儀同三司。
A little over a year later the governor-general of Yunzhou, Helou Zigan, died. The emperor mourned him at length and said to his attendants, "Yulin is a strategic linchpin of the realm—where shall we find another man like Zigan?" A few days later he said, "Of all those fit to secure Yulin, none surpasses Du Yan." Yan was then recalled and appointed governor-general of Yunzhou. Whenever the Turks raided, Yan captured and executed the raiders. The northern tribes came to fear him, and Turkic horsemen no longer dared approach the frontier. Some years later the court again registered his past service, and his son Baoqian was ennobled as Duke of Cheng. In the eighteenth year of Kaihuang, during the Liaodong campaign, he served as campaign commander and followed the Prince of Han as far as Yingzhou. Knowing Yan's mastery of military affairs, the emperor put him in overall charge of fifty encampments. On his return he was made governor-general of Shuozhou. When the Turks raided Yunzhou again, the emperor sent Yang Su to repel them. He still feared further border trouble, and because the Turks had long dreaded Yan, Yan was again appointed governor-general of Yunzhou. Not long afterward he was recalled because of illness and died, aged sixty. His son Baoqian, at the close of the Daye era, was assistant administrator of Wencheng commandery. Gao Mai, courtesy name Jingde, was from Su in Bohai—the son of Gao Yue, grand commandant of Qi and Prince of Qinghe. As a boy he was quick-witted and handsome, famed for his filial devotion, and a favorite of Emperor Xianzu of Qi. At seven he succeeded to the title Prince of Qinghe. At fourteen he was made inspector of Qingzhou, and went on to hold the posts of general of the Right Guards, commander-in-chief of the palace guards, minister of temple affairs, and director of departments of the third rank of yitong; he was then re-created Prince of Lean. He was upright by nature, capable and forceful, and widely respected. Hulu Guang held him in the highest regard and routinely took him as his second-in-command on campaign. He was promoted to attendant-in-ordinary and right vice-minister of the Masters of Writing. After the later sovereign was routed by Zhou forces, Mai escorted the empress dowager back to Ye. Eunuchs ran rampant at court; the yitong Gou Ziyi was said above all to enjoy imperial favor, and Mai was on the point of executing him as an example. The empress dowager intervened and Mai spared him. Liu Wenshu whispered to him, "Men like Ziyi can make or break fortunes with a word—how can you go so far!" Mai flung up his sleeves and said, "The western enemy presses closer every day and so many of the great families have gone over to the enemy. It is precisely because men like these abuse power that the ruling elite has come apart." If I can kill him today and die for it tomorrow, I shall have no regrets. Wenshu was deeply ashamed. Once they had reached Ye, Mai urged the sovereign, "Place the families of all officials of the fifth rank and above on the Three Terraces and threaten them: if we do not win the battle, we shall burn them." They cherish their wives and children and will fight to the death—we can defeat the enemy that way." The sovereign refused, abandoned Ye, and fled east. Mai covered the rear and was captured by Zhou troops. Emperor Wu of Zhou received him, spoke with him, and was greatly pleased; he then asked why Qi had fallen. Mai spoke through tears, overcome with grief, and the emperor's countenance softened in response. He was made director of departments of the third rank of yitong.

高祖為丞相，謂勱曰：「齊所以亡者，由任邪佞。 公父子忠良，聞於鄰境，宜善自愛。 」勱再拜謝曰：「勱亡齊末屬，世荷恩榮，不能扶危定傾，以致淪覆。 既蒙獲宥，恩幸已多，況複濫叨名位，致速官謗。 」高祖甚器之，以勱檢校揚州事。 後拜楚州刺史，民安之。 先是，城北有伍子胥廟，其俗敬鬼。 祈禱者必以牛酒，至破產業。 勱歎曰：「子胥賢者，豈宜損百姓乎？ 」乃告諭所部，自此遂止，百姓賴之。
When Emperor Gaozu was grand chancellor, he told Mai, "Qi fell because it entrusted wicked flatterers." Your father and you have been known far and wide for loyalty and integrity; look well to yourselves." Mai bowed twice and replied, "I am a survivor of fallen Qi, one whose house has long enjoyed imperial favor; I failed to shore up a tottering state, and thus brought ruin upon it." I have already received far more mercy than I deserve; how much more shameful would it be to accept rank and title I do not merit and invite censure from the officials." The emperor was deeply impressed and put him in charge of Yangzhou as acting administrator. He was later appointed inspector of Chuzhou, where the people lived in peace under his rule. North of the city stood a shrine to Wu Zixu, and the local custom was to lavish offerings on spirits. Worshippers had to bring cattle and wine, often until their households were ruined. Mai sighed and said, "Wu Zixu was a worthy man—would he truly want the people harmed in his name?" He issued orders throughout his district, and the practice stopped; the people were greatly relieved.
4
七年，轉光州刺史，上取陳五策，又上表曰：「臣聞夷凶翦暴，王者之懋功； 取亂侮亡，往賢之雅誥。 是以苗民逆命，爰興兩階之舞； 有扈不賓，終召六師之伐。 皆所以寧一宇內，匡濟群生者也。 自昔晉氏失馭，天網絕維，群凶於焉蝟起，三方因而鼎立。 陳氏乘其際運，拔起細微，茜頊縱其長蛇，竊據吳會； 叔寶肆其昏虐，毒被金陵。 數年已來，荒悖滋甚。 牝雞司旦，昵近奸回，尚方役徒，積骸千數，疆埸防守，長戍三年。 或微行暴露，沉湎王侯之宅； 或賓士駿騎，顛墜康衢之首。 有功不賞，無辜獲戮，烽燧日警，未以為虞，耽淫靡嫚，不知紀極。 天厭亂德，妖實人興，或空裡時有大聲，或行路共傳鬼怪，或刳人肝以祠天狗，或自捨身以厭妖訛。 民神怨憤，災異薦發，天時人事，昭然可知。 臣以庸才，猥蒙朝寄，頻曆籓任，與其鄰接，密邇仇讎，知其動靜，天討有罪，此即其時。 若戎車雷動，戈船電邁，臣難驚怯，請效鷹犬。 」高祖覽表嘉之，答以優詔。 及大舉伐陳，以勱為行軍總管，從宜陽公王世積下陳江州。 以功拜上開府，賜物三千段。
In the seventh year he was transferred to inspector of Guangzhou. He submitted five plans against Chen and added a memorial: "I have heard that subduing the barbarous and cutting down the violent is the crowning achievement of a true king;" and that seizing upon disorder to strike the doomed is the ancient lesson of the sages." When the Miao defied the mandate, the dance of the two staircases was performed; when Youhu refused allegiance, the six hosts were finally called out to chastise them. All to secure peace under heaven and succor the people. Ever since the Jin house lost the mandate, the bonds of rule were broken, rebels swarmed like hedgehogs, and the realm split into three contending powers. The house of Chen seized its moment, rose from humble origins, and Chen Baxian, like a coiled serpent, seized Wu and the lower Yangzi; while Chen Shubao has grown ever more cruel and debauched, spreading misery through Jinling. In recent years his excesses have only worsened. Women rule at dawn; he dotes on wicked minions; corpses of conscripts from the imperial workshops lie in heaps by the thousands; frontier garrisons serve three-year tours without relief. He goes abroad in disguise and debauches himself in the houses of princes; or races fine horses and is thrown headlong in the public streets. Merit goes unrewarded and the innocent are killed; beacon-fires rise daily yet he feels no alarm; he wallows in debauchery without limit. Heaven has grown weary of his conduct and omens multiply—strange cries in the air, demons reported on the roads, human livers cut out to appease the celestial dog, bodies offered to ward off evil signs. The people and the spirits alike resent him; disasters multiply; the signs in heaven and on earth are plain for all to see. I am a man of little talent, yet the court has repeatedly entrusted me with border posts adjoining Chen; I know their movements well. Heaven's judgment falls on the guilty—now is the time. When the war-chariots roll like thunder and the war-fleet strikes like lightning, though I am no bold warrior, I beg to serve as your hound." The emperor read the memorial with approval and answered with a commendatory decree. When the great invasion of Chen was launched, Mai served as campaign commander under Wang Shiji, Duke of Yiyang, descending on Jiangzhou. For his achievements he was made upper director of departments and given three thousand bolts of goods.

隴右諸羌數為寇亂，朝廷以勱有威名，拜洮州刺史。 下車大崇威惠，民夷悅附，其山谷間生羌相率詣府稱謁，前後至者，數千餘戶。 豪猾屏跡，路不拾遺，在職數年，稱為治理。 後遇吐谷渾來寇，勱遇疾不能拒戰，賊遂大掠而去。 憲司奏勱亡失戶口，又言受羌饋遺，竟坐免官。 後卒于家，時年五十六。 子士廉，最知名。 爾朱敞爾硃敞，字乾羅，秀容契胡人，爾硃榮之族子也。 父彥伯，官至司徒、博陵王。 齊神武帝韓陵之捷，盡誅爾硃氏，敞小，隨母養于宮中。 及年十二，自竇而走，至於大街，見童兒群戲者，敞解所著綺羅金翠之服，易衣而遁。 追騎尋至，初不識敞，便執綺衣兒。 比究問知非，會日已暮，由是得免。 遂入一村，見長孫氏媼踞胡床而坐。 敞再拜求哀，長孫氏湣之，藏於複壁。 三年，購之愈急，跡且至，長孫氏曰：「事急矣，不可久留。 」資而遣之。 遂詐為道士，變姓名，隱嵩山，略涉經史。 數年之間，人頗異之。 嘗獨坐岩石之下，泫然而歎曰：「吾豈終於此乎？ 伍子胥獨何人也！ 」於是間行微服，西歸於周。 太祖見而禮之，拜大都督、行台郎中，封靈壽縣伯，邑千五百戶。 遷通直散騎常侍，轉車騎大將軍、儀同三司，進爵為侯。 保定中，遷使持節、驃騎大將軍、開府儀同三司。 天和中，增邑五百戶，曆信、臨、熊、潼四州刺史，進爵為公。 武帝東征，上表求從，許之。 攻城陷陣，所當皆破，進位上開府。 除南光州刺史，入為護軍大將軍。 歲餘，轉膠州刺史。 於是迎長孫氏及弟置於家，厚資給之。 高祖受禪，改封邊城郡公。 黔安蠻叛，命敞討平之。 師旋，拜金州總管。 尋轉徐州總管。 在職數年，號為明肅，民吏懼之。 後以年老，上表乞骸骨，賜二馬軺車，歸於河內，卒于家，時年七十二。 子最嗣。 周搖周搖，字世安，其先與後魏同源，初為普乃氏，及居洛陽，改為周氏。 曾祖拔拔，祖右六肱，俱為北平王。 父恕延，曆行台僕射、南荊州總管。 搖少剛果，有武藝，性謹厚，動遵法度。 仕魏，官至開府儀同三司。 周閔帝受禪，賜姓車非氏，封金水郡公。 曆夙、楚二州刺史，吏民安之。 從帝平齊，每戰有功，超授柱國，進封夔國公。 未幾，拜晉州總管。 時高祖為定州總管，文獻皇后自京師詣高祖，路經搖所，主禮甚薄。 既而白後曰：「公廨甚富於財，限法不敢輒費。 又王臣無得效私。 」其質直如此。 高祖以其奉法，每嘉之。 及為丞相，徙封濟北郡公，尋拜豫州總管。 高祖受禪，複姓周氏。 開皇初，突厥寇邊，燕、薊多被其患，前總管李崇為虜所殺，上思所以鎮之，臨朝曰：「無以加周搖者。 」拜為幽州總管六州五十鎮諸軍事。 搖修鄣塞，謹斥候，邊民以安。 後六載，徙為壽州。 初，自以年老，乞骸骨，上召之。 既引見，上勞之曰：「公積行累仁，曆仕三代，克終富貴，保茲遐壽，良足善也。 」賜坐褥，歸於第。 歲餘，終於家，諡曰恭，時年八十四。 獨孤楷獨孤楷，字修則，不知何許人也，本姓李氏。 父屯，從齊神武帝與周師戰于沙苑，齊師敗績，因為柱國獨孤信所擒，配為士伍，給使信家，漸得親近，因賜姓獨孤氏。 楷少謹厚，便弄馬槊，為宇文護執刀，累轉車騎將軍。 其後數從征伐，賜爵廣阿縣公，邑千戶，拜右侍下大夫。 週末，從韋孝寬平淮南，以功賜子景雲爵西河縣公。 高祖為丞相，進授開府，每督親信兵。 及受禪，拜右監門將軍，進封汝陽郡公。 數歲，遷右衛將軍。 仁壽初，出為原州總管。 時蜀王秀鎮益州，上征之，猶豫未發。 朝廷恐秀生變，拜楷益州總管，馳傳代之。 秀果有異志，楷諷諭久之，乃就路。 楷察秀有悔色，因勒兵為備。 秀至興樂，去益州四十餘裡，將反襲楷，密令左右覘所為，知楷不可犯而止。 楷在益州，甚有惠政，蜀中父老於今稱之。 煬帝即位，轉并州總管。 遇疾喪明，上表乞骸骨。 帝曰：「公先朝舊臣，曆職二代，高風素望，臥以鎮之，無勞躬親簿領也。 」遣其長子淩雲監省郡事。 其見重如此。 數載，轉長平太守，未視事而卒。 諡曰恭。 子淩雲、平雲，彥雲，皆知名。 楷弟盛，見《誠節傳》。 乞伏慧乞伏慧，字令和，馬邑鮮卑人也。 祖周，魏銀青光祿大夫，父纂，金紫光祿大夫，並為第一領民酋長。 慧少慷慨有大節，便弓馬，好鷹犬。 齊文襄帝時，為行台左丞，加蕩寇將軍，累遷右衛將軍、太僕卿，自永甯縣公封宜民郡王。 其兄貴和又以軍功為王，一門二王，稱為貴顯。 周武平齊，授使持節、開府儀同大將軍，拜佽飛右旅下大夫，轉熊渠中大夫。 高祖為丞相，從韋孝寬擊尉惇于武陟，所當皆破，授大將軍，賜物八百段。 及平尉迥，進位柱國，賜爵西河郡公，邑三千戶，賚物二千三百段。 請以官爵讓兄，朝廷不許，論者義之。 高祖受禪，拜曹州刺史。 曹土舊俗，民多奸隱，戶口名簿帳，恆不以實。 慧下車按察，得戶數萬。 遷涼州總管。 先是，突厥屢為寇抄，慧於是嚴警烽燧，遠為斥候，虜亦素憚其名，竟不入境。 歲餘，轉齊州刺史，得隱戶數千。 遷壽州總管。 其年，左轉杞州刺史，在職數年，遷徐州總管。 時年逾七十，上表求致仕，不許。 俄轉荊州總管，又領潭、桂二州總管三十一州諸軍事。 其俗輕剽，慧躬行樸素以矯之，風化大洽。 曾見人以穀捕魚者，出絹買而放之，其仁心如此。 百姓美之，號其處曰西河公穀。 轉秦州總管。 煬帝即位，為天水太守。 大業五年，征吐谷渾，郡濱西境，民苦勞役，又遇帝西巡，坐為道不整，獻食疏薄，帝大怒，命左右斬之。 見其無發，乃釋，除名為民。 卒於家。 張威張威，不知何許人也。 父琛，魏弘農太守。 威少倜儻有大志，善騎射，膂力過人。 在周，數從征伐，位至柱國、京兆尹，封長壽縣公，邑千戶。 王謙作亂，高祖以威為行軍總管，從元帥梁睿擊之。 軍次通穀，謙守將李三王擁勁兵拒守，睿以威為先鋒。 三王初閉壘不戰，威令人詈侮以激怒之，三王果出陣。 威令壯士奮擊，三王軍潰，大兵繼至，於是擒斬四千餘人。 進至開遠，謙將趙儼眾十萬，連營三十裡。 威鑿山通道，自西嶺攻其背，儼遂敗走。 追至成都，與謙大戰，威將中軍。 及謙平，進位上柱國，拜瀘州總管。 高祖受禪，曆幽、洛二州總管，改封晉熙郡公。 尋拜河北道行台僕射，後督晉王軍府事。 數年，拜青州總管，賜錢八十萬，米五百石，雜彩三百段。 威在青州，頗治產業，遣家奴于民間鬻蘆菔根，其奴緣此侵擾百姓。 上深加譴責，坐廢於家。 後從上祠太山，至洛陽，上謂威曰：「自朕之有天下，每委公以重鎮，可謂推赤心矣。 何乃不修名行，唯利是視？ 豈直孤負朕心，亦且累卿名德。 」因問威曰：「公所執笏今安在？ 」威頓首曰：「臣負罪虧憲，無顏複執，謹藏於家。 」上曰：「可持來。 」威明日奉笏以見，上曰：「公雖不遵法度，功效實多，朕不忘之。 今還公笏。 」於是複拜洛州刺史，後封睆城郡公。 尋轉相州刺史，卒官。 有子植，大業中，至武賁郎將。 和洪和洪，汝南人也。 少有武力，勇烈過人。 周武帝時，數從征伐，以戰功累遷車騎大將軍、儀同三司。 時龍州蠻任公忻、李國立等聚眾為亂，刺史獨孤善不能禦。 朝議以洪有武略，代善為刺史。 月餘，擒公忻、國立，皆斬首梟之，餘党悉平。 從帝攻河陰，洪力戰，陷其西門。 帝壯之，賞物千段。 複從帝平齊，進位上儀同，賜爵北平侯，邑八百戶，拜左勳曹下大夫。 柱國王軌之擒吳明徹也，洪有功焉，加位開府，遷折衝中大夫。 尉迥作亂相州，以洪為行軍總管，從韋孝寬擊之。 軍至河陽，迥遣兵圍懷州，洪與總管宇文述等擊走。 又破尉惇于武陟。 及平相州，每戰有功，拜柱國，封廣武郡公，邑二千戶。 前後賜物萬段，奴婢五十口，金銀各百挺，牛馬百匹。 時東夏初平，物情尚梗，高祖以洪有威名，令領冀州事，甚得人和。 數歲，征入朝，為漕渠總管監，轉拜泗州刺史。 屬突厥寇邊，詔洪為北道行軍總管，擊走虜，至磧而還。 後遷徐州總管，卒，時年六十四。 侯莫陳穎侯莫陳穎，字遵道，代人也。 與魏南遷，世為列將。 父崇，魏、周之際，曆職顯要，官至大司空。 穎少有器量，風神警發，為時輩所推。 魏大統末，以父軍功賜爵廣平侯，累遷開府儀同三司。 周武帝時，從滕王逌擊龍泉、文城叛胡，與柱國豆盧勣各帥兵分路而進。 穎懸軍五百餘裡，破其三柵。 先是，稽胡叛亂，輒略邊人為奴婢。 至是詔胡敢有壓匿良人者誅，籍沒其妻子。 有人言為胡村所隱匿者，勣將誅之，穎謂勣曰：「將在外，君命有所不行。 諸胡固非悉反，但相迫脅為亂耳。 大兵臨之，首亂者知懼，脅從者思降。 今漸加撫慰，自可不戰而定。 如即誅之，轉相驚恐，為難不細。 未若召其渠帥，以隱匿者付之，令自歸首，則群胡可安。 」勣從之。 群胡感悅，爭來降附，北土以安。 遷司武，加振威中大夫。 高祖為丞相，拜昌州刺史。 會受禪，竟不行，加上開府，進爵升平郡公。 俄拜延州刺史。 數年，轉陳州刺史。 平陳之役，以行軍總管從秦王俊出魯山道。 屬陳將荀法尚、陳紀降，穎與行軍總管段文振度江安集初附。 尋拜饒州刺史，未之官，遷瀛州刺史，甚有惠政。 在職數年，坐與秦王俊交通免官。 百姓將送者，莫不流涕，因相與立碑，頌穎清德。 未幾，檢校汾州事，俄拜邢州刺史。 仁壽中，吏部尚書牛弘持節巡撫山東，以穎為第一。 高祖嘉歎，優詔褒揚。 時朝廷以嶺南刺史、縣令多貪鄙，蠻夷怨叛，妙簡清吏以鎮撫之，於是征穎入朝。 及進見，上與穎言及平生，以為歡笑。 數日，進位大將軍，拜桂州總管十七州諸軍事，賜物而遣之。 及到官，大崇恩信，民夷悅服，溪洞生越，多來歸附。 煬帝即位，穎兄梁國公芮坐事徙邊，朝廷恐穎不自安，征歸京師。 數年，拜恆山太守。 其年，嶺南、閩越多不附，帝以穎前在桂州有惠政，為南土所信伏，複拜南海太守。 後四歲，卒官。 諡曰定。 子虔會，最知名。 【論】史臣曰：杜彥東夏、南服，屢有戰功，作鎮朔垂，胡塵不起。 高勱死亡之際，志氣懍然，疾彼奸邪，致茲餘慶。 爾硃敞幼有權奇，終能止足，崇基墜而複構，不亦仁且智乎！ 周搖以質實見知，獨孤以恤人流譽，乞伏慧能以國讓，侯莫陳所居治理，或知牧人之道，或踐仁義之路，皆有可稱焉。 慧以供帳不厚，至於放黜，並結髮登朝，出入三代，終享祿位，不夭性齡，蓋其任心而行，不為矯飾之所致也。
The Qiang tribes of Longyou had repeatedly raided, and because Mai's reputation for severity was well known, he was appointed inspector of Taozhou. On taking office he combined stern authority with generous kindness, and both Chinese and tribesmen willingly submitted. Raw Qiang from the valleys came in groups to pay homage at his yamen—several thousand households in all. Local bullies vanished, lost goods lay untouched on the roads, and for several years his district was celebrated as well governed. Later, when Tuyuhun invaded, Mai was ill and unable to fight; the enemy raided heavily and withdrew. The censorate charged him with losing population records and accepting gifts from the Qiang; he was dismissed from office. He later died at home, aged fifty-six. His son Shilian was the best known. Erzhu Chang, courtesy name Qianluo, was a Qihu tribesman from Xiurong and a clansman of Erzhu Rong. His father Yanbo rose to minister of education and Prince of Boling. After Emperor Shenwu of Qi's victory at Hanling, the entire Erzhu clan was slaughtered. Chang was still a boy and was raised in the palace with his mother. At twelve he broke out through a hole in the wall and came to the main street. Seeing children at play in a group, he stripped off his silks and jeweled finery and traded clothes with one of them to slip away. Pursuers soon arrived; they did not recognize Chang at first and seized the child in the fine garments instead. Before they realized their mistake, night had fallen, and he escaped. He came to a village and found an old woman of the Changsun clan seated on a folding stool. Chang bowed to her in appeal for mercy; pitying him, she hid him in a concealed chamber within the wall. After three years the manhunt intensified and pursuers were closing in. The Changsun lady said, "This has become urgent—you cannot remain here." She gave him provisions and sent him away. He then passed himself off as a Daoist priest, assumed a new name, and lived in seclusion on Mount Song, reading a little in the classics and histories. Within a few years the locals came to regard him as something out of the ordinary. Once, sitting alone beneath a rock, he wept and sighed, "Am I to finish my life like this?" What was Wu Zixu, that he alone could not do the same! He then made his way west to Zhou by back roads and in disguise. Emperor Taizu received him with honor, appointed him grand commander and acting director of the mobile administration, and enfeoffed him as Baron of Lingshou with a fief of fifteen hundred households. He was promoted to regular attendant and supernumerary cavalry constant attendant, then to chariot-and-cavalry grand general and director of departments of the third rank of yitong, and advanced to marquis. During the Baoding era he was made bearer of credentials, chief general of swift cavalry, and director of departments of the third rank of yitong. In the Tianhe era his fief was increased by five hundred households; he served as inspector of Xin, Lin, Xiong, and Tong, and was advanced to duke. When Emperor Wu marched east, he submitted a memorial asking to accompany the campaign and was permitted to do so. In storming cities and breaking enemy lines he prevailed wherever he fought and was raised to upper director of departments. He was appointed inspector of southern Guangzhou and later entered the capital as protector-general grand marshal. A little over a year later he was transferred to inspector of Jiaozhou. He then brought the Changsun lady and his younger brother into his household and supported them generously. When Emperor Gaozu took the throne, his title was changed to Duke of Biancheng. When the tribes of Qian'an rebelled, he was ordered to suppress them. On his return he was appointed governor-general of Jinzhou. He was soon transferred to governor-general of Xuzhou. For several years in office he was known for clarity and severity, and both people and officials stood in awe of him. Later, citing his age, he asked to retire; the court granted him two light carriages, he returned to Hedong, and died at home at seventy-two. His son Zui succeeded to his line. Zhou Yao, courtesy name Shi'an, came from the same stock as Later Wei: his clan had originally been the Punai, and after settling in Luoyang they adopted the surname Zhou. His great-grandfather Baba and grandfather Youliugun had both been Princes of Beiping. His father Shuyan had served as vice minister of the mobile administration and governor-general of southern Jingzhou. As a youth Yao was firm and resolute, skilled in arms, discreet and sincere by nature, and scrupulous in observing the law. In Wei service he rose to director of departments of the third rank of yitong. When Emperor Min of Zhou took the throne, he was granted the surname Chefei and created Duke of Jinshui. He served as inspector of Su and Chu, where officials and people alike were content under his rule. In the pacification of Qi he distinguished himself in every battle, was exceptionally made pillar of state, and was advanced to Duke of Kui. Before long he was appointed governor-general of Jinzhou. At that time Emperor Gaozu was governor-general of Dingzhou. When Empress Dowager Wenxian traveled from the capital to visit him and passed through Yao's jurisdiction, his hospitality was notably plain. Afterward he explained to the empress, "The government offices here are well supplied with funds, but the law forbids me to spend them freely." "Besides, a subject of the throne may not offer private favors." Such was his plain honesty. Emperor Gaozu always praised him for his strict adherence to the law. When he became grand chancellor his title was changed to Duke of Jibei, and he was soon appointed governor-general of Yuzhou. When Emperor Gaozu took the throne, he resumed the surname Zhou. At the beginning of Kaihuang the Turks raided the frontier, and Yan and Ji suffered heavily. The previous governor-general Li Chong had been killed by the enemy. The emperor considered how best to secure the region and said at court, "No one is better suited than Zhou Yao." He was appointed governor-general of Youzhou with authority over six prefectures, fifty garrisons, and all related military affairs. Yao repaired the border defenses, maintained strict scouting, and the frontier people lived in security. Six years later he was transferred to Shouzhou. Earlier, believing himself too old for office, he had asked to retire, and the emperor summoned him to court. When he was received in audience, the emperor comforted him: "You have heaped up merit through many years of service, held office under three dynasties, attained wealth and honor to the end, and preserved a long life—this is truly admirable." He was given a seat cushion and sent home. A little over a year later he died at home. His posthumous title was Gong, and he was eighty-four. Du Gukai, courtesy name Xiuzi, was of unknown origin and had originally borne the surname Li. His father Tun had followed Emperor Shenwu of Qi against Zhou forces at Shayuan. After the Qi army was defeated, he was captured by the pillar of state Du Guxin, assigned as a soldier in Xin's household, gradually won his trust, and was granted the surname Du Gu. As a youth Kai was discreet and sincere and skilled with the cavalry lance. He served as blade-carrier for Yuwen Hu and rose repeatedly to chariot-and-cavalry general. He later took part in many campaigns, was created Duke of Guang'a with a fief of a thousand households, and appointed right attendant lower grandee. At the end of Zhou he followed Wei Xiaokuan in pacifying Huainan, and for his achievements his son Jingyun was ennobled as Duke of Xihe. When Emperor Gaozu was grand chancellor he was further made director of departments and regularly commanded the personal guard. When the mandate was received he was appointed right gate-guard general and advanced to Duke of Ruyang. Several years later he was transferred to right guard general. At the beginning of Renshou he was sent out as governor-general of Yuanzhou. At that time the Prince of Shu, Xiu, held Yizhou. The emperor had summoned him, but Xiu hesitated to leave. The court feared Xiu would rebel, so Kai was appointed governor-general of Yizhou and hurried there by express post to replace him. Xiu did indeed harbor rebellious intentions, but after long admonition from Kai he finally set out on the road. Kai saw that Xiu looked regretful and therefore kept his troops on alert. At Xingle, still more than forty li from Yizhou, Xiu was about to turn back and attack Kai. He secretly ordered his men to watch Kai's movements, but seeing that Kai could not be taken by surprise, he abandoned the plan. In Yizhou Kai governed with notable kindness, and the elders of Shu still praise him to this day. When Emperor Yang came to the throne, he was transferred to governor-general of Bingzhou. He fell ill and lost his sight, then submitted a memorial asking to retire. The emperor said, "You are an old minister of the former court who served through two reigns, a man of lofty conduct and long-standing repute. Rest where you are and secure the post; you need not attend to the ledgers yourself." He sent his eldest son Lingyun to oversee the affairs of the commandery. Such was the esteem in which he was held. Several years later he was transferred to administrator of Changping but died before he could take up the post. His posthumous title was Gong. His sons Lingyun, Pingyun, and Yanyun all became well known. Kai's younger brother Sheng is discussed in the the Biography of Loyal Martyrs. Qi Fuhui, courtesy name Linghe, was a Xianbei from Mayi. His grandfather Zhou had been a silver-gleam grand master for illuminating virtue in Wei, and his father Zuan a golden-purple grand master for illuminating virtue; both had been first-rank tribal chiefs. In youth Hui was open-handed and high-minded, skilled at archery and horsemanship, and fond of hawking and hunting. Under Emperor Wenxiang of Qi he served as left aide of the mobile administration, was given the additional title Pacifying-Rebels general, rose to right guard general and grand master of stud, and advanced from Duke of Yongning to Prince of Yimin. His elder brother Guihe also became a prince through military merit, so that one household held two princely titles and was regarded as eminent. When Emperor Wu of Zhou pacified Qi, he was granted bearer of credentials and director of departments equal to great general, appointed flying-right-wing lower grandee, and transferred to Bear-Brigade middle grandee. When Emperor Gaozu was grand chancellor he followed Wei Xiaokuan against Yuwen Dun at Wuzhi, prevailed in every engagement, was made great general, and received eight hundred bolts of goods. After the pacification of Yuwen Jiong he was advanced to pillar of state, created Duke of Xihe with a fief of three thousand households, and given twenty-three hundred bolts of goods. He asked to yield his rank and title to his elder brother, but the court refused; commentators praised his righteousness. When Emperor Gaozu took the throne he was appointed inspector of Caozhou. Caozhou had long been a place where people concealed themselves from registration, and household records were habitually false. On taking office Hui conducted an investigation and registered tens of thousands of additional households. He was transferred to governor-general of Liangzhou. The Turks had long raided the region, but Hui tightened the beacon system and posted scouts far out. The enemy had long dreaded his name and did not cross the border during his tenure. A little over a year later he was transferred to inspector of Qizhou and uncovered several thousand hidden households. He was then transferred to governor-general of Shouzhou. That year he was demoted to inspector of Qizhou; after several years he was transferred to governor-general of Xuzhou. By then he was over seventy and asked to retire, but the request was denied. He was soon transferred to governor-general of Jingzhou and also given oversight of Tan and Gui and military authority over thirty-one prefectures. The local custom was restless and light-minded; Hui corrected it by living plainly himself, and customs were greatly improved. Once he saw someone using grain to bait fish; he paid out silk to buy the fish and set them free—such was his humane disposition. The people praised him and called the place the Duke of Xihe's Grain. He was transferred to governor-general of Qinzhou. When Emperor Yang came to the throne he was made administrator of Tianshui. In the fifth year of Daye, during the campaign against Tuyuhun, his commandery lay on the western frontier and the people were worn down by forced labor. When the emperor toured the west, Hui was charged with failing to repair the roads and offering meager provisions. The emperor was furious and ordered his attendants to behead him. Seeing that he was bald, the emperor spared him but struck his name from the rolls and reduced him to commoner status. He later died at home. Zhang Wei was of unknown origin. His father Chen had served as administrator of Hongnong under Wei. As a youth Wei was free-spirited and ambitious, skilled at horsemanship and archery, and possessed exceptional strength. Under Zhou he took part in many campaigns, rose to pillar of state and metropolitan governor of Jingzhao, and was created Duke of Changshou with a fief of a thousand households. When Wang Qian rebelled, Emperor Gaozu appointed Wei campaign commander under the commander-in-chief Liang Rui. When the army reached Tonggu, Qian's defender Li Sanwang held the pass with strong forces. Rui made Wei his vanguard. Sanwang at first kept his gates shut. Wei had men hurl insults to provoke him, and Sanwang finally came out to fight. Wei sent his strongest men in a fierce assault; Sanwang's army broke, the main force came up, and more than four thousand of the enemy were captured or killed. Advancing to Kaiyuan, they found Qian's general Zhao Yan with a force of a hundred thousand, encamped in a line thirty li long. Wei cut a road through the mountains and attacked from the western ridge against their rear; Yan was routed and fled. Pursuing to Chengdu, they fought a great battle with Qian, with Wei commanding the center. After Qian was subdued, Wei was advanced to upper pillar of state and appointed governor-general of Luzhou. When Emperor Gaozu took the throne, he served as governor-general of You and Luo and was re-created Duke of Jinxi. He was soon appointed vice minister of the Hebei circuit mobile administration and later oversaw the military staff of the Prince of Jin. Several years later he was appointed governor-general of Qingzhou and given eight hundred thousand cash, five hundred shi of grain, and three hundred bolts of silks. In Qingzhou Wei built up his private holdings and sent household slaves into the market to sell radish roots; the slaves used this as a pretext to harass the people. The emperor sternly rebuked him and he was dismissed to remain at home. Later, when he accompanied the emperor to sacrifice at Mount Tai and reached Luoyang, the emperor said to him, "Since I gained the realm I have repeatedly entrusted you with weighty posts—that was placing my full trust in you." Why then have you failed to cultivate your reputation and looked only to profit?" You have not only betrayed my trust but also stained your own good name." He then asked, "Where is the court tablet you once held?" Wei kowtowed and said, "I have offended and fallen short of the law; I had no face to hold it again and have kept it at home." The emperor said, "Bring it here." The next day Wei presented the tablet. The emperor said, "Though you did not observe the law, your achievements have been many, and I have not forgotten them." I now return your tablet to you." He was then reappointed inspector of Luozhou and later created Duke of Huancheng. He was soon transferred to inspector of Xiangzhou and died in office. His son Zhi, during the Daye era, rose to martial-guard commandant. He Hong was from Runan. In youth he possessed great physical strength, and his courage and fierceness surpassed other men. Under Emperor Wu of Zhou he took part in many campaigns and, for his achievements, rose repeatedly to chariot-and-cavalry grand general and director of departments of the third rank of yitong. At that time the Longzhou tribesmen Ren Gongxin and Li Guoli and others gathered forces in rebellion, and the inspector Du Gushan could not repel them. The court judged that Hong had military ability and replaced Shan as inspector. Within a little over a month he captured Gongxin and Guoli, beheaded them and displayed their heads, and the remaining rebels were all pacified. In the attack on Heyin he fought fiercely and broke through the western gate. The emperor admired his valor and rewarded him with a thousand bolts of goods. He again followed the emperor in pacifying Qi, was advanced to upper yitong, created Marquis of Beiping with a fief of eight hundred households, and appointed left meritorious-service lower grandee. When the pillar of state Wang Gui captured Wu Mingche, Hong distinguished himself and was made director of departments, then transferred to breaker-of-the-enemy middle grandee. When Yuwen Jiong rebelled at Xiangzhou, Hong was made campaign commander under Wei Xiaokuan. When the army reached Heyang, Jiong sent troops to besiege Huaizhou; Hong, with Yuwen Shu and others, drove them off. He again defeated Yuwen Dun at Wuzhi. After Xiangzhou was pacified he distinguished himself in every battle, was made pillar of state, and created Duke of Guangwu with a fief of two thousand households. In all he received ten thousand bolts of goods, fifty household slaves, a hundred ingots each of gold and silver, and a hundred head of cattle and horses. Eastern territories had only recently been pacified and popular sentiment was still unsettled. Because Hong's reputation for severity was well known, Emperor Gaozu put him in charge of Jizhou, where he won broad goodwill. Several years later he was recalled to court, served as overseer of the canal-route governor-general, and was transferred to inspector of Sizhou. When the Turks raided the frontier, he was ordered as northern-route campaign commander, drove off the enemy, and returned after reaching the desert. He was later transferred to governor-general of Xuzhou and died at sixty-four. Houmo Chenying, courtesy name Zundao, was from Dai. When the court moved south with Wei, his family for generations had served as ranked generals. His father Chong, in the transition between Wei and Zhou, had held high office and risen to grand minister of works. In youth Ying had breadth of character and a keen, commanding presence, and was esteemed by his contemporaries. At the end of the Wei Great Unity era he was ennobled as Marquis of Guangping for his father's military merit and rose repeatedly to director of departments of the third rank of yitong. Under Emperor Wu of Zhou he followed Prince Teng You against the rebellious Hu of Longquan and Wencheng, advancing by separate routes with the pillar of state Dou Lu Ji. Ying pressed forward more than five hundred li and broke three of their stockades. Previously, whenever the Ji Hu rebelled they would seize frontier people as slaves. An edict now declared that any Hu who concealed commoners would be executed and his wife and children confiscated. When someone reported that people were hidden in a Hu village, Ji was about to execute the villagers. Ying told him, "A general in the field may disregard the ruler's command when necessary." The Hu are not all rebels; most were forced into rebellion under duress. When a great army presses upon them, the ringleaders will fear and those coerced will wish to surrender. If we gradually reassure them, we can secure the region without fighting. If we execute them at once, they will panic one another and the trouble will be far greater. It would be better to summon their chieftains, hand over those who were hidden to them, and let them surrender on their own; then the Hu can be pacified." Ji followed his advice. The Hu were moved and pleased, came forward in numbers to submit, and the north was secured. He was transferred to director of martial affairs and given the additional title queller-of-awe middle grandee. When Emperor Gaozu was grand chancellor he was appointed inspector of Changzhou. Just as he was to take up the post the mandate was received; he did not go after all, was made upper director of departments, and advanced to Duke of Shengping. He was soon appointed inspector of Yanzhou. Several years later he was transferred to inspector of Chenzhou. In the conquest of Chen he served as campaign commander under Qin Prince Jun on the Lushan route. When the Chen generals Xun Fashang and Chen Ji surrendered, Ying and campaign commander Duan Wenzhen crossed the river to settle the newly submitted. He was soon appointed inspector of Raozhou but did not take up the post; he was transferred to inspector of Yingzhou, where he governed with notable kindness. After several years in office he was dismissed for communicating with Qin Prince Jun. The people who came to see him off wept; they jointly erected a stele praising his pure conduct. Before long he was put in charge of Fenzhou as acting administrator; he was soon appointed inspector of Xingzhou. During Renshou, Minister of Personnel Niu Hong toured Shandong with imperial credentials and ranked Ying first. Emperor Gaozu expressed admiration and issued a commendatory edict. The court believed that the inspectors and magistrates of Lingnan were mostly greedy and that the tribes resented and rebelled; upright officials were sought to pacify the region, and Ying was summoned to court. When he was received in audience, the emperor spoke with him of their shared past with evident pleasure. Several days later he was advanced to great general, appointed governor-general of Guizhou with authority over seventeen prefectures, given gifts, and sent to his post. On reaching his post he greatly emphasized grace and trustworthiness; both Chinese and tribes were pleased to submit, and many Yue of the stream valleys came to attach themselves. When Emperor Yang came to the throne, Ying's elder brother the Duke of Liang Rui was punished and exiled. The court feared Ying would feel insecure and recalled him to the capital. Several years later he was appointed administrator of Hengshan. That year much of Lingnan and Min-Yue would not submit. Because Ying's earlier benevolent rule in Guizhou had won the trust of the south, he was again appointed administrator of Nanhai. Four years later he died in office. His posthumous title was Ding. His son Qianhui was the best known. [Commentary] The historiographer says: Du Yan won repeated victories in the east and south; as warden of the northern frontier he kept the Turkic threat at bay. At the moment of his fall, Gao Mai's spirit remained unbroken; his hatred of treachery brought lasting honor to his house. Erzhu Chang showed remarkable resourcefulness in youth and in the end knew when to stop; though his house fell, he rebuilt it—was this not both humane and wise? Zhou Yao was known for plain honesty, Du Gukai for compassion, Qi Fuhui for yielding rank to his brother, and Houmo Chenying for good governance—some knew how to shepherd the people, some walked the path of benevolence and righteousness; each had qualities worth praise. Hui was dismissed because his provisions were deemed insufficient; yet all had entered service young, passed through three dynasties, and enjoyed rank to the end of their natural lives—likely because they acted from the heart rather than from artifice.

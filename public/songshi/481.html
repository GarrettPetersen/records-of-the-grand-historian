1
世家四○南漢劉氏
Hereditary Houses 4: The Liu Clan of Southern Han
2
南漢劉鋹，其先蔡州上蔡人，高祖安仁，仕唐為潮州刺史，因家嶺表。 安仁生謙，為廣州牙校，累遷封州刺史、賀水鎮遏使。 謙生隱，謙卒，隱代領其任。 唐昭宗以薛王知柔鎮南海，辟為行軍司馬，委以兵柄。 及宰相徐彥若代知柔，以為節度副使。 時唐室已季，彥若威令不振，事皆決於隱。 彥若卒，遺表薦隱自代，昭宗不從，以崔遠代之。 遠至江陵，遷延不進，乃以隱為留後，未幾，授以節旄。 梁開平初，兼靜海軍節度使，封南海王。 隱卒，弟陟襲位。 貞明三年，僭帝號，國稱大漢，改元乾亨，行郊祀禮。 改名岩，又改龔，終改。 「」讀為「儼」，字書不載，蓋其妄作也。 晉天福七年，卒，子玢嗣，為弟晟所殺。 晟遂自立，性尤酷暴，周顯德五年，卒，事具《五代史》。
Liu Chang of Southern Han traced his line to Shangcai in Cizhou. His great-grandfather Anren had served the Tang as prefect of Chaozhou and settled his family in Lingnan. Anren's son Qian served as a military aide at Guangzhou and rose in time to prefect of Feng and commissioner for suppressing unrest on the He River. Qian's son Yin succeeded him when Qian died and assumed his post. When Emperor Zhaozong of Tang sent Prince of Xue Zhi Rou to govern the Southern Sea, he recruited Yin as campaigning marshal and entrusted him with military power. When Chief Minister Xu Yanruo replaced Zhi Rou, Yin was appointed deputy military commissioner. The Tang dynasty was already in decline; Yanruo's orders carried no weight, and Yin decided everything. On Yanruo's death he memorialized the throne urging that Yin succeed him, but Zhaozong refused and appointed Cui Yuan in his place. Cui Yuan reached Jiangling but lingered without advancing, so Yin was made acting commissioner; shortly afterward he received the commissioner's insignia. Early in the Liang reign era Kaiping, he also became military commissioner of the Jinghai Army and was enfeoffed as King of Nanhai. After Yin died, his younger brother Yang took the throne. In the third year of Zhenming (917), he declared himself emperor, named his state Great Han, adopted the era name Qianheng, and performed suburban sacrifices. He changed his name to Yan, then to Gong, and finally to a character of his own invention (the text's final form). The final character was pronounced like "yan," as in the word for majestic bearing; it appears in no dictionary and was plainly his own fabrication. He died in the seventh year of Tianfu (942). His son Bin succeeded him but was killed by his younger brother Sheng. Sheng then seized power himself. He was exceptionally cruel and violent. He died in the fifth year of Xiande (958); the full account is given in the History of the Five Dynasties.
3
鋹即晟長子也，初名繼興，封衛王，襲父位，改今名，改元大寶。 性昏懦，委政宦官龔澄樞及才人盧瓊仙，每詳覽可否，皆瓊仙指之。 鋹日與宮人、波斯女等遊戲。 內官陳延壽引女巫樊胡入宮，言玉皇遣樊胡命鋹為太子皇帝，乃於宮中施帷幄，羅列珍玩，設玉皇坐。 樊胡遠遊冠、紫衣、紫霞裙，坐宣禍福，令鋹再拜聽命； 嘗雲瓊仙、澄樞、延壽皆玉皇遣輔太子皇帝，有過不得治。 又有梁山師、馬媼、何擬之徒出入宮掖。 宮中婦人皆具冠帶，領外事。
Chang was Sheng's eldest son. Originally named Jixing and enfeoffed as Prince of Wei, he succeeded his father, adopted his present name, and proclaimed the era name Dabao. Dull and timid by nature, he handed affairs of state to the eunuch Gong Chengshu and the talented consort Lu Qiongxian; on every matter he weighed, it was Qiongxian who guided his decision. Chang passed his days in amusements with palace women and Persian girls. The inner attendant Chen Yanshou brought the witch Fan Hu into the palace, claiming the Jade Emperor had sent her to name Chang crown emperor. Curtains were hung, rare treasures displayed, and a throne for the Jade Emperor installed. Fan Hu wore a traveling cap, purple robe, and purple cloud skirt, sat pronouncing fortune and disaster, and made Chang bow repeatedly to receive her commands; She once declared that Qiongxian, Chengshu, and Yanshou had all been sent by the Jade Emperor to assist the crown emperor and could not be punished for any offense. Others such as Liangshan Shi, Nurse Ma, and He Ni also moved freely through the inner palace. Every woman in the palace wore official cap and belt and handled affairs outside the palace.
4
初，雖寵任中官，其數裁三百餘，位不過掖庭諸局令丞。 至晟時千餘人，稍增內常侍、諸謁者之稱。 至鋹漸至七千餘，有為三師、三公，但其上加「內」字，諸使名不翅二百，女官亦有師傅、令仆之號。 目百官為「門外人」，群臣小過及士人、釋、道有才略可備問者，皆下蠶室，令得出入宮闈。 作燒煮剝剔、刀山劍樹之刑，或令罪人鬥虎抵象。 又賦斂煩重，邕民入城者人輸一錢，瓊州米斗稅四五錢。 置媚川都，定其課，令入海五百尺采珠。 所居宮殿以珠、玳瑁飾之。 陳延壽作諸淫巧，日費數萬金。 宮城左右離宮數十，鋹遊幸常至月餘或旬日。 以豪民為課戶，供宴犒之費。
At first, even though the court favored eunuchs, their number was barely three hundred, and none rose above the rank of director or assistant in the palace women's bureaus. Under Sheng they exceeded a thousand, and titles such as inner regular attendant and palace usher were gradually introduced. Under Chang the number rose to more than seven thousand. Some held the ranks of the Three Preceptors and Three Dukes with the word "inner" prefixed; envoy titles alone numbered at least two hundred, and female officials bore titles such as master, tutor, director, and vice-director. He called the civil officials "outsiders at the gate." Ministers guilty of minor faults, and any scholar, monk, or priest of talent fit for consultation, were castrated and allowed to move in and out of the inner palace. He invented tortures of burning, boiling, flaying, knife mountains, and sword trees, or forced criminals to fight tigers and elephants. Taxes were oppressive as well: every resident of Yong who entered the capital paid one cash, and in Qiongzhou each dou of rice was taxed four or five cash. He set up the Meichuan Directorate, fixed its quota, and ordered divers five hundred feet down into the sea to collect pearls. The palaces he lived in were ornamented with pearls and tortoise shell. Chen Yanshou devised every sort of licentious novelty, spending tens of thousands in gold every day. Several dozen detached palaces flanked the capital, and Chang's tours of pleasure often lasted a month or more, sometimes ten days at a stretch. Wealthy families were designated tax households to fund banquets and rewards.
5
乾德中，太祖命師克郴州，獲其內品十餘人。 有餘延業者，人質麼麽，太祖問曰：「爾在嶺南為何官？ 」對曰：「為扈駕弓箭手官。 」命授之弓矢，延業極力控弦不開。 太祖因笑問鋹為治之跡，延業備言其奢酷，太祖驚駭曰：「吾當救此一方之民。」
During Qiande, Emperor Taizu ordered his forces to capture Chen prefecture and took more than ten of Southern Han's palace eunuchs. One was Yu Yanye, a man of wretchedly slight build. The Emperor asked, "What post did you hold in Lingnan? " He answered, "I was officer of the bowmen who escort the imperial carriage. " The Emperor had bow and arrows handed to him; Yanye pulled with all his strength but could not draw the string. The Emperor laughed and asked how Chang governed. Yanye described his extravagance and cruelty in full, and the Emperor said in alarm, "I must save the people of that land."
6
先是，晟因湖南馬氏之亂，襲取桂、郴、賀等州。 開寶初，鋹又舉兵侵道州，刺史王繼勳上言。 鋹為政昏暴，民被其毒，請討之。 太祖難其事，令江南李煜遣使以書諭鋹使稱臣，歸湖南舊地。 鋹不從。 煜又遣其給事中龔慎儀遺書曰：
Earlier, taking advantage of the Ma clan's disorder in Hunan, Sheng had seized Gui, Chen, He, and other prefectures. Early in Kaibao, Chang again marched against Dao prefecture, and Prefect Wang Jixun submitted a memorial. Chang's rule was benighted and brutal, the people groaned under him, and [Wang] asked that he be attacked. Taizu hesitated over the campaign and ordered Li Yu of Jiangnan to send envoys with a letter urging Chang to submit as a vassal and return the former Hunan lands. Chang refused. Yu then sent his Secretariat attendant Gong Shenyi with a letter that read:
7
煜與足下叨累世之睦，繼祖考之盟，情若弟兄，義敦交契，憂戚之患，曷嘗不同。 每思會麵而論此懷，抵掌而談此事，交議其所短，各陳其所長； 使中心釋然，利害不惑，而相去萬里，斯願莫伸。 凡於事機不得款會，屢達誠素，冀明此心； 而足下視之，謂書檄一時之儀，近國梗概之事，外貌而待之，泛濫而觀之，使忠告確論如水投石，若此則又何必事虛詞而勞往復哉？ 殊非宿心之所望也。
You and I enjoy the friendship of many generations and inherit the pact our fathers made. In feeling we are brothers; in duty our bond runs deep. Sorrow and trouble — when have we not borne them together? I often wish we could meet face to face and speak frankly, debate with open hands, each exposing the other's faults and each stating his own strengths; until our hearts were clear and profit and loss no longer clouded our judgment — but ten thousand li separate us, and that wish cannot be granted. Whenever urgent matters could not be discussed in person, I have written again and again in plain sincerity, hoping you would see my true intent; yet you have treated them as mere diplomatic formalities, brief notes about a neighbor's affairs — received with outward courtesy and read with indifference, so that honest counsel lands like water on stone. If that is so, why waste words and weary messengers with empty correspondence? That is far from what I have long hoped for between us.
8
今則復遣人使罄申鄙懷，又慮行人失辭，不盡深素，是以再寄翰墨，重布腹心，以代會麵之談與抵掌之議也。 足下誠聽其言如交友諫爭之言，視其心如親戚急難之心，然後三復其言，三思其心，則忠乎不忠，斯可見矣，從乎不從，斯可決矣。
I now send another envoy to state my thoughts fully, yet I fear he may miss his words and fail to convey my deepest sincerity. I therefore write again and lay bare my heart once more, in place of meeting in person and debating hand to hand. Hear these words as a friend's frank counsel; read this heart as a kinsman's cry in desperate need. Read them three times and weigh the intent three times — then whether I speak in loyalty will be clear, and whether you should heed me can be decided.
9
昨以大朝南伐，圖復楚疆，交兵已來，遂成釁隙。 詳觀事勢，深切憂懷，冀息大朝之兵，求契親仁之願，引領南望，於今累年。 臨昨使臣入貢大朝，大朝皇帝果以此事宣示曰：「彼若以事大之禮而事我，則何苦而伐之； 若欲興戎而爭我，則以必取為度矣。 」見今點閱大眾，仍以上秋為期，令弊邑以書復敘前意，是用奔走人使，遽貢直言。 深料大朝之心非有唯利之貪，蓋怒人之不賓而已； 足下非有不得已之事與不可易之謀，殆一時之忿而已。
Recently the Great Court marched south to recover the Chu lands. Since the armies clashed, a rift has opened between us. Watching events closely, I have been deeply troubled, longing to see the Great Court's armies stand down and our bond of kinship restored. For years I have strained southward in hope. When my envoy lately presented tribute at the Great Court, the Emperor declared on this very matter: "If they will treat me with the ceremony owed a suzerain, why should I trouble to attack them? If they wish to take up arms and fight me, I shall regard conquest as inevitable." The armies are even now being reviewed, with late autumn still the deadline. My state was told to restate our earlier position in writing, and so I hurry envoys to you with plain speaking. I am convinced the Great Court is moved not by greed for gain alone, but by anger that subjects refuse to bow; you have no unavoidable cause and no unalterable design — this is surely no more than a passing fit of anger.
10
觀夫古之用武者，不顧小大強弱之殊而必戰者有四； 父母宗廟之仇，此必戰也； 彼此烏合，民無定心，存亡之機以戰為命，此必戰也； 敵人有進，必不舍我，求和不得，退守無路，戰亦亡，不戰亦亡，奮不顧命，此必戰也； 彼有天亡之兆，我懷進取之機，此必戰也。 今足下與大朝非有父母宗廟之仇也，非同烏合存亡之際也，既殊進退不舍、奮不顧命也，又異乘機進取之時也。 無故而坐受天下之兵，將決一旦之命，既大朝許以通好，又拒而不從，有國家、利社稷者當若是乎？
In antiquity, rulers went to war regardless of relative strength in only four circumstances: vengeance for parents and ancestral temples — war was inevitable; when both sides were mere rabbles, the people unsettled, and survival itself depended on fighting — war was inevitable; when the enemy pressed on relentlessly, peace was impossible, and retreat offered no escape — to fight was death, not to fight was death, and men threw away their lives — war was inevitable; when Heaven showed the foe was doomed and we held our chance to advance — war was inevitable. You and the Great Court bear no feud of parents and temples; you are not two rabble armies fighting for bare survival; you are not cornered with no retreat; nor are you seizing Heaven's moment to strike. Without cause you would invite the armies of the empire and stake your realm on a single throw. The Great Court has offered peace, yet you refuse. Is that how a ruler who loves his state and altars should act?
11
夫稱帝稱王，角立傑出，今古之常事也； 割地以通好，玉帛以事人，亦古今之常事也。 盈虛消息、取與翕張，屈伸萬端，在我而已，何必膠柱而用壯，輕禍而爭雄哉？ 且足下以英明之姿，撫百越之眾，北距五嶺，南負重溟，藉累世之基，有及民之澤，眾數十萬，表裏山川，此足下所以慨然而自負也。 然違天不祥，好戰危事，天方相楚，尚未可爭。 恭以大朝師武臣力，實謂天讚也。 登太行而伐上黨，士無難色； 絕劍閣而舉庸蜀，役不淹時。 是知大朝之力難測也，萬里之境難保也。 十戰而九勝，亦一敗可憂； 六奇而五中，則一失何補！
To call oneself emperor or king and stand as a rival power is as old as history itself; and to cede land for peace and send tribute in jade and silk is equally an age-old practice. Fortune rises and falls; giving and taking, advance and retreat — all lie in your own hands. Why cling stubbornly to force, treat disaster lightly, and fight for supremacy? You are gifted and enlightened, ruling the peoples of the Hundred Yue, with the Five Ridges to the north and the deep sea behind you, heir to generations of power, blessed with grace that reaches your people, armies in the hundreds of thousands, mountains and rivers as your shield — no wonder you feel proud. Yet to defy Heaven is ill-omened, and love of war is perilous. Heaven now favors Chu; the moment to fight has not come. I look at the Great Court's armies and ministers and see Heaven's clear approval. They climbed the Taihang to strike Shangdang, and the troops did not flinch; they crossed Jian'ge and subdued Yong and Shu before the season turned. This shows how immeasurable the Great Court's strength is, and how hard a realm of ten thousand li is to hold. Nine victories in ten battles still leave one defeat to dread; five of six stratagems succeed — yet what good is the one that fails?
12
況人自以我國險，家自以我兵強，蓋揣於此而不揣於彼，經其成而末經其敗也。 何則？ 國莫險於劍閣，而庸蜀已亡矣； 兵莫強於上黨，而太行不守矣。 人之情，端坐而思之，意滄海可涉也，及風濤驟興，奔舟失馭，與夫坐思之時蓋有殊矣。 是以智者慮於未萌，機者重其先見； 圖難於其易，居存不忘亡，故曰計禍不及，慮福過之。 良以福者人之所樂，心樂之，故其望也過； 禍者人之所惡，心惡之，故其思也忽。 是以福或修於慊望，禍多出於不期。
Men trust in their rugged land and strong armies, measuring only their own side, not the enemy's — they have seen victory but not yet defeat. Why? No land was more defensible than Jian'ge, yet Yong and Shu fell; no army was stronger than that of Shangdang, yet the Taihang could not be held. Human nature is thus: sitting at ease, one thinks the ocean can be crossed; but when wind and waves rise and the boat loses its helm, the reality is nothing like the reverie. The wise plan before trouble sprouts; the shrewd prize foresight; they tackle hardship while it is still easy and live in security without forgetting ruin. Hence the saying: we reckon disaster too little and dream of fortune too much. Fortune is what men love, and because the heart loves it, hope runs too high; disaster is what men hate, and because the heart hates it, thought runs too low. Fortune is often built on satisfied hopes; disaster usually arrives unlooked-for.
13
又或慮有矜功好名之臣，獻尊主強國之議者，必曰：「慎無和也。 五嶺之險，山高水深，輜重不並行，士卒不成列； 高壘清野而絕其運糧，依山阻水而射以強弩，使進無所得，退無所歸。 」此其一也。 又或曰：「彼所長者，利在平地，今舍其所長，就其所短，雖有百萬之眾，無若我何。 」此其二也。 其次或曰：「戰而勝，則霸業可成，戰而不勝，則泛巨舟而浮滄海，終不為人下。 」此大約皆說士孟浪之談，謀臣捭闔之策，坐而論之也則易，行之如意也則難。
You may also hear ministers hungry for glory urging you to exalt your throne and strengthen your realm. They will say: "Do not sue for peace. The Five Ridges are impassable — mountains high, waters deep; supply trains cannot march abreast, troops cannot keep formation; raise ramparts, strip the countryside, cut their grain lines, hold the mountains and waters, and shoot them with heavy crossbows until advance gains nothing and retreat finds no road home." That is the first argument. Others will say: "Their strength is on open ground. If they leave that and fight where we excel, a million men cannot touch us. That is the second. Still others will say: "Win, and empire is ours; lose, and we sail great ships upon the open sea — we shall never bow to another. Such talk is the loose bragging of itinerant persuaders and the schemes of court plotters — easy at the desk, hard in the field.
14
何則？ 今荊湘以南、庸蜀之地，皆是便山水、習險阻之民，不動中國之兵，精卒已逾於十萬矣。 況足下與大朝封疆接畛，水陸同途，殆雞犬之相聞，豈馬牛之不及？ 一旦緣邊悉舉，諸道進攻，豈可俱絕其運糧，盡保其城壁？ 若諸險悉固，誠善莫加焉； 苟尺水橫流，則長堤虛設矣。 其次曰，或大朝用吳越之眾，自泉州泛海以趣國都，則不數日至城下矣。 當其人心疑惑，兵勢動搖，岸上舟中皆為敵國，忠臣義士能復幾人？ 懷進退者步步生心，顧妻子者滔滔皆是。 變故難測，須臾萬端，非惟暫乖始圖，實恐有誤壯誌，又非巨舟之可及，滄海之可遊也。 然此等皆戰伐之常事，兵家之預謀，雖勝負未知，成敗相半。 苟不得已而為也，固斷在不疑； 若無大故而思之，又深可痛惜。
Why? South of Jing and Xiang, in Yong and Shu, live peoples at home in mountains and rivers, trained in rugged country. Without even moving the central armies, elite troops already exceed a hundred thousand. Your borders touch the Great Court's on every side; water and land routes are the same. Neighbors can hear each other's dogs bark — how could armies fail to reach you? Once every frontier district mobilizes and attacks from every direction, how can you cut off all their supplies and hold every fortress? If every pass were impregnable, nothing could be better; but if water breaches even a foot of the line, the whole long dike is useless. Others say the Great Court may use Wu-Yue troops, sail from Quanzhou across the sea to your capital, and reach your walls within days. When minds waver and armies falter, land and sea alike become enemy ground — how many loyal men will remain? Men weighing flight will scheme at every step; men thinking of wives and children will be beyond counting. Disaster is unpredictable; in a moment everything changes. You may not only miss your first plan but ruin your grand design — and this is no matter of great ships or escape upon the open sea. Yet all this is the common stuff of war and military planning — victory uncertain, success and failure each half likely. If you must fight because there is no choice, the decision is clear; but to contemplate war without grave cause is deeply to be regretted.
15
且小之事大，理固然也。 遠古之例不能備談，本朝當楊氏之建吳也，亦入貢莊宗。 恭自烈祖開基，中原多故，事大之禮，因循未遑，以至交兵，幾成危殆。 非不欲憑大江之險，恃眾多之力，尋悟知難則退，遂修出境之盟； 一介之使才行，萬里之兵頓息，惠民和眾，於今賴之。 自足下祖德之開基，亦通好中國，以闡霸圖。 願修祖宗之謀，以尋中國之好，蕩無益之忿，棄不急之爭； 知存知亡，能強能弱，屈己以濟億兆，談笑而定國家，至德大業無虧也，宗廟社稷無損也。 玉帛朝聘之禮才出於境，而天下之兵已息矣，豈不易如反掌，固如太山哉？ 何必扼腕盱衡，履腸蹀血，然後為勇也。 故曰：「德如毛，民鮮克舉之，我儀圖之。 」又曰：「知止不殆，可以長久。 」又曰：「沈潛剛克，高明柔克。 」此聖賢之事業，何恥而不為哉？
Moreover, the lesser serving the greater is the natural order of things. Ancient precedents are too many to recount, but when the Yang clan founded Wu in our own time, they too paid tribute to Emperor Zhuangzong. Since our Founding Ancestor established the state, turmoil in the Central Plains delayed our observance of tribute to the suzerain until war broke out and we nearly perished. We did not lack the Yangtze's barrier or ample troops, but we saw that when the odds were impossible, retreat was wisdom, and we renewed the treaty beyond our borders; one envoy had barely departed when armies ten thousand li away stood down — the people were spared, the realm calmed, and we still live by that choice today. Since your ancestors founded your realm, you too have kept peace with the Central Court while pursuing your own ambitions. Renew your ancestors' policy, seek the Central Court's friendship again, cast off useless anger, and abandon a fight that cannot wait; know when to survive and when to perish, be strong or yield as needed, humble yourself to save your people, settle the realm with ease — your supreme virtue and great enterprise intact, your temples and altars unharmed. Let tribute cross your border, and the empire's armies will stand down — easier than turning your hand, surer than Mount Tai. Why must you clench your fists, glare about, tread guts and wade in blood before you call it courage? Hence the saying: "Virtue is light as a feather, yet few can bear it up; I will make it my aim. " And: "Knowing when to stop avoids danger; thus one endures. " And: "The hidden and firm overcome by steadfastness; the lofty and bright overcome by yielding. " These are the deeds of sages — what shame could there be in following them?

Moreover, the Great Court's Emperor is a hero of the age who rules the Central Plains, holds the Mandate of the Five Phases, and commands the four quarters — all bow before him; the Xiongnu and Taiyuan fell without a strenuous campaign — who else could turn south and bring the banners home? He is even now holding back the empire's armies, awaiting your gracious response — then the great state's honor will be satisfied and your anger can cease. If you stand firm, it must benefit your temples and altars, your people, the empire, or yourself. Yet none of the four will gain. Why abandon virtue and nurse hatred, make enemies of your own, and turn your glorious southern realm into a furnace of disaster — how can you draw near such flames? Even a small victory cannot secure what follows; if fortune turns against you, the great cause is lost.

Shouzheng's son Kechang held a third-rank service post; Guochang held a borrowed-rank post.
30
龔澄樞，廣州南海人。 性廉謹，不妄交遊。 幼事為內供奉官，累遷內給事。 晟襲位，任閹人林延遇為甘泉宮使，頗預政事。 延遇病將死，言於晟曰：「臣死，惟龔澄樞可用。 」即日擢知承宣院兼內侍省，改德陵使兼龍德宮使。 鋹嗣位，加特進、開府儀同三司、萬華宮使、驃騎大將軍，改上將軍、左龍虎軍觀軍容使、內太師，軍國之務皆決於澄樞。 澄樞與李托、薛崇譽置酷法之具，民甚苦之。
Gong Chengshu was from Nanhai in Guangzhou. He was frugal and cautious and did not socialize casually. In youth he served the court as an inner tribute attendant and rose to inner provisioner. When Sheng seized the throne he appointed the eunuch Lin Yanyu commissioner of Sweet Spring Palace, who took a considerable role in government. As Yanyu lay dying, he told Sheng, "When I am gone, only Gong Chengshu can serve you. " That same day Chengshu was made administrator of the Proclamation Court and the Inner Attendant Service, and commissioner of Virtue Tomb and Dragon Virtue Palace. When Chang succeeded, Chengshu received special advancement, the rank of Grand Preceptor with the Golden Seal, and posts including commissioner of Myriad Splendor Palace and General-in-Chief of Fast Cavalry, then General-in-Chief, observer of the Left Dragon-Tiger Army, and Inner Grand Preceptor. All military and civil affairs were decided by Chengshu. Chengshu, Li Tuo, and Xue Chongyu devised instruments of cruel punishment, and the people suffered terribly.
31
初，岩改名龔，有術者言不利，名龔，當敗國事，遂改名。 後鋹用澄樞，以其姓卒亡其國，澄樞亦被誅。
Earlier, when Yan changed his name to Gong, a diviner said the name was ill-omened and would ruin the state, so he adopted his invented character instead. Later Chang employed Chengshu; because of that surname the state was lost, and Chengshu was executed as well.
32
李托，封州封川人。 少習騎射，以謹願事為內府局令。 晟襲位，遷內侍省內侍，充宮闈諸衛押番兼秀華宮使。 鋹立，改玩華宮使、內侍監兼列聖、景陽二宮使。 托納二女於鋹，鋹以其長為貴妃，次為美人，政事皆訪托而後行。 加特進、開府儀同三司、甘泉宮使兼六軍觀軍容使、行內中尉，遷驃騎上將軍、內太師。
Li Tuo was from Fengchuan in Feng Prefecture. He studied riding and archery in youth and, dutiful and compliant, served the court as director of an inner palace bureau. When Sheng took the throne, Tuo became an inner attendant and served as palace guard rotation commander and commissioner of Splendid Bloom Palace. When Chang took the throne, Tuo became commissioner of Play Bloom Palace, superintendent of inner attendants, and commissioner of the Listed Sages and Sunny Vista palaces. Tuo presented two daughters to Chang, who made the elder an honored consort and the younger a beauty. All government affairs were referred to Tuo before action. He received special advancement, the rank of Grand Preceptor with the Golden Seal, commissioner of Sweet Spring Palace and observer of the Six Armies, acting inner commandant, and was promoted to General-in-Chief of Fast Cavalry and Inner Grand Preceptor.
33
太祖命師伐鋹，既克韶州，統軍使李承渥戰死，節度副使辛延渥間道遣人勸鋹降，托堅沮其議。 及就擒至許田，太祖遣使問托等：「昨已約降，復率眾來拒戰，及軍敗又縱火焚府庫，誰為之謀也？ 」托俯首不能對。 鋹諫議大夫王珪謂托曰：「昔在廣州，機務並爾輩所專，火又自內起，今天子遣使案問，爾復欲推過何人？ 」遂唾而批其頰，托乃引伏，後至京斬之。
When Taizu ordered the campaign against Chang and Shao Prefecture fell, Army Commander Li Chenwo was killed in battle. Deputy Commissioner Xin Yanwo sent envoys by a secret route urging surrender, but Tuo firmly blocked the plan. After the capture at Xutian, Taizu sent envoys to question Tuo and the others: "You had agreed to surrender, yet led troops to resist again, and after defeat set fire to the treasuries. Whose counsel was this? " Tuo bowed his head and could not answer. Chang's Remonstrance Grandee Wang Gui said to Tuo, "In Guangzhou you controlled all affairs of state, and the fire began within the palace. Now the Son of Heaven sends investigators — upon whom will you shift blame? " He spat and struck Tuo's cheek. Tuo confessed, and was beheaded when they reached the capital.
34
薛崇譽，韶州曲江人。 善《孫子五曹算》。 晟署為內門使兼太倉使。 鋹嗣位，遷內中尉、特進、開府儀同三司、簽書點檢司事。 太祖命師克廣州，崇譽縱火焚倉廩，擒至京，與李托同戮。
Xue Chongyu was from Qujiang in Shao Prefecture. He was skilled in Sunzi's Five Offices Calculation. Sheng appointed him inner gate commissioner and grand granary commissioner. When Chang succeeded, Chongyu became inner commandant, received special advancement and the rank of Grand Preceptor with the Golden Seal, and was made drafter for inspection of bureau affairs. When Taizu's army took Guangzhou, Chongyu set fire to the granaries. Captured and brought to the capital, he was executed together with Li Tuo.
35
潘崇徹，廣州南海人。 事為內侍省局丞。 頗讀兵書，立戰功。 晟嘗遣大將吳懷恩伐桂州平之，懷恩為部下所殺，命崇徹代之。 鋹襲位，加西北面都統。 歲餘，鋹頗疑崇徹，遣薛宗譽使其軍以察之。 崇譽還，遂白崇徹日以伶人百餘衣錦繡、吹玉笛，為長夜之飲，不恤軍政。 鋹怒，召歸，奪其兵柄，自是居常怏怏。 太祖命師度嶺，鋹復命崇徹領兵五萬戍賀江，崇徹不為效命。 鋹敗，至京，太祖知其事，特赦之，授汝州別駕，卒。
Pan Chongche was from Nanhai in Guangzhou. He served the court as a bureau assistant in the Inner Attendant Service. He studied military texts and won distinction in battle. Sheng once sent General Wu Huai'en to conquer Gui Prefecture. When Huai'en was killed by his own men, Sheng appointed Chongche in his place. When Chang succeeded, Chongche was made commander-in-chief of the northwest sector. After more than a year Chang grew suspicious of Chongche and sent Xue Zongyu to inspect his army. When Chongyu returned, he reported that Chongche spent his days with more than a hundred entertainers in brocade, playing jade flutes and feasting through the night, heedless of military affairs. Chang in anger recalled him and stripped him of command. Thereafter he lived in constant resentment. When Taizu ordered the army across the mountains, Chang again put Chongche in command of fifty thousand men on the He River, but Chongche would not fight effectively. After Chang's defeat, Chongche reached the capital. Taizu learned the circumstances, pardoned him specially, appointed him vice prefect of Ruzhou, and he died in that office.

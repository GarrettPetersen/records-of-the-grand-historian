1
韓琦，字稚圭，相州安陽人。 父國華，自有傳。 琦風骨秀異，弱冠舉進士，名在第二。 方唱名，太史奏日下五色雲見，左右皆賀。 授將作監丞、通判淄州，入直集賢院、監左藏庫。 時方貴高科，多徑去為顯職，琦獨滯筦庫，眾以為非宜，琦處之自若。 禁中需金帛，皆內臣直批旨取之，無印可驗，琦請復舊制，置傳宣合同司，以相防察。 又每綱運至，必俟內臣監蒞，始得受，往往數日不至，暴露廡下，衙校以為病，琦奏罷之。
Han Qi, styled Zhigui, was a native of Anyang in Xiang Prefecture. His father Guohua has his own biography. Qi had a striking presence and exceptional bearing; in his early twenties he passed the jinshi examination, placing second. As his name was being announced, the court astronomer reported five-colored clouds beneath the sun, and everyone around him offered congratulations. He was appointed vice commissioner of the Directorate of Palace Buildings and concurrent magistrate of Zizhou, took up duty in the Hall of Assembled Talents, and supervised the Left Treasury. At the time top examination honors were highly prized and many went straight into prominent posts; Qi alone lingered in the treasury, which people thought unfitting, yet he remained entirely at ease. Whenever the palace needed gold and silks, eunuchs took them on direct written orders with no seal to verify; Qi asked that the old system be restored and a Transmit-and-Proclaim Contract Office set up so the two sides could check each other. Moreover, whenever convoy shipments arrived they had to wait for eunuch overseers before they could be received; the overseers often did not come for days, and goods lay exposed under the portico. The clerks regarded this as a serious abuse, and Qi memorialized to abolish the practice.
2
歷開封府推官、三司度支判官，拜右司諫。 時宰相王隨、陳堯佐，參知政事韓億、石中立，在中書罕所建明，琦連疏其過，四人同日罷。 又請停內降，抑僥倖。 凡事有不便，未嘗不言，每以明得失、正紀綱、親忠直、遠邪佞為急，前後七十餘疏。 王曾為相，謂之曰：「今言者不激，則多畏顧，何補上德？ 如君言，可謂切而不迂矣。 」曾聞望方崇，罕所獎與，琦聞其語，益自信。 權知制誥。
He served in turn as investigating officer of the Kaifeng prefecture and fiscal judge of the Three Departments, and was appointed remonstrator of the Right Bureau. At the time the chief ministers Wang Sui and Chen Yaozuo and the vice commissioners Han Yi and Shi Zhongli seldom put forward policy in the Secretariat; Qi submitted successive memorials detailing their faults, and all four were dismissed on the same day. He also asked that palace directives be halted and favor-seeking curbed. Whenever anything was amiss he spoke out; he always pressed to clarify what worked and what failed, set discipline right, draw the loyal and upright close, and keep the wicked and fawning at a distance—more than seventy memorials in all. When Wang Zeng was chief minister he said to him, "Nowadays remonstrators who are not forceful mostly fear consequences and hold back—how does that serve the sovereign's virtue? What you say may be called pointed without being pedantic." Zeng's reputation was then at its height and he rarely offered praise; when Qi heard this he grew all the more confident in himself. He served as acting commissioner for drafting edicts.
3
益、利歲饑，為體量安撫使。 異時郡縣督賦調繁急，市上供綺繡諸物不予直，琦為緩調蠲給之，逐貪殘不職吏，汰冗役數百，活饑民百九十萬。 趙元昊反，琦適自蜀歸，論西師形勢甚悉，即命為陝西安撫使。 劉平與賊戰，敗，為所執，時宰入他誣，收繫平子弟，琦辨直其冤。
When Yi and Li circuits suffered famine year after year, he was appointed commissioner for assessing and pacifying the region. In former times prefectures and counties had pressed tax levies harshly; silks and embroideries and other goods supplied to the market were not paid fair value. Qi eased the levies and granted relief, expelled greedy and cruel officials who neglected their duties, cut several hundred superfluous corvée posts, and kept alive 1.9 million people stricken by famine. When Zhao Yuanhao rebelled, Qi had just returned from Shu and discussed the western army's situation in full detail; he was immediately appointed pacification commissioner of Shaanxi. Liu Ping fought the rebels, was defeated, and taken captive; the chief ministers accepted other slanders and arrested Ping's sons and younger brothers; Qi argued his case and cleared the wrongful conviction.
4
進樞密直學士，副夏竦為經略安撫、招討使。 詔遣使督出兵，琦亦欲先發以制賊，而合府固爭，元昊遂寇鎮戎。 琦畫攻守二策馳入奏，仁宗欲用攻策，執政者難之，琦言：「元昊雖傾國入寇，眾不過四五萬人，吾逐路重兵自為守，勢分力弱，遇敵輒不支。 若倂出一道，鼓行而前，乘賊驕惰，破之必矣。 」乃詔鄜延、涇原同出征。 既還營，元昊來求盟，琦曰：「無約而請和者，謀也。 」命諸將戒嚴，賊果犯山外。 琦悉兵付大將任福，令自懷遠城趨德勝砦出賊後，如未可戰，即據險置伏，要其歸。 及行，戒之至再。 又移檄申約，苟違節度，雖有功，亦斬。 福竟為賊誘，沒于好水川。 竦使人收散兵，得琦檄於福衣帶間，言罪不在琦。 琦亦上章自劾，猶奪一官，知秦州，尋復之。
He was promoted to Hanlin academician of the Bureau of Military Affairs and served as deputy to Xia Song as frontier pacification and suppression commissioner. An edict sent envoys to supervise troop movements; Qi also wanted to strike first and seize the initiative, but the whole headquarters stubbornly opposed him, and Yuanhao then raided Zhenrong. Qi drew up offensive and defensive plans and rushed them to court; Emperor Renzong wished to adopt the offensive plan but the chief administrators balked. Qi said, "Although Yuanhao has thrown his whole state into the invasion, his forces number no more than forty or fifty thousand; we keep heavy troops on each route merely to hold our own ground, our strength is divided and weak, and whenever we meet the enemy we cannot stand." If we unite on a single route and advance with drums beating, taking advantage of the rebels' arrogance and slackness, we are sure to break them." An edict then ordered the Fuyan and Jingyuan circuits to campaign together. After returning to camp Yuanhao came seeking peace; Qi said, "Anyone who seeks peace without a prior agreement is plotting." He ordered the generals to stand on alert, and the rebels did indeed attack beyond the mountains. Qi entrusted all the troops to the great general Ren Fu, ordering him to march from Huaiyuan city toward Desheng fort and come out behind the rebels; if battle was not yet feasible, he was to hold difficult ground, set ambushes, and cut off their retreat. When Fu set out, Qi admonished him again and again. He also sent a written order restating the agreement: anyone who violated command would be executed, even if he had merit. Fu was ultimately lured into a trap by the rebels and perished at Hao River. Xia Song sent men to gather the scattered troops and found Qi's order in Fu's belt, stating that the blame did not lie with Qi. Qi also submitted a memorial impeaching himself; he still lost one rank, was made prefect of Qinzhou, and soon had it restored.
5
會四路置帥，以琦兼秦鳳經略安撫、招討使。 慶曆二年，與三帥皆換觀察使，范仲淹、龐籍、王沿不肯拜，琦獨受不辭。 未幾，還舊職，為陝西四路經略安撫、招討使，屯涇州。 琦與范仲淹在兵間久，名重一時，人心歸之，朝廷倚以為重，故天下稱為「韓范」。 東兵從宿衛來，不習勞苦，琦奏增土兵以代戍，建德順軍以蔽蕭關、嗚沙之道。 方謀取橫山，規河南，而元昊稱臣，召為樞密副使。
When commanders were appointed for the four circuits, Qi was made concurrently frontier pacification and suppression commissioner of Qinfeng. In the second year of Qingli he and the three commanders were all transferred to observer commissioner posts; Fan Zhongyan, Pang Ji, and Wang Yan refused to accept, but Qi alone accepted without demur. Before long he returned to his former post as frontier pacification and suppression commissioner of the four Shaanxi circuits, stationed at Jingzhou. Qi and Fan Zhongyan had long been in the field; their fame weighed on the age, hearts turned to them, and the court relied on them heavily—so the realm called them "Han and Fan." Eastern troops coming from palace guard service were unused to hardship; Qi memorialized to increase local troops to replace garrison duty and established the Deshun army to screen the routes through Xiaoguan and Wusha. He was just planning to take Hengshan and target the region south of the river when Yuanhao submitted as a vassal and Qi was summoned as vice commissioner of the Bureau of Military Affairs.
6
元昊介契丹為援，強邀索無厭，宰相晏殊等厭兵，將一切從之。 琦陳其不便，條所宜先行者七事：一曰清政本，二曰念邊計，三曰擢材賢，四曰備河北，五曰固河東，六曰收民心，七曰營洛邑。 繼又陳救弊八事，欲選將帥，明按察，豐財利，遏僥倖，進能吏，退不才，謹入官，去冗食。 謂：「數者之舉，謗必隨之，願委計輔臣，聽其注措。 」帝悉嘉納。 遂宣撫陝西，討平群盜張海、郭邈山； 禁卒羸老不任用者，悉汰之； 盡修鄜延城障，須敵悉歸所侵地，乃許和。 歸陳西北四策，以為：「今當以和好為權宜，戰守為實務。 請繕甲厲兵，營修都城，密定討伐之計。」
Yuanhao relied on Khitan support and pressed demands without end; chief minister Yan Shu and others were weary of war and were about to yield on everything. Qi set forth the drawbacks and listed seven matters that should be undertaken first: clarify the foundation of government; heed frontier planning; promote worthy talent; prepare Hebei; secure Hedong; win the people's hearts; and build the Luo capital. He then set forth eight measures to remedy abuses: select generals and commanders, clarify inspection, enrich finances, curb favor-seeking, advance capable officials, dismiss the untalented, tighten entry into office, and remove superfluous consumption. He said, "When such measures are undertaken, slander is sure to follow; I hope Your Majesty will entrust the plan to your supporting ministers and let them carry it out." The emperor approved and accepted all of them. He was then made pacification commissioner of Shaanxi and suppressed the bandit chiefs Zhang Hai and Guo Miaoshan; palace guards who were weak, old, and unfit for service were all eliminated; he fully repaired the fortifications of Fuyan circuit and would permit peace only when the enemy had wholly returned all seized territory. On his return he set forth four strategies for the northwest, arguing that for the present peace should be treated as an expedient while fighting and defense remained the real task. I ask that armor be repaired, troops sharpened, the capital fortified, and plans for campaign settled in secret."
7
時二府合班奏事，琦必盡言，雖事屬中書，亦指陳其實。 同列或不悅，帝獨識之，曰：「韓琦性直。 」琦與范仲淹、富弼皆以海內人望，同時登用，中外跂想其勳業。 仲淹等亦以天下為己任，群小不便之，毀言日聞。 仲淹、弼繼罷，琦為辨析，不報。 尹洙與劉滬爭城水洛事，琦右洙，朝論不謂然。 乃請外，以資政殿學士知揚州，徙鄆州、成德軍、定州。 兼安撫使，進大學士，又加觀文殿學士。
At the time the Two Departments reported together in court audience; Qi always spoke his mind fully, and even when a matter belonged to the Secretariat he would point out how things really stood. Colleagues were sometimes displeased, but the emperor alone understood him and said, "Han Qi is by nature straightforward." Qi, Fan Zhongyan, and Fu Bi were all men of renown throughout the realm and were appointed at the same time; within and without the court all looked eagerly to their achievements. Fan and the others also took the realm as their own charge; petty men found this inconvenient, and slander was heard daily. Fan and Fu were dismissed in succession; Qi argued to clarify their case but received no reply. Yin Zhu and Liu Hu disputed the fortification of Shuiluo; Qi sided with Zhu, and court opinion did not agree. He then requested an outside post, was made academician of the Hall for Assisting Governance and prefect of Yangzhou, and was transferred to Yanzhou, the Chengde army, and Dingzhou. He was also made pacification commissioner, promoted to grand academician, and further given the title of academician of the Hall for Viewing Culture.
8
初，定州兵狃平貝州功，需賞賚，出怨語，至欲噪城下。 琦聞之，以為不治且亂，用軍制勒習，誅其尤無良者。 士死攻戰，則賞賻其家，籍其孤嫠繼稟之，威恩並行。 又仿古三陣法，日月訓齊之，由是中山兵精勁冠河朔。 京師發龍猛卒戍保州，在道為人害，至定，琦悉留不遣，易素教者使之北，又振活饑民數百萬。 璽書褒激，鄰道視以為準。
At first the Dingzhou troops, accustomed to the glory of pacifying Beizhou, demanded rewards, uttered resentful words, and even wished to riot beneath the walls. When Qi heard of this he thought that if the matter were not corrected there would soon be disorder; he applied military regulations to compel drill and executed the worst offenders. When soldiers died in attack or battle he rewarded their families, registered their orphans and widows and continued their rations, combining sternness and kindness. He also modeled the ancient three-formations method and trained them day by day until they moved as one; thereby the Zhongshan troops became the finest and strongest north of the Yellow River. The capital sent Longmeng guards to garrison Baozhou; on the road they harmed people, and when they reached Dingzhou Qi kept them all and would not send them on, exchanging plain and tractable men to send north; he also revived several million people stricken by famine. Imperial letters praised and encouraged him, and neighboring circuits took him as their standard.
9
拜武康軍節度使、知幷州。 承受廖浩然，怙中貴勢貪恣，既誣逐前帥李昭亮，所為益不法，琦奏還之，帝命鞭諸本省。 契丹冒占天池廟地，琦召其酋豪，示以曩日彼所求修廟檄，無以對，遂歸我斥地。 既又侵耕陽武砦地，琦鑿塹立石以限之。 始，潘美鎮河東，患寇鈔，令民悉內徙，而空塞下不耕，於是忻、代、寧化、大山之北多廢壤。 琦以為此皆良田，今棄不耕，適足以資敵，將皆為所有矣。 遂請距北界十里為禁地，其南則募弓箭手居之，墾田至九千六百頃。 久之，求知相州。
He was appointed military commissioner of the Wukang army and prefect of Bingzhou. The receiver Liao Haoran relied on eunuch power, was greedy and unrestrained, had already slandered and driven out the former commander Li Zhaoliang, and his conduct grew still more lawless; Qi memorialized to have him recalled and the emperor ordered him flogged at the relevant bureau. The Khitan encroached on and seized Tianchi Temple land; Qi summoned their chieftains and showed them the petition they had once submitted to repair the temple—they had no answer and returned the land we had been forced to yield. They then also encroached on farmland at Yangwu stockade; Qi dug trenches and set up boundary stones to mark the limit. At the outset, when Pan Mei held Hedong, fearing bandit raids he ordered the people all to move inward, leaving the frontier empty and untilled; thus north of Xin, Dai, Ninghua, and Dashan there was much abandoned land. Qi held that all this was fine farmland; abandoned and left untilled, it would only supply the enemy, and in time all would become theirs. He then requested that within ten li of the northern border be forbidden ground, and south of that archers be recruited to settle; land brought under cultivation reached 9,600 qing. After a long while he requested appointment as prefect of his home Xiang Prefecture.
10
嘉祐元年，召為三司使，未至，迎拜樞密使。 三年六月，拜同中書門下平章事、集賢殿大學士。 六年閏八月，遷昭文館大學士、監修國史，封儀國公。 帝既連失三王，自至和中病疾，不能御殿。 中外惴恐，臣下爭以立嗣固根本為言，包拯、范鎮尤激切。 積五六歲，依違未之行，言者亦稍怠。 至是，琦乘間進曰：「皇嗣者，天下安危之所系。 自昔禍亂之起，皆由策不早定。 陛下春秋高，未有建立，何不擇宗室之賢者，以為宗廟社稷計？ 」帝曰：「後宮將有就館者，姑待之。 」已又生女。
In the first year of Jiayou he was summoned as commissioner of the Three Departments; before he arrived he was met on the road and appointed commissioner of the Bureau of Military Affairs. In the sixth month of the third year he was appointed grand councilor of the Tongzhongshu Menxia Pingzhangshi rank and grand academician of the Hall of Assembled Talents. In the intercalary eighth month of the sixth year he was transferred to grand academician of the Hall of Illustrious Literature and supervisor of compiling the national history, and enfeoffed as Duke of Yi. The emperor had already lost three princes in succession; from the Zhihe era he was ill and could not hold court. Within and without the court all were fearful; officials vied to speak of establishing an heir to secure the foundation, Bao Zheng and Fan Zhen especially forceful. For five or six years the matter dragged on without action, and those who spoke also gradually slackened. At this point Qi seized an opening and said, "The imperial heir is what the realm's safety and peril depend upon. From of old calamity and disorder have arisen because the plan was not settled early. Your Majesty's years are advanced and no heir has yet been established—why not select a worthy member of the imperial clan for the sake of the ancestral temple and the altars of soil and grain?" The emperor said, "Someone in the inner palace is about to enter confinement—let us wait for now." Soon afterward another daughter was born.
11
一日，琦懷《漢書·孔光傳》以進，曰：「成帝無嗣，立弟之子。 彼中材之主，猶能如是，況陛下乎。 願以太祖之心為心，則無不可者。 」又與曾公亮、張昇、歐陽修極言之。 會司馬光、呂誨皆有請，琦進讀二疏，未及有所啟，帝遽曰：「朕有意久矣，誰可者？ 」琦皇恐對曰：「此非臣輩所可議，當出自聖擇。 」帝曰：「宮中嘗養二子，小者甚純，近不慧，大者可也。 」琦請其名，帝以宗實告。 宗實，英宗舊名也。 琦等遂力贊之，議乃定。
One day Qi brought in the Biography of Kong Guang in the Book of Han and said, "Emperor Cheng had no heir and established his younger brother's son. That was a ruler of only middling talent, yet he could act thus—how much more can Your Majesty? I hope Your Majesty will take Taizu's heart as your own heart—then nothing will be impossible." He also spoke to the utmost on the matter with Zeng Gongliang, Zhang Sheng, and Ouyang Xiu. When Sima Guang and Lü Hui both made requests, Qi advanced to read the two memorials; before he could speak further the emperor suddenly said, "I have had this in mind for a long time—who would be suitable?" Qi answered in fear and trembling, "This is not something we ministers may discuss—it should come from Your Majesty's own choice." The emperor said, "The palace once raised two sons; the younger is very pure-hearted but lately has not been clever—the elder will do." Qi asked his name and the emperor told him Zongshi. Zongshi was Yingzong's former name. Qi and the others then strongly supported him and the decision was settled.
12
英宗居濮王喪，議起知宗正。 琦曰：「事若行，不可中止。 陛下斷自不疑，乞內中批出。 」帝意不欲宮人知，曰：「只中書行足矣。 」命下，英宗固辭。 帝復問琦，琦對曰：「陛下既知其賢而選之，今不敢遽當，蓋器識遠大，所以為賢也。 願固起之。 」英宗既終喪，猶堅臥不起。 琦言：「宗正之命初出，外人皆知必為皇子，不若遂正其名。 」乃下詔立為皇子。 明年，英宗嗣位，以琦為仁宗山陵使，加門下侍郎，進封衛國公。
Yingzong was in mourning for Prince Pu; it was proposed to appoint him director of the imperial clan. Qi said, "If this matter proceeds it cannot be halted midway. Your Majesty must decide without doubt—I beg that an order be issued from within the palace." The emperor did not wish palace women to know and said, "Let the Secretariat handle it—that is enough." When the order was issued Yingzong firmly declined. The emperor again asked Qi; Qi answered, "Your Majesty already knew his worth and chose him; that he now dares not accept at once is precisely because his capacity and vision are far-reaching—that is why he is worthy." I hope Your Majesty will firmly press him to take office." After Yingzong had completed his mourning he still lay abed and would not rise. Qi said, "When the order from the Directorate of Imperial Clans first went out, everyone outside the palace already knew he would be made imperial prince. It would be better to go ahead and formally establish his title. An edict was then issued establishing him as imperial prince. The following year, when Yingzong succeeded to the throne, Qi was appointed overseer of Emperor Renzong's mausoleum, promoted to Vice Director of the Secretariat, and enfeoffed as Duke of Wei.
13
琦既輔立英宗，門人親客，或從容語及定策事，琦必正色曰：「此仁宗聖德神斷，為天下計，皇太后內助之力，臣子何與焉。 」英宗暴得疾，太后垂簾聽政。 帝疾甚，舉措或改常度，遇宦官尤少恩，左右多不悅者，乃共為讒間，兩宮遂成隙。 琦與歐陽修奏事簾前，太后嗚咽流涕，具道所以，琦曰：「此病固爾，病已，必不然。 子疾，母可不容之乎？ 」修亦委曲進言，太后意稍和，久之而罷。 後數日，琦獨見上，上曰：「太后待我無恩。 」琦對曰：「自古聖帝明王，不為少矣。 然獨稱舜為大孝，豈其餘盡不孝耶？ 父母慈愛而子孝，此常事不足道； 惟父母不慈，而子不失孝，乃為可稱。 但恐陛下事之未至爾，父母豈有不慈者哉。 」帝大感悟。 及疾愈，琦請乘輿因禱雨具素服以出，人情乃安。 太后還政，拜琦右僕射，封魏國公。
After Qi had helped establish Yingzong as heir, some among his students and close associates would casually mention the succession decision. Qi always replied sternly, "This was Emperor Renzong's sagely virtue and divine resolve, undertaken for the sake of the realm, with the Empress Dowager's support from within. What part did we subjects have in it? Yingzong suddenly fell ill, and the Empress Dowager held court from behind the curtain. The emperor's illness was severe. His conduct sometimes departed from his usual manner, and he showed eunuchs especially little favor. Many of those around him were displeased and together wove slander to drive a wedge between the two palaces, and a rift arose between emperor and empress dowager. His rank was restored to Grand Master of Palace Affairs, and he finally retired with the title of Grand Master for Court Audience. He died at the age of seventy-two. His son Zhi, under Emperor Huizong, served as Vice Director of the Court of the Imperial Stud and was posted to govern Xiang Prefecture. Illness led him to request a sinecure; his son Xiaozhou was appointed in his place. He has a separate biography.

Zeng Gongliang, styled Mingzhong, was a native of Jinjiang in Quan Prefecture. He placed in the top tier of the jinshi examination and was appointed magistrate of Kuaiji County. Local fields lay beside Mirror Lake, and each year the people suffered when the lake overflowed. Gongliang built sluice gates to drain overflow into the Cao'e River, to the people's great benefit. Because his father had purchased land within his jurisdiction, he was demoted to supervisor of the Huzhou wine monopoly. After some time he became Direct Lecturer of the Directorate of Education, then Lecturer of the Various Princes' Households. When his term ended he should by precedent have tested for a palace appointment; instead he submitted writings of his own and was appointed Collator of the Hall of Assembled Worthies, Lecturer of the Hall of Heavenly Manifestations, and Compiler of the Daily Record. He was promoted to Reserve Master of the Hall of Heavenly Manifestations and granted the gold seal and purple robe of high office. Until then, Reserve Masters had not changed their official dress. Emperor Renzong personally bestowed the honor, saying: "I grant this from my own lecture hall, to honor and exalt a Confucian minister." He was then made Drafting Officer for Proclamations and Compiler for the Historiography Hall, and served as Hanlin Academician directing the Three Ranks Bureau. The clerks of the Three Ranks Bureau were a disorderly lot; nothing moved without a bribe, and the sons of powerful families relied on patronage to get their way. Gongliang compiled every relevant regulation and held the clerks to them so strictly that they could find no leverage against him. As Academician of the Hall of Brilliant Governance he governed Zheng Prefecture with such success that thieves fled to neighboring regions and people slept with their doors unlatched at night. Once a visiting envoy lost something from his baggage and wrote to demand the thief. Gongliang replied: "My jurisdiction does not harbor thieves—it was likely hidden by one of your attendants." A search was made, and so it proved. He returned to the capital as Hanlin Academician and Governor of Kaifeng. Before long he was promoted to Supervisor of Remonstrance and Vice Grand Counselor. He was given the additional title of Vice Minister of Rites and appointed Commissioner of the Bureau of Military Affairs. In the sixth year of Jiayou he was appointed Vice Minister of Personnel, Grand Counselor of the Secretariat-Chancellery, and Grand Academician of the Hall of Assembled Worthies.

Gongliang was thoroughly versed in law and precedent; after long service he knew every regulation of court and secretariat, and Chief Minister Han Qi consulted him constantly. In Renzong's final years Qi proposed establishing an heir, and together with Gongliang and others settled the succession. In Mizhou some fields yielded silver; when people stole it, the Court of Judicial Review would classify the offense as robbery. Gongliang argued: "Silver is a prohibited substance; though the taking may be forcible, it is not the same as robbing an ordinary household." He argued forcefully until the case was referred for review; by analogy to the law on prohibited goods, the thief escaped execution. Before this, eastern circuits had often invoked capital statutes in such cases; after Gongliang's ruling, no one was executed for such offenses.

The Khitan allowed their people to fish in the border river and repeatedly sent salt boats across it; local officials dared not interfere, saying that pushing back would only provoke trouble. Gongliang said: "If we fail to check this at the first sign, what will we do when it grows worse?" Zhao Zi of Xiong Prefecture is brave and shrewd—he is the man for this. An envoy was sent to convey the court's intent, and the border trouble ended. When Emperor Yingzong succeeded, Gongliang was promoted to Vice Director of the Secretariat and Minister of Rites, and soon after given the additional title of Minister of Revenue. When the emperor fell ill, Liao envoys arrived but could not have audience; Gongliang was ordered to host them at the guest house. They refused to attend. Gongliang challenged them: "To refuse a banquet granted by the sovereign is to show contempt for his command." The sovereign is ill, yet you insist he must appear in person—is that reasonable? The envoys immediately took their seats. When Emperor Shenzong succeeded, he was promoted to Vice Director of the Chancellery and Minister of Personnel.

Gongliang was sober, dignified, and deeply deliberative; in ordinary conduct he rigorously observed every rule and regulation; yet he was stingy by nature and amassed wealth on a vast scale; the emperor once compared him to Zhang Anshi. He had early recommended Wang Anshi; once they served together he saw which way the emperor leaned and quietly looked after his family's interests. On every reform he went along entirely, while outwardly seeming to stand apart. He once sent his son Xiaokuan to join Wang's planning sessions; before the throne Xiaokuan raised scarcely any objection, and the emperor's trust in Wang only grew. Wang was grateful for this support and in return elevated Xiaokuan to the Bureau of Military Affairs. Su Shi once gently reproached Gongliang for failing to check the reforms. Gongliang replied: "The emperor and Jiefu are as one—this is Heaven's will." Contemporaries mocked him for clinging to office and currying favor. His son was Xiaokuan; his nephews were Xiaoguang and Xiaoyun.

Yan Prefecture had a temple to Mencius. Xiaokuan petitioned the court and won for Mencius the title Duke of Zou and a place in sacrificial rites alongside Confucius. Xiaoguang, styled Zhongxi. At the end of the Yuanfeng era he served as Vice Director of the Northern Outer Waterways Office. During the Yuanyou era, when senior ministers debated restoring the Yellow River to its old channel, Xiaoguang was summoned for his opinion. He argued against the plan and was sent out as Vice Prefect of Baozhou. After some time he was again appointed Director of the Waterways Office. Previously, when envoys on imperial missions arrived with timber rafts in tow, they could not proceed to the Selection Bureau until an inspection confirmed nothing was missing. Supervising clerks took bribes and delayed their departure indefinitely. Xiaoguang drew up registers with each man's name and strictly tracked arrivals and departures. Within a year, a hundred parties had returned for appointment.

When the Yellow River broke through at Neihuang, an edict ordered Xiaoguang to inspect the breach. He dredged Sucun, cut through Juanye, and redirected the northern stream, relieving the flooding at Chan, Hua, Shen, and Ying. He was promoted to Commissioner of Waterways. The Luo River had swollen year after year, gnawing at the north bank. Xiaoguang inspected the dikes, found the remains of an abandoned sluice, and said, "This is how our predecessors tamed the flood. " That same day he dredged the outlet open, piled stones into a barrier, and from then on the river gave no further trouble. He was sent out as Judicial Intendant of Yongxing Circuit, then Vice Transport Commissioner for Shaanxi and Jingxi. Recalled to the capital, he rose from Director of the Left Section to Vice Minister of Revenue and then Minister. He was punished because funds and cloth had not been disbursed on schedule, demoted to Academician without assignment at the Hall of Heavenly Manifestations, and sent out as prefect of Hangzhou. He was also stripped of rank for impropriety on an earlier mission to the Khitan. Soon his rank was restored. He was transferred to Tanzhou, then given the title Academician in Direct Attendance at the Hall of Manifest Worthies and made prefect of Yan Prefecture.

Xiaoguang was close to Hu Anguo and Zou Hao. All three had crossed the chief minister during the Daguan era. When censors impeached them, Xiaoguang was again demoted and sent out as prefect of Raozhou. A year later he was transferred to Guangzhou, then served at Chengde Army and Taiyuan Prefecture. He recovered his former rank before he died at sixty and was posthumously awarded Grand Master of Rectifying Governance. Xiaoguang was famed for harsh rule. When thieves were caught, he had their hands broken.

孝蘊，字處善。 紹聖中，管幹發運司糶糴事，建言揚之瓜洲，潤之京口，常之奔牛，易堰為閘，以便漕運、商賈。 既成，公私便之。 提舉兩浙常平，改轉運判官，知臨江軍，召為左司員外郎，遷起居舍人。
Xiaoyun, styled Chushan. During the Shaosheng era he oversaw grain sale and purchase for the Transport Office. He proposed replacing weirs with locks at Guazhou in Yang Prefecture, Jingkou in Run Prefecture, and Benniu in Chang Prefecture to ease grain transport and trade. Once the work was finished, officials and merchants alike found it a great convenience. He served as Intendant of the Two-Zhejiang Ever-Normal Granaries, then as Fiscal Judge and prefect of Linjiang Army. Recalled to court, he became Vice Director of the Left Section and then Attendant at the Office for Recording the Emperor's Actions.

Robbers were active in the capital, and Emperor Huizong was furious. He gave the metropolitan prefect three days to catch them—or face punishment himself. Xiaoyun memorialized: "Press the hunt too hard and the robbers flee farther away; ease off a little and they will show themselves. " The emperor took his advice, and the robbers were caught. When the Directorate Within the Hall was created in the Chongning era, he was made its Supervisor. Within months, critics charged that he was close to Zhang Shangying. Chen Shengzhi, styled Yangshu, was a native of Jianyang in Jian Prefecture. He passed the jinshi examination and served as prefect of Feng Prefecture and Hanyang Military Prefecture in turn. He entered court as Investigating Censor and Remonstrator of the Right Bureau, then became Attendant at the Office for Recording the Emperor's Actions and Director of the Remonstrance Bureau. It was then the fashion to hoard letters from friends and kin. When disputes arose, people denounced one another, and officials built their cases on such testimony. Shengzhi said, "This is the habit of tale-bearing and denunciation. I ask that it be forbidden. " He also said, "The Three Academies are the crown of a gentleman's career, yet lately appointments have grown ever more casual until the posts became a ladder for well-connected climbers. Selection should be made strict. " An edict followed: from then on, when officials sought appointments for their sons and grandsons, those posts could not be in the Academies and Archives.

" When Shenzong succeeded, he was promoted to Academician-in-Chief. Gui had drafted inner and outer edicts for eighteen years—the longest tenure of anyone. Once, performing rites at the fasting palace, he composed a poem moved by his feelings, and the emperor saw it and took pity on him. In the third year of Xining he was appointed Vice Grand Counselor. In the ninth year he was promoted to Grand Counselor, Chancellor of the Secretariat and Chancellery, and Grand Academician at the Hall for Gathering Worthies.
61
元豐官制行，由禮部侍郎超授銀青光祿大夫。 五年，正三省官名，拜尚書左僕射兼門下侍郎，以蔡確為右僕射。 先是，神宗謂執政曰：「官制將行，欲新舊人兩用之。 」又曰：「御史大夫，非司馬光不可。 」珪、確相顧失色。 珪憂甚，不知所出。 確曰：「陛下久欲收靈武，公能任責，則相位可保也。 」珪喜，謝確。 帝嘗欲召司馬光，珪薦俞充帥慶，使上平西夏策。 珪意以為既用兵深入，必不召光，雖召，將不至。 已而光果不召。 永樂之敗，死者十餘萬人，實珪啟之。
When the Yuanfeng bureaucracy was implemented, he was promoted directly from Vice Minister of Rites to Silver Light Grand Master of Splendid Happiness. In the fifth year, with the formal titles of the Three Departments established, he was appointed Left Vice Director of the Department of State Affairs concurrent with Vice Director of the Chancellery, and Cai Que was made Right Vice Director. Previously Emperor Shenzong had told the chief administrators, "When the new bureaucracy is implemented, I wish to use men of old and new together. He also said, "For Censor-in-Chief, no one but Sima Guang will do." " Gui and Que looked at each other, their faces turning pale. Gui was deeply worried and did not know what to do. Que said, "Your Majesty has long wished to recover Lingzhou. If you can take responsibility for it, your chancellorship can be preserved." " Gui was pleased and thanked Que. The emperor once wished to summon Sima Guang. Gui recommended Yu Chong to command Qing Prefecture and present a plan to pacify Western Xia. Gui reckoned that with troops already committed to a deep campaign, Guang would certainly not be summoned—and even if summoned, would not come. Before long, Guang indeed was not summoned. The disaster at Yongle cost more than a hundred thousand lives—and it was Gui who had set it in motion.
62
八年，帝有疾，珪白皇太后，請立延安郡王為太子。 太子立，是為哲宗。 進珪金紫光祿大夫，封岐國公。 五月，卒於位，年六十七。 特輟朝五日，賻金帛五千，贈太師，諡曰文恭。 賜壽昌甲第。
In the eighth year the emperor fell ill. Gui informed the Empress Dowager and requested that the Prince of Yan'an be installed as heir apparent. The heir was installed—this was Zhezong. Gui was promoted to Grand Master of Golden Casque and Cyan Luster and enfeoffed as Duke of Qi. In the fifth month he died in office, aged sixty-seven. The court was specially suspended for five days. Five thousand in gold and silk was granted for funeral expenses, and he was posthumously made Grand Preceptor with the posthumous title Wengong, "Cultured and Reverent." He was granted a premier residence in Shouchang.
63
從父罕
Paternal uncle: Han
64
罕，字師言，以蔭知宜興縣。 縣多湖田，歲訴水，輕重失其平。 罕躬至田處，列高下為圖，明年訴牒至，按圖示之，某戶可免，某戶不可免，眾皆服。 范仲淹在潤，奏下其式于諸道。 西方用兵，仍年科箭羽于東南，價踴貴，富室至豫貯以待鬻。 罕白郡守，倍其直市之，而令民輸錢。 旁州聞之，皆願如常州法。 累遷戶部判官。 修太宗別廟，中貴人大慮材，將一新之。 罕白是特歲久丹漆黯暗，但當致飾耳，榱櫨皆如故，唯易一楹，省緡錢十萬。
Han, styled Shiyan, entered office by privilege as magistrate of Yixing County. The county had many lakeside fields, and each year people petitioned over flood damage—but assessments of severity lost their fairness. Han went in person to the fields and charted high and low ground in a map. The next year when petitions arrived, he showed them the map—this household could be exempted, that one could not—and everyone was satisfied. When Fan Zhongyan was serving in Run Prefecture, he memorialized to distribute his method to all circuits. With war in the west, arrow-feathers were levied year after year from the southeast. Prices soared, and wealthy households even stockpiled them in advance to sell. Han told the prefect to buy them at double the market price and have the people pay in cash. Neighboring prefectures heard of this and all wished to follow Changzhou's method. He was repeatedly promoted to Associate Administrator of the Ministry of Revenue. When the separate shrine to Emperor Taizong was being renovated, a palace eunuch greatly overestimated the materials needed and was about to replace everything anew. Han said it was merely that after years the lacquer and cinnabar had dimmed and only needed refurbishing. Beams and rafters were all as before—only one pillar was replaced, saving a hundred thousand strings of cash.
65
出為廣東轉運使。 儂智高入寇，罕行部在潮，廣州守仲簡自圍中遣書邀罕，罕報曰：「吾家亦受困，非不欲歸，顧獨歸無益，當求所以相濟者。 」遂還惠州。 州之惡少年正相率為盜，里落驚擾，惠人要罕出城，及郊，遮道求救護者數千計。 罕擇父老可語者問以策，曰：「吾屬皆有田客，欲給以兵，使相保聚。 」罕曰：「有田客者如是，得矣，無者奈何？ 」乃呼耆長發里民，補壯丁，每長二百人； 又令邑尉增弓手二千。 巳時下令，約申而集。 募有方略者，許以官秩、金帛，使為甲首。 久之，無至者。 有婦人訴為僕奪釵珥，捕得之，並執奪攘者十八輩，皆梟首決口置道左，傳曰：「此耆長發為壯丁不肯行者也。 」觀者始有怖色。 至期，得六百人，尉所部亦至。 於是染庫帛為旗； 授之。 割牛革為盾形，柔之湯中，每盾削竹簽十六，穿於革，以木為鼻，使持之自蔽。 斷苦竹數千，銛其末，使操為兵。 悉出公私戎器。 檄告屬城，仿而行之。 數日，眾大振，向之惡少年，皆隸行伍，無敢動。 乃簡卒三千，方舟建旗，伐鼓作樂，順流而下。 將至廣，悉眾登岸，斬木為鹿角，積高數仞，營于南門。 智高戴黃蓋臨觀，相去三十步，見已嚴備，不敢犯。 罕徐開門而入，智高遂解去。 時南道郵驛斷絕，罕上事，不得通； 而提點刑獄鮑軻遁處南雄，數具奏。 及賊平，軻受賞，罕謫監信州酒。 安撫使孫沔言罕實有功，復以為西路轉運使。 或傳智高不死，走火峒，儂宗旦據險聚眾，邕守蕭注謀擊之。 罕呼宗旦子日新謂之曰：「汝父內為交阯所仇，外為邊將希賞之餌，非計也。 汝歸報，擇利而為之。 於是父子俱降。
He was sent out as Fiscal Commissioner of Guangdong. When Nong Zhigao raided, Han was on an inspection tour in Chao Prefecture. The Guangzhou prefect Zhong Jian, trapped in the siege, sent a letter inviting Han in. Han replied, "My family is also besieged—not that I do not wish to return, but returning alone would do no good. I must find some way we can help each other." " He then returned to Huizhou. Ruffian youths in the prefecture were banding together as robbers, and villages were thrown into alarm. The people of Huizhou wanted Han to leave the city, and when he reached the suburbs, several thousand blocked the road begging for protection. Han chose among the village elders someone he could speak with and asked for a plan. The man said, "We all have tenant farmers—we wish to supply them with arms so they can guard and gather together." " Han said, "For those with tenant farmers, that will do—but what of those who have none?" " He then summoned senior elders to mobilize villagers and supplement strong young men—two hundred per elder; He also ordered the district magistrate to add two thousand bowmen. He issued orders at si hour (about 9–11 a.m.), with assembly set for shen hour (about 3–5 p.m.). He recruited men with stratagems, promising them rank and gold and silk, to serve as squad leaders. After a long while, none came. A woman came to complain that a servant had robbed her hairpins and earrings. When the thief was caught, eighteen bandits who had been seizing goods were seized as well—all were beheaded and their mouths slit, the bodies placed on the left of the road with a placard reading, "These are the strong young men whom the senior elders mobilized but who refused to march." " Onlookers at last showed fear. When the deadline came, six hundred men had gathered, and the magistrate's troops arrived as well. They dyed treasury silks into flags; and distributed them. They cut oxhide into shield shapes and softened it in hot water. For each shield sixteen bamboo splints were whittled and pierced through the hide, with a wooden grip so each man could hold it to shield himself. They cut several thousand bitter bamboo poles, sharpened the tips, and had men wield them as weapons. All public and private military gear was brought out. He sent proclamations to subordinate prefectures ordering them to follow suit. Within days morale surged. The ruffian youths of before were all enrolled in the ranks and dared not stir. He then selected three thousand soldiers, linked boats and raised flags, beat drums and played music, and sailed downstream. As they neared Guangzhou, he landed his entire force, cut trees into chevaux-de-frise piled several ren high, and encamped at the South Gate. Zhigao watched from beneath a yellow canopy; they were thirty paces apart. Seeing the defenses complete, he did not dare attack. Han slowly opened the gate and entered, and Zhigao then withdrew. At the time southern-route couriers were cut off, and Han's memorials could not get through; while Judicial Intendant Bao Ke had fled to Nanxiong and repeatedly submitted full reports. When the bandits were suppressed, Ke received rewards while Han was demoted to superintendent of Xin Prefecture's wine monopoly. Pacification Commissioner Sun Mian said Han had truly rendered service, and he was restored as Fiscal Commissioner of the Western Route. Some reported that Zhigao had not died but fled to Huotong, where Nong Zongdan held difficult terrain and gathered followers. Yongzhou prefect Xiao Zhu planned to attack. Han summoned Zongdan's son Rixin and said to him, "Your father is hated within by Jiaozhi and without serves as bait for border generals seeking rewards—this is no wise course. Return and report to him—choose what is advantageous and act on it." Thereupon father and son both surrendered.
66
徙知潭州。 擢戶部、度支副使，復為潭州。 為政務適人情，不加威罰。 有狂婦數訴事，出言無章，卻之則勃罵，前守每叱逐之。 罕獨引至前，委曲徐問，久稍可曉，乃本為人妻，無子，夫死，妾有子，遂逐婦而據家資，屢訴不得直，因憤恚發狂。 罕為治妾而反其資，婦良愈，郡人傳為神明。 監司上治狀，敕書褒諭，賜絹三百。 徙知明州。 以光祿卿卒，年八十。 兄之子珪少孤，罕教養有恩，後珪貴，每予書，必以盛滿為戒云。
He was transferred to serve as prefect of Tanzhou. Promoted to Vice Commissioner of the Ministries of Revenue and of Public Works, he again served as prefect of Tanzhou. In governing he strove to suit human feelings and did not impose heavy punishments. There was a mad woman who repeatedly petitioned over matters. Her speech was incoherent, and if turned away she would fly into curses—the previous prefect always shouted and drove her off. Han alone brought her forward and gently questioned her at length until her story gradually became clear. She had originally been a wife without children; when her husband died, a concubine who had a son drove her out and seized the family property. She had petitioned repeatedly without justice and, from anger and resentment, had gone mad. Han punished the concubine and restored the property to the wife. The woman fully recovered, and people in the prefecture spread word of him as a divine official. Supervisory officials submitted a report on his governance. An imperial letter praised and commended him, granting three hundred bolts of silk. He was transferred to serve as prefect of Mingzhou. He died as Minister of Imperial Sacrifices, aged eighty. His elder brother's son Gui was orphaned young, and Han raised and nurtured him with kindness. Later, when Gui rose high, whenever Han wrote to him he always warned against fullness and excess.
67
從兄琪
Paternal cousin: Qi
68
琪，字君玉，兒童時已能為歌詩。 起進士，調江都主簿。 上時務十二事，請建義倉，置營田，減度僧，罷鬻爵，禁錦綺、珠貝，行鄉飲、籍田，復制科，興學校。 仁宗嘉之，除館閣校勘、集賢校理。
Qi, styled Junyu, could already compose songs and poems as a child. He passed the jinshi examination and was appointed chief clerk of Jiangdu. He submitted twelve proposals on affairs of the age: establish charity granaries, set up garrison farms, reduce excess monks, abolish the sale of offices, ban brocade, silk gauze, pearls, and cowries, conduct village drinking ceremonies and the plowing ceremony, restore the special examination, and promote schools. Emperor Renzong commended this and appointed him Collation Officer in the Hall of Literature and Collator in the Hall for Gathering Worthies.
69
帝宴太清樓，命館閣臣作《山水石歌》，琪獨蒙褒賞。 詔通判舒州。 歲饑，奏發廩救民，未報，先振以公租，守以下皆不聽，琪挺身任之。 知復州，民毆佃客死，吏論如律。 琪疑之，留未決，已而新制下，凡如是者聽減死。 歷開封府推官，直集賢院、兩浙淮南轉運使、修起居注、鹽鐵判官、判戶部勾院、知制誥。 嘗入對便殿，帝從容謂曰：「卿雅有心計，若三司缺使，當無以易卿。」
The emperor banqueted at Taiping Tower and ordered academicians to compose "Song of Landscape and Stone." Qi alone received commendation. He was ordered to serve as vice prefect of Shuzhou. In a year of famine he memorialized to open the granaries to aid the people. Before a reply came, he advanced public rent grain first—the prefect and those below all refused, but Qi personally assumed responsibility. As prefect of Fu Prefecture, when a commoner beat a tenant farmer to death, the clerks judged according to law. Qi was doubtful and held the case undecided. Soon a new statute was issued permitting reduced death sentences in such cases. He served in turn as investigating officer of the Kaifeng prefecture, Academician directly attached to the Hall for Gathering Worthies, Fiscal Commissioner of the Two Zhe and Huai circuits, compiler of the Veritable Records, Salt Monopoly Commissioner, Reviewer of the Ministry of Revenue registry, and Draftsman of Edicts. Once, entering audience in the Informal Hall, the emperor said gently, "You are naturally shrewd in planning—if the Three Departments should lack a commissioner, no one should replace you."
70
會奉使契丹，因感疾還，上介誣其詐，責信州團練副使。 久之，以龍圖閣待制知潤州。 轉運使欲浚常、潤漕河，琪陳其不便，詔寢役。 而後議者卒請廢古城埭，破古函管而浚之，河反狹，舟不得方行，公私交病。 徙知江寧。 先是，府多火災，或託以鬼神，人不敢救。 琪召令廂邏，具為作賞捕之法，未幾，得奸人，誅之，火患遂息。 復知制誥，加樞密直學士、知鄧州，徙揚州，入判太常寺，又出知杭州，復為揚州、潤州。 以禮部侍郎致仕。 卒，年七十二。
Just then he was sent as envoy to Khitan. He returned ill, and a senior envoy falsely accused him of feigning sickness, for which he was demoted to Vice Military Training Commissioner of Xin. After a long while he was made Awaiting Draftsman at the Hall of Dragon Diagrams and prefect of Runzhou. The fiscal commissioner wished to dredge the Chang and Run transport canals. Qi set forth the drawbacks, and an edict halted the project. But later the advocates finally petitioned to abandon the old city weir, break the ancient sluice gates, and dredge—and the river became narrower instead. Boats could not sail abreast, and public and private interests alike suffered. He was transferred to serve as prefect of Jiangning. Previously the prefecture had suffered many fires. Some attributed them to spirits and ghosts, and people dared not rescue. Qi summoned the ward patrol leaders and fully established reward-and-capture regulations. Before long the culprits were caught and executed, and the fires ceased. He again served as Draftsman of Edicts, was made Academician directly attached to the Bureau of Military Affairs and prefect of Dengzhou, transferred to Yangzhou, entered as Vice Director of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices, went out again as prefect of Hangzhou, and again served at Yangzhou and Runzhou. He retired as Vice Minister of Rites. He died, aged seventy-two.
71
琪性孤介，不與時合。 數臨東南名鎮，政尚簡靜。 每疾俗吏飾廚傳以沽名譽，故待賓客頗闊略。 間造飛語起謗，終不自恤。 葬於真州。 詔真、揚二州發卒護其窆，蓋異數也。
Qi was by nature solitary and upright, ill suited to his times. He repeatedly governed famous posts in the southeast, and his administration favored simplicity and calm. He always resented vulgar officials who lavishly stocked kitchens and lodges to buy reputation, and so his reception of guests was rather sparse. From time to time slander was fabricated against him, yet he never looked after himself. He was buried in Zhenzhou. An edict ordered Zhen and Yang prefectures to dispatch soldiers to escort his interment—an extraordinary honor.
72
論曰：公亮靜重鎮浮，練達典憲，與韓琦並相，號稱老成。 升之自為言官，即著直聲。 然皆挾術任數，公亮疾琦專任，薦王安石以間之，升之陰助安石，陽為異同，以避清議，二人措慮如此，豈誠心謀國者乎？ 新法之行，何望其能正救也。 及安石去位，充、珪實代之，天下喁喁，思有所休息。 充力不逮心，同僚左掣右伺，至鞅鞅以死，傷哉，其不足與有行也。 珪容身固位，于勢何所重輕，而陰忌正人，以濟其患失之謀，鄙夫可與事君也與哉！
The commentary says: Gongliang was calm and weighty, restraining the frivolous, and thoroughly versed in statutes and precedents. As co-chancellor with Han Qi he was called mature and steady. Shengzhi, from the time he became a remonstrance official, won a reputation for blunt speech. Yet all relied on stratagem and scheming. Gongliang resented Qi's monopoly of power and recommended Wang Anshi to divide it; Shengzhi secretly assisted Anshi while openly feigning disagreement to evade pure criticism—their calculations were of this sort. Were they truly men who sincerely sought the state's good? With the new laws being implemented, how could one expect them to set things right and save the realm? When Anshi left office, Wu Chong and Gui in truth replaced him. Throughout the realm people murmured, longing for some respite. Chong's strength did not match his intentions; colleagues pulled from the left and watched from the right, until he died discontented—how sad! He was not equal to carrying reforms through. Gui preserved himself and held his post—what weight did he carry in the balance of power? Yet he secretly resented upright men to serve his fear of loss. Can a base man be entrusted with serving his ruler!

楊億，字大年，建州浦城人。 祖文逸，南唐玉山令。 億將生，文逸夢一道士，自稱懷玉山人來謁，未幾，億生，有毛被體，長尺餘，經月乃落。 能言，母以小經口授，隨即成誦。 七歲，能屬文，對客談論，有老成風。 雍熙初，年十一，太宗聞其名，詔江南轉運使張去華就試詞藝，送闕下。 連三日得對，試詩賦五篇，下筆立成。 太宗深加賞異，命內侍都知王仁睿送至中書，又賦詩一章，宰相驚其俊異，削章為賀。 翌日，下制曰：「汝方髫齔，不由師訓，精爽神助，文字生知。 越景絕塵，一日千里，予有望於汝也。 」即授秘書省正字，特賜袍笏。 俄丁外艱，服除，會從祖徽之知許州，億往依焉。 務學，晝夜不息，徽之間與語，歎曰：「興吾門者在汝矣。」
Yang Yi, styled Danian, was a native of Pucheng in Jianzhou. His grandfather Wen Yi had served as magistrate of Yushan under the Southern Tang. Shortly before Yi was born, Wen Yi dreamed that a Daoist identifying himself as the Hermit of Huaiyu came to call on him. Soon afterward Yi was born with hair covering his entire body, more than a foot in length; it did not fall away until a month had passed. He learned to speak early. His mother taught him the Lesser Classics by recitation, and he could repeat them back immediately. At the age of seven he could compose literary pieces, and when he conversed with guests he carried himself with the poise of a seasoned man. Early in the Yongxi reign, when he was eleven, Emperor Taizong heard of him and ordered Zhang Quhua, the Jiangnan transport commissioner, to examine his literary skill locally and then send him to court. He was granted audience three days running and asked to produce five poems and rhapsodies, each of which he completed the moment he set brush to paper. Taizong was deeply impressed and had the chief eunuch Wang Renrui escort him to the Secretariat, while the emperor himself composed a poem in his honor. The chief ministers were astonished at the boy's brilliance and reshaped their memorials into letters of congratulation. The next day an edict was issued: "Though you are still a child in your topknot, without formal instruction your spirit is keen—as if heaven aided you—and writing comes to you by innate gift. You outpace the horizon and leave the dust behind; in a single day you cover a thousand li. I have great expectations of you. " He was immediately appointed Rectifier of Texts in the Secretariat and was specially granted an official robe and court tablet. Before long he entered mourning for his father. When the mourning period ended, his grand-uncle Huizhi happened to be serving as prefect of Xuzhou, and Yi went to live under his care. He applied himself to study without cease, day and night. When Huizhi spoke with him in private he sighed and said, "The one who will revive our clan is you."
2
淳化中，詣闕獻文，改太常寺奉禮郎，仍令讀書秘閣。 獻《二京賦》，命試翰林，賜進士第，遷光祿寺丞。 屬後苑賞花曲宴，太宗召命賦詩於坐側； 又上《金明池頌》，太宗誦其警句於宰相。 明年三月，苑中曲宴，億復以詩獻。 太宗訝有司不時召，宰相言：「舊制，未貼職者不預。 」即以億直集賢院。 表求歸鄉里，賜錢十五萬。 至道初，太宗親製九弦琴、五弦阮，文士奏頌者眾，獨稱億為優，賜緋魚。 二年春，遷著作佐郎，帝知其貧，屢有沾賚，嘗命為越王生辰使。 時公卿表疏，多假文於億，名稱益著。
During the Chunhua reign he went to court to present his writings, was promoted to Ceremonial Attendant in the Court of Imperial Sacrifices, and was permitted to continue his studies in the Imperial Library. He presented his "Rhapsody on the Two Capitals," was examined by the Hanlin Academy, was granted the jinshi degree, and was promoted to Vice Director of the Court of Imperial Entertainments. On the occasion of a musical flower-viewing banquet in the rear garden, Taizong summoned him and ordered him to compose a poem at the emperor's side; he also submitted an "Ode to the Jinming Pond," and Taizong recited its most striking lines to the chief ministers. In the third month of the following year, at another musical banquet in the imperial garden, Yi again presented a poem. Taizong was surprised that the authorities had not summoned him in time. The chief ministers explained, "Under the old regulations, those without a posted appointment do not take part. " Yi was immediately assigned as a duty officer in the Hall of Assembled Worthies. He submitted a memorial asking to return to his home district and was granted one hundred fifty thousand cash. Early in the Zhidao reign Taizong personally crafted a nine-string zither and a five-string ruan. Many scholars submitted eulogies, but Yi alone was singled out as the finest and was granted crimson fish insignia. In the spring of the second year he was promoted to Assistant Compiler. Knowing that he was poor, the emperor repeatedly bestowed gifts upon him and once appointed him birthday envoy to the Prince of Yue. At that time many memorials from high officials were drafted with Yi's help, and his reputation grew ever more prominent.
3
真宗在京府，徽之為首僚，邸中書疏，悉億草定。 即位初，超拜左正言。 詔錢若水修《太宗實錄》，奏億參預，凡八十卷，而億獨草五十六卷。 書成，乞外補就養，知處州。 真宗稱其才長於史學，留不遣，固請，乃許之任。 郡人周啟明篤學有文，深加禮待。 召還，拜左司諫、知制誥，賜金紫。
When Zhenzong was still heir apparent in the Eastern Palace, Huizhi served as his chief staff officer, and Yi drafted all the official documents for the residence. Shortly after Zhenzong's accession, Yi was promoted out of turn to Left Remonstrator. The court ordered Qian Ruoshui to compile the Veritable Records of Taizong. He recommended Yi for the project; the work ran to eighty juan in all, of which Yi alone drafted fifty-six. When the compilation was finished he requested an outside post so that he could care for his parents and was appointed prefect of Chuzhou. Zhenzong praised his talent for historical scholarship and refused to let him leave. Yi pressed his request repeatedly, and only then was he permitted to take up the appointment. A local scholar named Zhou Qiming was devoted to learning and gifted in letters; Yi treated him with exceptional respect. He was recalled to court, appointed Left Censorate Remonstrator and Drafting Drafter for Imperial Edicts, and granted the gold seal and purple robe.

咸平中，西鄙未寧，詔近臣議靈州棄守之事。 億上疏曰：
During the Xianping reign, with the western frontier still unsettled, the court ordered senior officials to debate whether Lingzhou should be abandoned. Yi submitted a memorial that read:
5
「臣嘗讀史，見漢武北築朔方之郡，平津侯諫，以為罷敝中國，以奉無用之地，願罷之。 上使辯士朱買臣等發十策以難平津，平津不能對。 臣以為平津為賢相，非不能折買臣之舌，蓋所以將順人君之意爾。 舊稱朔方，地在要荒之外，聲教不及。 元朔中，大將軍衛青奮兵掠地，列置郡縣。 今靈州蓋朔方之故墟，僻介西鄙，數百里間無有水草，烽火亭障不相望。 當其道路不壅，饢饋無虞，猶足以張大國之威聲，為中原之扞蔽。 自邊境屢驚，凶黨猖熾，爵賞之而不恭，討罰之而無獲。 自曹光實、白守榮、馬紹忠及王榮之敗，資糧屝屨，所失至多，將士丁夫，相枕而死。 以至募商人輸帛入穀，償價數倍； 孤壤築城，邊民繹騷，國帑匱乏，不能制邊人之命，及濟靈武之急。 數年之間，凶黨逾盛。 靈武危堞，巋然僅存，河外五城，繼聞陷沒。 但堅壁清野，坐食糗糧，閉壘枕戈，苟度朝夕，未粕出一兵，馳一騎，敢與之角。 此靈武之存無益，明矣。 平津所言罷敝中國以奉無用之地，正今日謂也。
"Your servant has read in history that when Emperor Wu of Han established the Shuo-fang commandery in the north, the Marquis of Pingjin remonstrated, arguing that it would exhaust the empire to sustain a useless region and asking that the project be abandoned. The emperor had debaters such as Zhu Maichen raise ten objections to refute the marquis, and Pingjin could not answer them. Your servant believes that Pingjin was a worthy minister and was not incapable of silencing Zhu Maichen; he simply chose to follow and accommodate his sovereign's wishes. The region once known as Shuo-fang lay beyond the outer marches, where the court's civilizing influence did not reach. During the Yuanshuo reign, Grand General Wei Qing led his armies in conquest and established a network of commanderies and counties there. Present-day Lingzhou occupies what was once the territory of Shuo-fang. Remote on the western frontier, for hundreds of li there is neither water nor pasture, and beacon towers and frontier posts stand too far apart to see one another. When its roads were open and supplies could be transported without fear, it could still magnify the prestige of the empire and serve as a shield for the central plains. Since the frontier has been repeatedly disturbed, hostile factions have grown ever bolder: honors and rewards fail to win their submission, and punitive campaigns gain nothing. Since the defeats suffered by Cao Guangshi, Bai Shourong, Ma Shaozhong, and Wang Rong, supplies and equipment have been lost in vast quantities, and soldiers and laborers have died in heaps upon one another. Matters have reached the point where merchants must be recruited to deliver silk in exchange for grain, with compensation several times the goods' value; fortresses are built on barren ground, frontier populations are in constant turmoil, the national treasury is exhausted, and the court can neither command the border peoples nor relieve the crisis at Lingwu. Within a few years the hostile factions have grown stronger still. Lingwu's battered walls stand barely intact, while five cities beyond the river have fallen in succession. The garrison merely strengthens its walls, clears the countryside, consumes its stores, bars the gates, and sleeps beside its weapons, barely surviving from day to day—never yet sending out a single soldier or rider to contest the enemy. It is plain that holding Lingwu serves no purpose. What the Marquis of Pingjin said about exhausting the empire to sustain a useless region applies exactly to the present situation.
6
臣以為存有大害，棄有大利，國家免粟之勞，士卒流離之苦，悉皆免焉。 堯、舜、禹，聖之盛者也，地不過數千里，而明德格天，四門穆穆。 武丁、成王，商、周之明主也，然地東不過江、黃，西不過氐、羌，南不過蠻荊，北不過太原，而頌聲並作，號為至治。 及秦、漢窮兵拓土，肝腦塗地，校其功德，豈可同年而語哉！ 昔西漢賈捐之建議棄朱崖，當時公卿，亦有異論，元帝力排眾說，奮乎獨見，下詔廢之，人頌其德。 故其詔曰：「議者以棄朱崖羞威不行，夫通於時變，即憂萬民之饑鋨，危孰大焉。 且宗廟之祭，凶年不備，況乎避不嫌之辱哉？ 」臣以為類於靈武也，必以失地為言，即燕、薊八州，河湟五郡，所失多矣，何必此為？
Your servant believes that retaining it brings great harm while abandoning it brings great benefit: the state would be spared the labor of grain transport and the troops the suffering of exile—all of this would be eliminated at once. Yao, Shun, and Yu were the greatest of sages. Their domains extended no more than several thousand li, yet their luminous virtue reached heaven and peace reigned at the Four Gates. King Wuding of Shang and King Cheng of Zhou were enlightened sovereigns, yet their domains extended no farther east than the Yangtze and Yellow rivers, no farther west than the Di and Qiang peoples, no farther south than the southern barbarians and Jing region, and no farther north than Taiyuan—yet songs of praise arose everywhere and their reigns were hailed as the height of good government. When Qin and Han exhausted their armies in territorial expansion until brains and blood stained the ground, how can their achievements be mentioned in the same breath as those of the sage kings! In Western Han, Jia Juanzhi proposed abandoning Zhuya. Many high officials disagreed, but Emperor Yuan forcefully rejected the majority view, acted on his own conviction, issued an edict abolishing the commandery, and the people praised his wisdom. His edict declared: "Critics say that abandoning Zhuya would shame us because our prestige would go unenforced—but one who understands the needs of the times must first worry over the people's hunger and distress. What peril could be greater? Moreover, when famine years come the ancestral sacrifices cannot even be properly performed—how much less should one cling to the shame of a slight loss of face!" " Your servant believes the case is analogous to Lingwu. If the argument is that we must not lose territory, consider that the eight prefectures of Yan and Ji and the five commanderies of the Hehuang region have already been lost in far greater measure—why fix on this one place alone?
7
臣竊惟太祖命姚內斌領慶州，董遵誨領環州，統兵才五六千，悉付以閫外之事，士卒效命，疆埸晏然，朝廷無旰食之憂，疆埸無羽書之警。 臣乞選將臨邊，賜給廩賦，資以策略，許以便宜而行。 儻寇擾內屬，撓之以勁兵，示之以大信，懷荒振遠，諭以賞格，彼則奔潰眾叛，安能與大邦為敵哉？ 若欲謀成廟堂，功在漏刻，臣以為彼眾方黠，積財猶豐，未可以歲月破也。 直須棄靈州，保環慶，然後以計困之爾。 如臣之策，得驍將數人，提銳兵一二萬，給數縣賦以資所用，令分守邊城，則寇可就擒，而朝廷得以無虞矣。」
Your servant recalls that Taizu appointed Yao Neibin to command Qingzhou and Dong Zunhui to command Huanzhou with only five or six thousand troops each, yet entrusted them with full authority beyond the passes. Their soldiers served devotedly, the frontier remained tranquil, the court had no need to dine late in anxiety, and no urgent dispatches arrived from the border. Your servant asks that able generals be chosen to command the frontier, granted grain rations and local revenues, supplied with strategy, and permitted to act on their own discretion as circumstances require. If the enemy harasses tribes that have submitted, strike them with strong forces, show them the court's good faith, win over distant peoples, and offer clear rewards—then they will scatter in rout and their followers will desert them. How could they stand as enemies against a great power? If one hopes to complete the court's strategy and achieve success within a matter of hours, your servant believes the enemy is now cunning and their accumulated wealth still abundant—they cannot be broken within a few months or years. One must simply abandon Lingzhou, secure Huanzhou and Qingzhou, and then use strategy to wear the enemy down. According to your servant's plan, if several able generals are obtained, ten or twenty thousand elite troops are led forth, revenues from several counties are granted for their expenses, and they are ordered to divide forces and guard the frontier cities, the enemy can be brought to bay and the court will be free of concern."
8
景德初，以家貧，乞典郡江左，詔令知通進銀司兼門下封駁事。 時以吏部銓主事前宜黃簿王太沖為大理評事，億以丞吏之賤，不宜任清秩，即封詔還。 未幾，太沖補外。 俄判史館，會修《冊府元龜》，億與王欽若同總其事。 其序次體制，皆億所定，群僚分撰篇序，詔經億竄定方用之。 三年，召為翰林學士，又同修國史，凡變例多出億手。 大中祥符初，加兵部員外郎、戶部郎中。
Early in the Jingde reign, citing his family's poverty, he requested an appointment as prefect on the lower Yangtze. The court instead ordered him to direct the Office for Presenting Memorials and Tribute while concurrently handling seal-and-return review at the Gate Department. At that time Wang Taichong, a chief clerk in the Ministry of Personnel who had formerly kept the yellow registers, was appointed Assessor in the Court of Judicial Review. Yi argued that a lowly clerical officer should not hold so exalted a post and immediately sealed the edict and returned it. Before long Wang was reassigned to an outside post. Soon afterward he was made concurrent director of the History Office. When the court undertook the compilation of the Comprehensive Mirror of the Imperial Archives, Yi and Wang Qinruo jointly directed the project. The work's organization and editorial principles were all established by Yi. Other officials drafted individual sections and prefaces, but an edict required that everything pass through Yi's revision before it could be used. In the third year he was summoned as Hanlin Academician and also appointed to compile the National History jointly; most revisions to precedent came from his hand. Early in the Dazhong Xiangfu reign he was additionally appointed Vice Director of the Ministry of War and Director of the Ministry of Revenue.
9
五年，以疾在告，遣中使致太醫視之，億拜章謝，上作詩批紙尾，有「副予前席待名賢」之句。 以久疾，求解近職，優詔不許，但權免朝直。 億剛介寡合，在書局，唯與李維、路振、刁衎、陳越、劉筠輩厚善。 當時文士，咸賴其題品，或被貶議者，退多怨誹。 王欽若驟貴，億素薄其人，欽若銜之，屢抉其失； 陳彭年方以文史售進，忌億名出其右，相與毀訾。 上素重億，皆不惑其說。 億有別墅在陽翟，億母往視之，因得疾，請歸省，不待報而行。 上親緘藥劑，加金帛以賜。 億素體羸，至是，以病聞，請解官。 有嗾憲官劾億不俟命而去，授太常少卿，分司西京，許就所居養療。 嘗作《君可思賦》，以抒忠憤。 《冊府元龜》成，進秩秘書監。
In the fifth year he took sick leave, and the court sent an imperial envoy with the court physician to examine him. Yi submitted a memorial of thanks, and the emperor wrote a poem on the margin that included the line, "worthy of the seat I keep ready for eminent men." Because of his prolonged illness he asked to be relieved of his court duties. A gracious edict refused the request but temporarily excused him from dawn attendance at court. Yi was upright and aloof and had few close associates. In the book office he was on especially close terms only with Li Wei, Lu Zhen, Diao Yan, Chen Yue, Liu Yun, and their circle. Literati of the day all relied on his judgments of literary merit. Those he criticized or passed over often nursed resentment and spoke against him in private. Wang Qinruo rose swiftly in rank. Yi had long held him in low regard, and Wang bore a grudge, repeatedly seizing on Yi's faults; Chen Pengnian was then advancing through his literary and historical accomplishments. Jealous that Yi's reputation outshone his own, he joined Wang in denigrating him. The emperor had always held Yi in high regard and was not swayed by their accusations. Yi owned a villa at Yangdi. When his mother went there to visit and fell ill, he requested leave to return home and set out without waiting for a reply. The emperor personally sealed medicines for her and added gifts of gold and silk. Yi had always been frail. Now, with word of his illness reaching court, he requested to resign his post. Someone prompted a censor to impeach Yi for departing without awaiting permission. He was appointed Vice Director of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices with duty at the Western Capital and was permitted to recuperate at his home. He once composed the "Rhapsody on Whether the Ruler May Be Pondered" to give voice to his loyal indignation. When the Comprehensive Mirror of the Imperial Archives was completed, he was promoted to Director of the Secretariat.
10
七年，病愈，起知汝州。 會加上玉皇聖號，表求陪預，即代還，以為參詳儀制副使，知禮儀院，判秘閣、太常寺。 天禧二年冬，拜工部侍郎。 明年，權同知貢舉，坐考較差謬，降授秘書監。 丁內艱，屬行郊禮，以億典司禮樂，未卒哭，起復工部侍郎，令視事。 四年，復為翰林學士，受詔注釋御集，又兼史館修撰、判館事，權景靈宮副使。 十二月，卒，年四十七。 錄其子紘為太常寺奉禮郎。
In the seventh year, after he recovered, he was reappointed prefect of Ruzhou. When the court added the Sagely Title of the Jade Emperor, he memorialized asking to take part in the ceremonies. He was immediately recalled and appointed Deputy Commissioner for Reviewing Ritual Regulations, director of the Court of Ritual, and concurrent director of the Imperial Library and the Court of Imperial Sacrifices. In the winter of the second year of Tianxi he was appointed Vice Minister of Works. The following year he served as acting Associate Commissioner for the civil service examinations. Because of errors in grading he was demoted to Director of the Secretariat. He entered mourning for his mother. Because a suburban sacrifice was scheduled and Yi was responsible for ritual and music, he was recalled to service before the mourning period had ended, restored as Vice Minister of Works, and ordered to resume his duties. In the fourth year he was again appointed Hanlin Academician and received an edict to annotate the emperor's collected writings. He also served concurrently as compiler in the History Office and director of that office, with acting duty as deputy commissioner of the Jingling Palace. In the twelfth month he died at the age of forty-seven. His son Hong was entered on the register as Ceremonial Attendant in the Court of Imperial Sacrifices.
11
億天性穎悟，自幼及終，不離翰墨。 文格雄健，才思敏捷，略不凝滯，對客談笑，揮翰不輟。 精密有規裁，善細字起草，一幅數千言，不加點竄，當時學者，翕然宗之。 而博覽強記，尤長典章制度，時多取正。 喜誨誘後進，以成名者甚眾。 人有片辭可紀，必為諷誦。 手集當世之述作，為《筆苑時文錄》數十篇。 重交遊，性耿介，尚名節。 多周給親友，故廩祿亦隨而盡。 留心釋典禪觀之學，所著《括蒼》、《武夷》、《潁陰》、《韓城》、《退居》、《汝陽》、《蓬山》、《冠鼇》等集、《內外制》、《刀筆》，共一百九十四卷。 弟倚，景德中舉進士，得第三等及第； 以億故，升為第二等。 億無子，以從子紘為後。 弟偉。
Yi was keen-witted by nature and from childhood to the end of his life never set aside brush and ink. His prose was vigorous, his wit swift, and he scarcely ever hesitated over a word. Even while conversing and laughing with guests he wrote without pause. He was meticulous and exacting in judgment, skilled at drafting in fine hand: a single sheet of several thousand characters required no revision, and scholars of the day revered him as a master. He read widely and remembered with exceptional strength, especially in statutes and institutional precedent, which many of his contemporaries consulted him to settle. He delighted in guiding younger scholars, and a great many who achieved renown did so under his tutelage. Whenever someone produced even a line worth remembering, he would recite it aloud in appreciation. He personally collected contemporary writings into several dozen sections of the Records of Brush-Garden Timely Literature. He valued friendship, was upright and unyielding by nature, and held reputation and integrity in the highest regard. He gave generously to relatives and friends, and so his official salary was always spent as fast as it came. He devoted himself to Buddhist scriptures and Chan meditation. His collected works included the Kuocang, Wuyi, Yingyin, Hancheng, Tuiju, Ruyang, Pengshan, and Guan'ao anthologies, along with Inner and Outer Drafts and Brush Notes—one hundred ninety-four juan in all. His younger brother Yi passed the jinshi examination during the Jingde reign and was placed in the third rank; because of Yi's standing he was promoted to the second rank. Yi had no sons and adopted his nephew Hong as his heir. His younger brother Wei.

Wei, styled Ziqi, studied in his youth under his brother Yi. In the first year of Tianxi he presented a eulogy, was summoned for examination at the Hanlin Academy, and was granted the jinshi degree. After passing the examination he was appointed Proofreader of the Secretariat and magistrate of Longyou County in Quzhou. He later served as Recorder in Qizhou and was recommended by the Directorate of Education as Erudite Lecturer. When Li Zunxu, Commandant-in-Chief of the Imperial Son-in-Law, was stationed at Danzhou, he recruited Wei as drafting secretary on the staff of the Zhenning military governor. He was promoted to Vice Director of the Court of Judicial Review and appointed magistrate of Hejian County, then promoted again to Erudite in the Court of Imperial Sacrifices. On the recommendation of a close court official he was appointed Collator in the Hall of Assembled Worthies and administrative vice-prefect of Danzhou. When patrol inspector Li Su joined with more than two hundred prefectural troops in a plot to kill the patrol inspector, they forced their way through the drum-and-horn gate. The prefectural commander dared not emerge. Wei stepped forward and demanded, "What are you rebelling for? " They all replied, "We mean to lodge a complaint with the prefecture—we are not rebelling. " Wei said, "You come bearing arms—if this is not rebellion, what is it? You all have parents, wives, and children—would you slaughter them all in a single fit of anger? He was transferred to magistrate of Xiangfu County, then appointed Commissioner for the counties and towns within the Kaifeng metropolitan boundary, acting vice-prefect of Kaifeng, and judge of the Three Departments' unpacking office. Hong, styled Wangzhi, entered office through yin privilege and was appointed magistrate of Yin County. Yin lay on the coast, where ruffians who trafficked in fish and salt gathered on offshore islets and sometimes robbed merchants before fleeing to sea—officials could not stop them. Hong devised a scheme: trusted men would hold the ruffians' boats as pledge and return them only when the owners came back, with a stern warning. After that they dared not turn to banditry. Because of Yi's literary works presented to the throne, he was granted jinshi standing. He served as vice-prefect of Yuezhou, prefect of Yunzhou, and Commissioner for judicial affairs in Jiangdong, then was appointed transport and investigation commissioner. When famine struck Jiangdong, Hong opened the charity granary to provide relief. His subordinates objected, but Hong said, "The charity granary exists for the people—delay even briefly and they will starve."

Hong was severe with subordinates and often said, "Lawless men must not be spared. Remove one and only a single household suffers—how can an entire prefecture of millions be allowed to suffer together? " Wrongdoers fled at the mere rumor of his approach, and some dared not take up their posts even after their terms had expired. Together with Wang Ding and Wang Chuo he was known as the "Three Tigers of Jiangdong." After a demotion he was appointed prefect of Hengzhou, then transferred to Yuezhou. He served as transport commissioner in Jingnan, was transferred to Fujian but declined to go, was appointed prefect of Huzhou, and later returned as Jiangdong transport commissioner. He rose to Vice Director of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices and died in office. Hong was stern by nature. Even at home his children dared not speak or laugh without permission. He amassed tens of thousands of juan of books, copied factual extracts by hand, and compiled them under the title Glimpsing the Leopard.

Chao Jiong, styled Mingyuan, came from a family long established at Qingfeng in Danzhou. His father Quan was the first to move the household to Pengmen. Jiong passed the jinshi examination and was appointed Assessor in the Court of Judicial Review. He served successively as Recorder in Yuezhou, was transferred to Vice Director of the Directorate of Works, and was gradually promoted to Director of the Palace Bureau. Because a prisoner died through his wrongful intervention he was stripped of two ranks. He was restored as Vice Director of Works, supervised tax collection in Xu and Wu prefectures, and was promoted to Vice Director of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices. When Zhenzong acceded to the throne, on the recommendation of Chief Minister Lü Duan and Vice Grand Councillor Li Hang he was promoted to Right Remonstrator and duty officer of the History Office. He presented fifty sections of the New Book of the Xianping Era and also a treatise entitled Pivot of Governance. When the emperor marched north, Prince Yong Yuanfen was left to guard the capital. Jiong was promoted to Right Remonstrating Grand Master, appointed staff judge, and advanced to Hanlin Academician. Before long he directed the Bureau of Review, served as ritual commissioner for the Mingde and Zhangmu imperial tombs, and was appointed joint compiler of the National History. He directed the civil service examinations in the first year of Dazhong Xiangfu. At the Mount Tai feng and Fen-yin sacrifices he worked with the Court of Imperial Sacrifices to fix ritual regulations and rose through successive promotions to Vice Minister of Works. Sent as envoy to the Khitan, he submitted the Record of the Northern Court on his return and was additionally appointed History Office compiler and director of the Office for Presenting Memorials and the Silver Terrace. At that time the court was revising ritual and literary regulations, and most edicts issued from Jiong's hand. Once he was summoned for a night audience, and the emperor ordered an inner attendant to escort him back to the Hanlin Academy with a candle. In the height of summer he was excused from overnight duty at the academy and told to report only every three to five days; Jiong declined on the grounds that this was without precedent, and was permitted to resume regular duty when autumn came. Promoted to Vice Minister of War, he requested a post at the Western Capital. He was specially appointed Minister of Works, Academician of the Hall of Assembled Worthies, and judge of the Western Capital branch of the Censorate. When Renzong acceded to the throne, Jiong was promoted to Minister of Rites. After six years at the Censorate he repeatedly memorialized for retirement and was granted leave as Junior Guardian of the Heir Apparent with full salary and seasonal gifts equal to those of a Hanlin academician. During the Tiansheng reign, when Jiong was eighty-one, he was summoned to a banquet at the Taiqing Tower and excused from the ceremonial dance of obeisance. His son Zongque, then serving as Drafting Drafter, attended the banquet with him as a court companion. Jiong was seated south of the Censor-in-Chief and, together with the chief ministers, was granted large characters in the emperor's flying-white calligraphy. When the banquet ended the gifts bestowed upon him were exceptionally generous, and he was advanced to Junior Tutor of the Heir Apparent. He was posthumously granted Grand Tutor of the Heir Apparent and given the posthumous title Wenyuan.

Jiong was skilled in breathing exercises and the arts of nurturing life, mastered Buddhist and Daoist writings, and developed a distinctive doctrine by applying the classics and their commentaries. By nature he was cheerful, easygoing, and magnanimous. He followed the Way and held to what was right, never bending before power or rank, and in all his offices he never allowed private feeling to harm others. Zhenzong repeatedly praised him as a devoted scholar and venerable elder. Yang Yi once remarked that Jiong's drafted edicts never exceeded due praise and always captured the proper style of imperial utterance. He delighted in resolving doubtful points in the classics and histories and in compiling classified lists of characters. When someone spoke to him of fate through divination, Jiong said, "What is natural in one's lot is Heaven's mandate. To rejoice in Heaven and not worry is to know one's mandate. Before he could return, he was appointed on the road Right Remonstrating Grand Master and Vice Grand Councillor. When the court prepared to grant Gusaluo a gold-inlaid barbarian couch and gold water vessels, Zongque said, "When Zhongshu of the state of Xi declined a fief and asked instead for the grand tassel, Confucius said, 'Better to give him more fiefs. Liu Yun, styled Ziyi, was a native of Daming. He passed the jinshi examination and was appointed sheriff of Guantao County. On his return, an edict ordered Yang Yi, then Drafting Drafter, to examine candidates for collating books at the Taiqing Tower. Yun ranked first and was appointed Collator of the Imperial Library with the rank of Assessor in the Court of Judicial Review. When Zhenzong toured the north, Yun was appointed administrative judge on the staff of the Daming observation commission. After warfare on the frontier ceased and the empire entered a period of peace, the emperor turned his attention to literature and gathered Confucian scholars to examine and discuss texts, producing a literary canon for the age. Yun took part in compiling the geographical treatise and the Comprehensive Mirror of the Imperial Archives and was regarded as exceptionally sharp and capable. When Zhenzong was preparing to sacrifice at Fen-sui, auspicious snow fell repeatedly. He summoned Yun and Investigating Censor Chen Congyi to the Chonghe Hall to compose songs and poems, and repeatedly praised their work. During the imperial tour of the west, the emperor again ordered Yun to compile records of local customs. At that time the regions submitted omens and auspicious signs, and the emperor was promoting ritual and literary projects. Yun repeatedly submitted rhapsodies and eulogies. The court approved his request. Yun had always loved Lujiang and built a house in the city, constructing a pavilion to store books the emperor had granted him over the years. The emperor wrote its name in flying-white calligraphy: "Pavilion for Reverent Keeping of Zhenzong's Sagely Texts." When he again served as prefect of Luzhou, he prepared his tomb and grave, made his own coffin, and carved his epitaph upon it. When illness came upon him he moved into his book pavilion and died there.
29
筠，景德以來，居文翰之選，其文辭善對偶，尤工為詩。 初為楊億所識拔，後遂與齊名，時號「楊劉」。 凡三入禁林，又三典貢部，以策論升降天下士，自筠始。 性不苟合，遇事明達，而其治尚簡嚴。 然晚為陽翟同姓富人奏求恩澤，清議少之。 著《冊府應言》、《榮遇》、《禁林》、《肥川》、《中司》、《汝陰》、《三入玉堂》凡七集。 一子蚤卒，田廬沒官。 包拯少時，頗為筠所知。 及拯顯，奏其族子為後，又請還所沒田廬云。
From the Jingde reign onward Yun stood at the forefront of literary talent. His prose excelled in parallel construction, and he was especially accomplished as a poet. He was first recognized and promoted by Yang Yi, and later came to share equal fame with him. Contemporaries called them "Yang and Liu." He entered the Hanlin Academy three times and directed the examination bureau three times. The practice of ranking candidates nationwide by policy essays began with him. By nature he did not compromise easily. In public affairs he was clear-sighted, and in governance he favored simplicity and strictness. Yet in his later years he memorialized on behalf of a wealthy clansman at Yangdi seeking imperial favors, and upright opinion held this against him. He authored seven collected works: Responses from the Imperial Archives, Honored Encounters, Forbidden Grove, Feichuan, Central Bureau, Ruyin, and Three Entries to the Jade Hall. His only son died young, and his fields and houses were confiscated by the state. When Bao Zheng was young, Yun took considerable notice of him. When Bao rose to prominence he memorialized that a clansman's son be made Yi's heir and also requested the return of the confiscated property.
30
薛映，字景陽，唐中書令元超八世孫，後家於蜀。 父允中，事孟氏為給事中。 歸朝，為尚書都官郎中。 映進士及第，授大理評事，歷通判綿、宋、昇州，累遷太常丞。 王化基薦為監察御史、知開封縣。 太宗召對，為江南轉運使，改左正言、直昭文館，為江、淮、兩浙茶鹽制置副使。 改京東轉運使，徙河東，兼河西隨軍。 求便親，知相州。 再領漕京東，積遷尚書禮部郎中，擢知制誥，權判吏部流內銓兼制置群牧使。 同梁顥安撫河北，還，權判度支。
Xue Ying, styled Jingyang, was an eighth-generation descendant of Tang Secretariat Director Xue Yuan Chao. His family later settled in Shu. His father Yunzhong served the Meng regime as Attendant-in-Ordinary. After submitting to the Song dynasty he became Director of the General Office in the Secretariat. Ying passed the jinshi examination with honors and was appointed Assessor in the Court of Judicial Review. He served successively as vice-prefect of Mian, Song, and Sheng prefectures and rose through successive promotions to Vice Director of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices. Wang Huaji recommended him as Investigating Censor and magistrate of Kaifeng County. Taizong summoned him for audience and appointed him Jiangnan transport commissioner. He was then promoted to Left Remonstrator and duty officer of the Zhaowen Hall and appointed Deputy Commissioner for Tea and Salt in the Jiang, Huai, and two Zhe regions. He was transferred to Jingdong transport commissioner, then to Hedong, with concurrent duty following the army in Hexi. He requested a post convenient to his family and was appointed prefect of Xiangzhou. He again headed transport in Jingdong, rose through successive promotions to Director of the Ministry of Rites, was elevated to Drafting Drafter, and served as acting judge of the Ministry of Personnel's internal selection with concurrent duty as Commissioner of Herds. Together with Liang Hao he served as pacification commissioner in Hebei. On his return he acted as judge of the Revenue Bureau.
31
映以右諫議大夫知杭州。 映臨決蜂銳，庭無留事。 轉運使姚鉉移屬州：「當直司毋得輒斷徒以上罪。 」映即奏：「徒、流、笞、杖，自有科條，苟情狀明白，何必繫獄，以累和氣。 請詔天下，凡徒流罪於長吏前對辨，無所異，聽遣決之。 」朝廷施用其言。 與鉉既不協，遂發鉉納部內女口及鬻銅器抑取其直，又廣市綾羅不輸稅。 真宗遣御史臺推勘官儲拱劾鉉，得實，貶連州文學。 映坐嘗召人取告鉉狀，當贖金，帝特貰之。
Ying was appointed Right Remonstrating Grand Master and prefect of Hangzhou. Ying decided cases with swift and piercing judgment, leaving no backlog in his court. Transport commissioner Yao Xian sent instructions to subordinate prefectures: "The duty office must not on its own decide cases involving penal servitude or heavier punishments. " Ying immediately memorialized: "Penal servitude, exile, beating with the rod, and cudgeling each have their statutory provisions. If the circumstances are clear, why must offenders be imprisoned and thereby harm public harmony? I request an edict to the empire that for all cases of penal servitude and exile, if the facts are argued before the chief official without dispute, the offender be permitted to be sentenced and released immediately. " The court adopted his proposal. Because he and Yao Xian were at odds, he exposed that Xian had seized women and children within his jurisdiction, sold bronze vessels while suppressing their prices, and purchased silk gauze on a large scale without paying tax. Zhenzong sent Censorate investigating officer Chu Gong to impeach Yao Xian. The charges were substantiated and Xian was demoted to Literary Officer at Lianzhou. Ying was implicated because he had once summoned someone to obtain evidence against Xian and was liable for a fine in redemption gold, but the emperor specially pardoned him.
32
在杭五年，入知通進銀臺司兼門下封駁事。 封泰山，為東京留守判官，遷給事中、勾當三班院，出知河南府。 祀汾陰還，駐蹕西京，以映有治狀，賜御書嘉獎。
After five years at Hangzhou he was recalled to direct the Office for Presenting Memorials and the Silver Terrace while concurrently handling seal-and-return review at the Gate Department. At the Mount Tai feng he served as staff judge for the Eastern Capital garrison, was promoted to Attendant-in-Ordinary and administrator of the Three-Rank Bureau, and was appointed prefect of Henan. Returning from the Fen-yin sacrifice, the court halted at the Western Capital. Because of Ying's record of effective governance, the emperor granted him an inscription of commendation in the imperial hand.
33
映好學有文，該覽強記，善筆劄，章奏尺牘，下筆立成。 為治嚴明，吏不能欺。 每五鼓冠帶，黎明據案決事，雖寒暑，無一日異也。 子耀卿，秘閣校理。 孫紳，直龍圖閣。
Ying loved learning and was accomplished in letters. He read widely and remembered with exceptional strength, and was skilled at drafting memorials and documents, which he completed the moment he set brush to paper. In governance he was stern and clear-sighted, and his subordinates could not deceive him. Every day at the fifth watch he donned his cap and belt, and at dawn he sat at his desk to decide cases. Through heat and cold alike, he never varied this routine by a single day. His son Yaoqing served as Collator of the Imperial Library. His grandson Shen served as duty academician of the Dragon Diagram Hall.
34
論曰：自唐末詞氣浸敝，迄於五季甚矣。 先民有言：「政厖土裂，大音不完，必混一而後振。 」宋一海內，文治日起。 楊億首以辭章擅天下，為時所宗，蓋其清忠鯁亮之氣，未卒大施，悉發於言，宜乎雄偉而浩博也。 劉筠後出，能與齊名，氣象似爾，至於文體之今古，時習使然，遑暇議是哉。 晁迥寬易，與物無忤，父子先後典書命，稱為名臣。 薛映學藝、吏術俱優，而挾忿以抉人之私，君子病之。
Commentary: From late Tang onward literary spirit gradually decayed, reaching its nadir in the Five Dynasties. The ancients said: "When government falls into disorder and the land splits apart, the great cultural voice cannot be whole—only after unification can it revive. " Once the Song unified the realm, literary culture rose day by day. Yang Yi was the first to dominate the literary world through his compositions and was revered by his age. His pure, loyal, and upright spirit, never fully deployed in public life, found its full expression in words—no wonder his style was grand and expansive. Liu Yun came later and could share equal fame; his literary bearing was much the same. Whether style should be ancient or modern was simply a matter of the habits of the age—who had leisure to debate the point? Chao Jiong was magnanimous and easygoing, at odds with no one. Father and son in succession directed imperial edicts and were acclaimed as eminent ministers. Xue Ying excelled in both scholarship and administrative skill, yet he harbored resentment and used it to expose others' private failings—a fault that men of principle regretted.

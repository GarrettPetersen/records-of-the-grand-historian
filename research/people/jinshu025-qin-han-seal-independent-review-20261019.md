## s0537: narrowly qualified proposed bound

The proposed `westernBounds.onOrAfter` of BC 207, year precision, is justified
for the **reported act of wearing**, as a conservative inference within the
specific Songshu/Jinshu historical narrative, subject to the independent date
auditor verifying its representation and citation in the eventual packet. It
is not a direct date in Jinshu, a verified first date of wear, or proof of a
single surviving physical seal. It does not establish continuous wear or an
upper endpoint. BC 207 and later years are included; BC 208 is excluded.

Evidence and reasoning:

1. [Songshu 18](https://www.donglishuzhai.net/chapter/1279.html) describes the
   First Emperor's Lantian jade seal, its animal knob and the same inscription
   found in Jinshu s0536. It says Gaozu obtained it on entering the passes and
   wore it, and that later generations called it the transmission seal. This
   is strong textual evidence that the two passages relate the same **literary
   seal tradition**. A direct UTF-8 HTTP response on review had SHA-256
   `e1cf0fad874aa6af461a6ec7b43c923a87c95aa0e010c3e48e168340c71c5d14`;
   page wrappers may change independently of the underlying text.
2. [Zizhi tongjian 9 with Hu Sanxing's notes](https://ancient-china-books.github.io/zizhitongjian/OEBPS/Text/5388.html)
   places Ziying's surrender at Bashang in Han year 1, winter, tenth month.
   The attached quotation attributed to Han guanyi says Ziying presented the
   First Emperor's seal and Gaozu then used/wore it; the immediately following
   Shen Yue quotation likewise associates entry into the passes with the
   Lantian seal and Jinshu's inscription. This is a textual association and
   reported order, not independent physical authentication. The Songshu text
   itself provides the more direct match; do not rely solely on juxtaposition
   of quotations to prove artifact identity.
3. The [Routledge Handbook of Imperial Chinese History timeline](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781317538233_A35095488/preview-9781317538233_A35095488.pdf),
   printed p. 20, explicitly puts Liu Bang's entry into Xianyang and the fall
   of Qin in **207 BCE**. Hu's note explicitly says the Qin year began in the
   tenth month. Thus the conventional retrospective designation Han year 1
   does not by itself justify replacing that tenth month with a Western 206
   BCE date. The Handbook supplies the Western-year anchor; the Tongjian
   supplies the historical sequence. Do not claim an independently computed
   Julian or Gregorian day from these sources.
4. The [Hou Hanshu 78 annotation attributed to Wei Hong](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh/%E5%BE%8C%E6%BC%A2%E6%9B%B8_(%E5%9B%9B%E5%BA%AB%E5%85%A8%E6%9B%B8%E6%9C%AC)/%E5%8D%B7078)
   preserves another transmission-seal tradition: its inscription differs,
   and it places wearing at Gaozu's accession. The wording also associates
   Ziying's presentation with the subjugation of the Three Qin, which does not
   match the conventional order of the surrender and later conquest. These
   differences cannot be used to silently identify the physical objects or
   assert the exact date of the Jinshu episode. If the accounts are treated as
   related reports, later accession would not contradict the proposed 207 BCE
   lower bound; the later account alone does not prove the Jinshu seal's date.
5. [Wanli yehuo bian, supplement 4](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E8%90%AC%E6%9B%86%E9%87%8E%E7%8D%B2%E7%B7%A8/%E8%A3%9C%E9%81%BA%E5%9B%9B)
   explicitly discusses competing inscriptions and succession accounts; it
   argues over potentially distinct seals and questions identifications. Its
   objections underscore that this is a bound on a reported tradition, not an
   independently established physical provenance. It does not by itself prove
   a particular number of historical seals.

## s0536: keep the hold

The Jinshu sentence attributes the seal to the First Emperor, but does not
date his possession, use, commissioning, or manufacture. Neither a reign nor
lifespan date supplies a justified date for a particular act. The above
acquisition narrative concerns Gaozu and does not resolve this separate claim.
Keep it unresolved pending claim-specific evidence.

## s0537: separate editorial concern

The candidate's idiomatic translation, “Liu Bang claimed both the
‘transmission seal’ and the snake sword as regalia,” attributes a joint claim
to Liu Bang. The Chinese instead reports that Gaozu wore the seal, that later
generations named it, and that the imperial carriage/court treasured it
together with the white-snake-slaying sword. It does not state Gaozu claimed
both as regalia. The existing literal translation preserves more content but
its final clause is awkward and should also be reviewed for clear attachment.
An editor could consider: “Han Gaozu wore it; later generations called it the
transmission seal. It and the sword used to slay the white serpent were both
treasured by the imperial court.” This is a review suggestion, not an
authorized correction; ensure any final pair of translations preserves the
source and that mentions and sealed fingerprints are reconciled afterward.

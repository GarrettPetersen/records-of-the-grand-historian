## s0190 坎牲之謀已構，斃犬之譖遂行；

Proposed literal: `The plot of the buried sacrifice had been devised, and the slander of the dead dog was carried out;`

Proposed idiomatic: `A plot like that of the buried sacrifice had been laid, and slander like that of the dead dog was put into effect;`

Proposed explanatory note: The *Zuo Tradition*, Duke Xiang 26, describes Yi Li
staging sacrificial evidence to accuse Crown Prince Cuo of Song of plotting
with Chu; the prince died after being detained. Duke Xi 4 describes Lady Li
poisoning sacrificial offerings returned by Jin's Prince Shen Sheng; a dog
died after receiving some, and she accused the prince. The historian invokes
those earlier stories as comparisons, not occurrences in Yu's life.

Evidence: [Zuo Tradition, Duke Xiang 26](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/春秋左氏傳/襄公#襄公二十六年)
and [Duke Xi 4](https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=281703&if=en).
The [Qing-era Xungai zuanwen discussion](https://www.shidianguji.com/zh/mid-page/7555251822035402798)
explicitly applies these two allusions to Jinshu 053; it is a later
interpretation, not an independent Jinshu edition. Verify links and textual
anchors at review. `斃犬` means a dog that died; it is not a dog burial or
proof that the historian describes a dog in Yu's own case.

## s0192 遂使冤逾楚建，酷甚戾園。

Proposed literal: `Thus his wrong surpassed that of Jian of Chu, and the cruelty exceeded what Li Garden endured.`

Proposed idiomatic: `Thus the injustice he suffered surpassed that of Prince Jian of Chu, and the cruelty exceeded that suffered by Han's Prince Li.`

Proposed explanatory note: `Li Garden` refers to the burial precinct of Han's
Crown Prince Liu Ju, posthumously called Prince Li. It is also used
metonymically for the prince himself. The historian compares Yu's treatment
with his fate, not with cruelty inflicted on a garden. The former comparison
is with Crown Prince Jian of Chu.

Evidence: [Hanshu 63](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/漢書/卷063) recounts
Liu Ju's fate and his posthumous designation; [Hou Hanshu 56, Zhang Hao](https://ctext.org/hou-han-shu/zhang-wang-zhong-chen-lie-zhuan/zh)
says `戾園興兵`, which is intelligible as the prince, not a burial place,
taking up arms. The [Xungai zuanwen](https://www.shidianguji.com/zh/mid-page/7555251822035402798)
explicitly identifies Jinshu 053's two references as Prince Jian of Chu and
Han's Prince Li. The metonym evidence qualifies the previous review's
suggestion that a literal translation must associate the cruelty with the
precinct: do not claim the place itself suffered cruelty. A reviewer should
check whether `Li Garden` and an explanatory note meet the project's literal
field and note conventions.

## s0197 掇蜂構隙，歸胙生災。

Proposed literal: `Picking the bee sowed estrangement; returning the sacrificial meat brought disaster.`

Proposed idiomatic: `As in the tale of the bee, a rift was contrived; as with the returned sacrificial meat, disaster followed.`

Proposed explanatory note: The bee evokes a tradition of a stepmother's
contrivance that estranged father and son. *Taiping yulan* 950 preserves a
version attributed there to *Lienü zhuan*, in which Boqi tries to remove a
bee tied to his stepmother's clothing and she raises an accusation. Different
versions circulate; do not treat this later compilation as proof of precisely
which version the Jinshu author had in mind. The sacrificial-meat allusion
echoes the Shen Sheng account in the *Zuo Tradition* cited above. Neither
the bee nor the meat is stated to be a literal incident in Yu's story.

Evidence: [Taiping yulan 950, bee](https://www.shidianguji.com/zh/book/SBCK435/chapter/1kgozxcncjz16),
[Hou Hanshu 61, Huang Qiong](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/後漢書/卷61),
and [Zuo Tradition, Duke Xi 4](https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=281703&if=en).
The Huang Qiong memorial, predating Jinshu's compilation, mentions Boqi's
exile among examples of harmful slander but does **not** itself mention a
bee. The much later commentary there attributes a bee version to *Shuoyuan*;
do not treat the commentator's attribution as an independently dated textual
witness to that full wording.
The *Taiping yulan* is a Northern Song witness to a story attributed to an
earlier work; its survival in this citation is not proof the exact story was
available in this form to Jinshu's compilers. A reviewer should retain the
interpretive qualification or identify an earlier securely attested witness.

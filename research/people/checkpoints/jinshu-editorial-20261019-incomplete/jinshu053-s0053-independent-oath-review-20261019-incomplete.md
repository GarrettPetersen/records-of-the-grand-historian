## Verdict

**Revise, not accept verbatim.** The proposal correctly restores Daowen, Jiang and the oath, and its caution about the status of the document is essential. But `if Heaven permits` adds an explicit conditional not marked in the Chinese; the available commentary does not settle that construction. Likewise, `eating raw flesh` does not literally represent 毛 (hair). In the idiomatic proposal `place Jiang over the inner court` also makes her sound like an appointed court administrator rather than someone designated as 內主. Hu's gloss supports a royal-consort interpretation, but his `立為后` does not itself specify the more elaborate English title “empress dowager.”

Suggested **literal**, as wording for further editorial consideration: “Chewing hair and drinking blood beneath the three luminaries, High Heaven permits it; we shall sweep away the harm, establish Daowen as king, and make Jiang the inner ruler.”

Suggested **idiomatic**, retaining ritual imagery and its proposed force: “Beneath the sun, moon, and stars, we pledge with hair and blood: Heaven gives its leave, and we shall sweep away the danger, make Daowen king, and make Jiang ruler of the inner court.”

The division `許 / 當` and anticipatory `it` are interpretations, not established punctuation: the text can also be read as Heaven permitting the proposed removal, and modal `當` may express obligation or expectation. These readings state the forged document's claimed divine permission, **not** an independently established divine approval. I do not see sufficient primary commentary here to prove or exclude a conditional sense conclusively; the proposal's unmarked `if` should not be passed off as a literal correspondence. The editor should retain an explicit philological hold if the review process requires resolution of that remaining ambiguity before accepting exact text. Neither wording asserts that the oath or plan was actually executed.

## Evidence and scope

1. [Jinshu 53](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%99%89%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7053) gives the local wording `茹毛飲血於三辰之下，皇天許當掃除患害，立道文為王，蔣為內主`. In context Pan Yue drafts the document, and the drunken crown prince is induced to copy it. The forged text must remain direct attributed quotation; the historian's narrative is not certifying its allegations or its appeal to Heaven.
2. [Zizhi Tongjian 83 with Hu Sanxing's commentary](https://ancient-china-books.github.io/zizhitongjian/OEBPS/Text/6227.html), section 8, has `蔣氏` instead of Jinshu's `蔣`. Hu explicitly glosses `茹毛飲血` as an oath (`謂盟誓也`), says Bin's courtesy name was Daowen, identifies Jiang as Bin's mother Jiang Baolin, and explains `內主` as `言將立為后也`. His separate note challenges the authenticity of the entire document. These are his historical and interpretive statements, not evidence that Jiang actually assumed the role or a mandate to merge contested extraction identities.
3. Hu does **not** explain `皇天許當` in that note. The offered splitting is a transparent grammatical reading, not a gloss credited to him. The subsequent `願成` is a wish for success; it is compatible with the forged speaker's claim of permission but cannot by itself prove the `if` construction.
4. In this passage, `茹毛飲血` is read by Hu as an oath formula; the literal hair and blood imagery is worth retaining in both fields. Hu's gloss does not establish which ritual actions occurred or exactly how the hair was consumed. The proposed idiomatic phrase is therefore an explanation of the text's force, not an independent report of ritual performance. `三辰` here conventionally denotes sun, moon and stars; “three luminaries” keeps the compact source phrase, and the idiomatic expansion makes it intelligible.
5. `后` can be a royal or imperial female title according to context; Hu's gloss supports a more specific interpretive translation than “inner ruler” but does not supply the modern English title by itself. Since this fabricated text says `立道文為王`, the neutral wording above preserves the local phrase without deciding whether to call Jiang “queen,” “empress,” or “empress dowager.” `Jiang` likewise leaves the identity question and the Jinshu/Tongjian textual difference visible.

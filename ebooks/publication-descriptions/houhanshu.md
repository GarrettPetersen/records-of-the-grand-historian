Fan Ye (范曄), the author of the Book of Later Han (Hou Hanshu, 後漢書), was a native of Shunyang during the Eastern Jin dynasty. The Book of Song says his mother gave birth to him in a privy, where he hit his head on a brick, earning him the childhood name, “Brick” (Zhuan, 磚).

The brick doesn't seem to have done any lasting damage, as he proved himself to be a bright child. He loved learning and reading, voraciously consuming classic texts and histories. He was skilled at writing and music. Yet his older brother Yan saw a darkness in him, saying, "This boy is greedy for gain; he will destroy the family in the end."

As a young adult, he worked on the staff of Liu Yu (the future emperor Wu of Liu Song). He had a series of jobs working on the staffs of various generals, even reluctantly joining a northern campaign managing the army's logistics.

One of Fan Ye's jobs was working as a staff officer for perhaps the most interesting and dynamic political figure of his day: Liu Yikang, the Prince of Pengcheng. Their relationship would be full of twists and turns, but at this early stage, Yikang was said to treat Fan Ye generously. Yikang was not an emperor, but he dominated the court through the strength of his personality.

The warm relationship between Fan Ye and Yikang was not to last. One night, before the funeral procession of the Grand Consort of Pengcheng, Fan Ye and a friend drank to excess, opened the windows, and amused themselves listening to the funeral dirges. When Yikang found out about this he was furious, and demoted Fan Ye to Administrator of Xuancheng.

His ambitions frustrated, Fan Ye put his time to productive use. He compiled existing historical documents on the later Han Dynasty, and used them to write the volume that would become the Book of Later Han, his most enduring legacy. He spent several years in this position, before eventually being promoted out of it.

Some time later, Fan Ye's legal mother became ill. He was in another city, and did not arrive in time to see her before her passing. He also brought singing girls and concubines with him when he travelled to his ailing mother. This breach of filial piety was noticed by other officials, and Fan Ye was impeached. However, the emperor chose not to punish him, valuing his talents. Fan Ye continued to advance in his career as an imperial official, though perhaps not to the heights of his ambitions.

It was at this point that Fan Ye made a dangerous friend: Kong Xixian. Xixian, whose family was quite wealthy, gambled with Fan Ye and lost on purpose to ingratiate himself with Ye. Fan Ye enjoyed Xixian's company and admired his literary talent. The two men became close friends.  
  
But Xixian had an ulterior motive. His family had a connection to Fan Ye's estranged former boss, Yikang, who had been demoted after years of dominating the court. Xixian wanted to repay Yikang for protecting his father years earlier, and he sought to recruit Fan Ye as a co-conspirator. He pointed out to Fan Ye that, despite his esteemed lineage, the imperial family would not intermarry with Fan Ye's family. This point hit home, and Fan Ye resolved to act against the throne.

Perhaps coincidentally, it was around this time that Yikang reached out to Fan Ye to heal their estrangement.

Xixian read the astrological signs and predicted the unnatural death of Emperor Wen, caused by familial conflict. He gathered conspirators among those with connections to Yikang, who were many, with the intent of toppling the emperor and installing Yikang in his place. Xixian gathered many secret conspirators. He was ruthless in his plotting, at one point using a maid to deliver conspiratorial messages and then poisoning her to death to prevent her from leaking information.

The conspirators drafted a proclamation, to be released after the conclusion of the plot, that blamed the official Zhao Bofu for killing the emperor. The plan was to kill both Emperor Wen and Bofu, and then pin blame on the latter for the former's death. Then, Yikang would be invited to take the throne. Fan Ye's most direct role in the conspiracy was to forge a letter from Yikang, endorsing the plot.

The conspirators planned to strike during a farewell ceremony presided over by the emperor. A conspiring palace guard gave the signal to start the attack, but Fan Ye didn't dare look at him, perhaps losing his nerve, and the opportunity was lost.

This would prove fatal, as soon thereafter, Xu Zhanzhi betrayed the entire conspiracy to the emperor. The other conspirators were arrested and most swiftly confessed, though Fan Ye at first attempted to lie his way out.

Fan Ye was executed at the age of 48, along with his 3 sons, and all the other conspirators (besides Xu Zhanzhi). Thus ended Fan Ye's life, though his legacy lived on in the historical volume you are now reading, which he began writing during the years of his demotion to Xuancheng. Perhaps it would have been better if he had stayed demoted, giving him more time to work on literature, music, and art, all of which he excelled at, and keeping him far away from palace intrigues, which were his ultimate undoing.

At the time of his death, Fan Ye had not completed the treatises he intended to add to Hou Hanshu, so the work was supplemented with eight treatises in 30 volumes written earlier by Sima Biao (司馬彪) during the Western Jin dynasty.

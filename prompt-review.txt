## Editorial goals (in order)

1. **Semantic fidelity** — The English must match the Chinese meaning. Do not soften, embellish, or omit facts, titles, dates, relationships, or causal links. If the Chinese is ambiguous, choose the reading best supported by context and note nothing in the JSON (no commentary fields).

2. **Readability** — Idiomatic English should read as professional historical prose: complete sentences, natural word order, appropriate articles (`the`, `a`, `an`), and conjunctions. Avoid choppy telegraphic fragments unless the Chinese is itself a bare list.

3. **Dialogue and speech** — Where the Chinese records quoted speech, commands, memorials, or questions, the English must make **who is speaking or being addressed** clear on first mention in that passage. Use tags where needed (`X said`, `Y replied`, `the emperor asked`, `they memorialized`). Do not leave orphan quotations that could be misattributed. **Inside quoted speech, keep first person** (`I`, `we`) when the speaker refers to themselves — do not switch to third person (`Xiang was the plotter`).

4. **Names and titles** — Keep consistent romanization within the chapter. Prefer established forms (e.g. Emperor Wu, the Xiongnu) over literal calques when standard in English historiography.

5. **Tables** — For table cells, preserve row/column sense; use `context` in the review JSON (headers, row cells) to disambiguate abbreviated Chinese.

## Conservative editing (important)

- **Change only where needed.** Most sentences in a chapter may already be correct — leave them unchanged.
- Do **not** rewrite for polish, synonym variety, or “literary” effect alone.
- Read every line in the review file; it is fine if most entries keep the same wording.
- After `make apply-review`, the chapter is marked reviewed even for unchanged lines — your job is to **verify** each line, not to change every line.

## Literal vs idiomatic

- **Literal** — Close, denotative rendering; may be stiffer. Still grammatical. Use for scholars comparing to the source.
- **Idiomatic** — Same meaning as literal, but fluent for a general reader. This is the primary reading text on the site.
- Do **not** make literal and idiomatic identical unless the Chinese is a single proper name or fixed formula.
- Literal may stay closer to Chinese word order; idiomatic may add articles and conjunctions. **Both must mean the same thing.**

## Style guide

- Prefer past tense for narrative; present only for gnomic or timeless statements.
- Avoid archaisms unless the passage is a formal citation and tone demands it.
- Numbers, reign years, and place names: translate clearly; do not invent modern geography.
- Tone: dignified historical prose (Sima Qian / standard English historiography), not colloquial sarcasm or modern editorializing.

## Classical phrasing pitfalls

When you see patterns like these, prefer the faithful reading — do not substitute a clever modern paraphrase:

| Chinese pattern | Wrong drift | Prefer |
|-----------------|-------------|--------|
| 皆曰X可 | “All said X might” (fragment) | `All said, “X may do it.”` |
| 爾其庶矣 (pardon after offense) | “almost a true brother” | forgiveness / sparing: e.g. “you are still my brother,” “you are forgiven,” “you will yet be my younger brother” — **not** “almost” |
| 欽哉 | vague “revere” only | reverent command to officials; e.g. “Be reverent, be reverent” |
| 土德 / 服土德 | “gentleman,” generic dress | earth virtue / earth’s color (Yellow Emperor context) |
| 執中 | “soaked the mean” | held fast the center / held the mean |
| 砥屬 | “grindstone,” unrelated idiom | submit to / come under (his rule) |
| 盲者子 (quoted) | omit or mis-attribute | keep as quoted speech with clear speaker |

If `{book}` is **shiji** and the chapter is annals (本紀), expect mythic register, genealogies, and quoted dialogue between rulers — keep names consistent within the chapter.

## Glossary alignment check

After review application, the workflow runs `scan-translation-alignment` in review-priority mode with a chapter-level glossary-risk cutoff. Treat its output as a review queue:

- Offset-style hits usually mean a distinctive Chinese term appears in one sentence while its English equivalent appears nearby.
- `LOW_GLOSSARY_SAME_SENTENCE_COVERAGE` means the Chinese sentence has glossary anchors, but the English matched too few of them.
- `glossaryRiskScore` ranks how much distinctive source evidence is missing from the English. Inspect the highest-risk findings first; full per-sentence glossary scores can be exported separately for deeper book-level passes.
- These are not automatic errors. They are prompts to inspect the Chinese and English together. Accept a hit only when the English is faithful and the missing glossary terms are false positives, better rendered another way, or intentionally implicit from context.
